When you're renting a small apartment, you don't always get to choose your appliances. Maybe your fridge is a decade old, your oven runs hot, or you're stuck with whatever was already installed when you moved in. That can make "reduce your energy use" advice feel a little useless if it all assumes you're buying new equipment.

The truth is, most of the real savings in a small kitchen come from habits, not purchases. A few small, free changes to how you cook, store food, and use your appliances can meaningfully lower your electric bill, no landlord approval or new equipment required.

Here's a practical, renter-friendly plan for cutting kitchen energy use in a small apartment, starting with the biggest wins and working down to the small stuff.

## Start With Your Refrigerator (It's Probably Your Biggest Energy User)

Unlike your oven or microwave, which run for short bursts, your [refrigerator](/blog/best-side-by-side-refrigerator/) runs continuously, 24 hours a day, every day of the year. That makes it one of the largest energy users in most kitchens, small or large, even though it's easy to overlook because it's just quietly humming in the corner.

* **Check the door seal.** Close the door on a piece of paper. If you can pull the paper out easily, the seal may be worn, letting cold air escape and forcing the compressor to run more.
* **Keep the coils clean.** Dust buildup on the coils (usually on the back or bottom of the fridge) makes it work harder to release heat. A quick vacuum every few months helps.
* **Don't set it colder than necessary.** Most fridges run efficiently in the mid-30s°F and freezers around 0°F. Colder than that just uses more energy without a real benefit.
* **Give it breathing room.** A fridge pushed tightly against a wall or cabinet can't vent heat properly, which makes the compressor run longer.
* **Keep it reasonably full, but not packed.** A fridge with some contents holds temperature more efficiently than an empty one, but an overpacked fridge blocks airflow inside.

**Renter-friendly:** yes, all of these are maintenance habits, not modifications.

## Cook With Lids and Match Pan Size to Burner Size

This is one of the simplest fixes and it costs nothing if you already own lids. A covered pot traps heat and steam, so water boils faster and food cooks in less time. An uncovered pot loses a steady stream of heat into the room the entire time it's on the burner.

Similarly, a pan smaller than your burner wastes heat around its edges, especially on electric coil stoves. [Matching pan size to burner size](/blog/how-to-choose-energy-efficient-cookware-for-a-small-kitchen/) is one of the simplest cookware fixes, and if you're missing lids for a few pots, a universal silicone lid is a cheap fix.

**What to get:** a universal adjustable silicone lid set, usually $15 to $25, if any of your pots are missing their original lids.

## Use the Right-Sized Appliance for the Job

Cooking a single potato in a full-size oven means heating a large box of air for one small item. A [toaster oven](/blog/best-small-ovens-and-toaster-ovens-for-baking-in-small-kitchens/), [air fryer](/blog/best-air-fryers-under-100/), or microwave handles small portions using a fraction of the energy, since they heat a much smaller space.

* Reserve your full oven for larger batches or dishes that genuinely need it.
* Use a microwave or toaster oven for reheating and small meals.
* If you're cooking for one or two people regularly, consider whether a compact appliance could replace your full-size one for daily use.

## Reduce Preheating and Door-Opening Habits

Every time you open an oven or air fryer door mid-cook, heat escapes and the appliance has to work to recover it. A few habits help:

* **Only preheat as long as necessary.** Many ovens beep when they've reached temperature; there's rarely a reason to preheat much longer than that.
* **Resist checking on food constantly.** Use the oven light and window instead of opening the door.
* **Skip preheating altogether for some dishes**, like casseroles or dishes that cook for a long time anyway, where a few extra minutes of ramp-up time won't matter.

## Batch Cook and Reheat Smart

Running an oven, stovetop, or air fryer once for a larger batch is more efficient than running it multiple times for smaller portions throughout the week, since you're not repeating the energy cost of heating up each time.

* Cook grains, proteins, or roasted vegetables in bigger batches and reheat portions through the week using a microwave, which is one of the most efficient reheating tools you own.
* Freeze extra portions instead of letting an oversized fridge slowly work through leftovers that eventually spoil, wasting both the food and the energy used to cook it.

## Unplug Idle Appliances

Many countertop appliances draw a small amount of power even when they're off but still plugged in, sometimes called standby or phantom power. It's a small draw per device, a coffee maker's clock, a microwave's display, but across a kitchen full of small appliances, it adds up over a full year.

* Unplug appliances you don't use daily, like a [stand mixer](/blog/best-hand-mixers/) or [waffle iron](/blog/best-waffle-maker-with-removable-plates/), between uses.
* Consider a power strip for your most-used counter appliances so you can switch several off at once when you're not cooking.

**Renter-friendly:** yes, this requires no changes to the apartment itself.

## Run Full Loads, Not Partial Ones

If you have a [dishwasher](/blog/how-to-clean-a-dishwasher/), running it only with a full load makes a real difference, since a partial load still uses nearly the same amount of water and energy as a full one. The same logic applies to laundry if your unit is in the kitchen or nearby, though that's a separate topic from cooking energy specifically.

If you're hand-washing dishes instead, batching them into one wash rather than repeatedly running the tap for a few items at a time saves both water and the energy used to heat it.

## Consider a Portable Induction Burner for Occasional Use

If your apartment's stove is older or inefficient, and a full replacement isn't an option as a renter, a [portable single-burner induction cooktop](/blog/best-induction-cooktop-for-the-money/) is a low-cost, plug-in way to cook more efficiently for everyday meals. Induction transfers heat directly to the pan rather than heating a coil or flame first, wasting much less heat into the surrounding air.

**Renter-friendly:** yes, it's a countertop unit, no installation required. You'll need induction-compatible cookware (anything a magnet sticks to).

**What to get:** a portable single-burner induction cooktop, usually $40 to $100.

## Time Your Cooking Around Natural Light and Heat

This isn't about your appliances directly, but it affects your overall apartment energy use, especially in warmer months. Running an oven or stovetop heats up your kitchen, which can trigger air conditioning to work harder if you have it, effectively doubling the energy cost of that meal. On hot days, favor appliances that produce less ambient heat, like a slow cooker, microwave, or countertop induction burner, over a full oven or extended stovetop cooking. In cooler months, this effect reverses, and a bit of oven-generated heat can modestly offset your heating needs, though it's not a substitute for an actual heating system.

## Check for Drafts and Insulation Around Kitchen Windows

If your kitchen has a window, drafts around it can affect how hard your refrigerator and any air conditioning or heating work, since a drafty kitchen struggles to hold a stable temperature. A simple, renter-friendly fix is a removable weatherstripping strip or a draft snake placed along the window sill, neither of which requires any permanent changes or landlord approval. This is a small, often-overlooked factor, but it's a genuinely free or very low-cost fix worth doing once and forgetting about.

## Keep a Simple Habit Checklist Somewhere Visible

Most of the energy-saving habits in this guide are easy individually but easy to forget in the middle of cooking dinner. A short list taped inside a cabinet door, lids on pots, full loads only, unplug when done, can serve as a quick visual reminder until these habits become automatic. This costs nothing and takes less than five minutes to set up, but it's one of the more effective ways to make sure good habits actually stick past the first week.

## Plan Your Grocery Shopping Around Your Fridge's Efficiency

How you shop affects how hard your fridge has to work. Buying a large amount of perishable food all at once, more than a small fridge can hold without overpacking, forces you to either cram the shelves (which blocks airflow and makes the compressor work harder) or let food sit out longer while you find room. Shopping in smaller, more frequent trips sized to what your fridge can hold comfortably keeps it running efficiently and tends to reduce food waste as a side benefit, since you're not letting produce get buried and forgotten in an overstuffed fridge.

## Use Your Dishwasher's Eco or Air-Dry Setting

If your apartment's dishwasher has an eco or energy-saving cycle, it's generally worth using for everyday loads that aren't heavily soiled. These cycles typically use lower water temperatures and longer wash times, which uses less total energy than a shorter, hotter cycle. Skipping the heated dry cycle in favor of air-drying, either by cracking the door at the end of the cycle or using an air-dry setting if your model has one, is another simple way to cut a meaningful chunk of a dishwasher's total energy use per cycle, since the heating element for drying draws real power for an extended time.

**Renter-friendly:** yes, this is just a setting change on equipment you already have.

## Watch Out for an Old or Inefficient Stove

Older gas and [electric stoves](/blog/best-slide-in-electric-range/) can lose efficiency over time. Warped electric coils don't sit flush with a pot's bottom, and older gas burners can develop uneven flame patterns that waste heat around the sides of a pan. If you notice one burner heating noticeably slower than the others, or a burner that looks visibly warped or damaged, it's worth mentioning to your landlord as a maintenance issue rather than assuming it's just something to work around. A repaired burner restores lost efficiency at no cost to you.

## Talk to Your Landlord About Old Appliances

If your building's fridge or oven is genuinely old and inefficient, it's worth a conversation with your landlord, especially if you're on a long-term lease. Some landlords are open to upgrading an old, energy-draining appliance, particularly if you frame it as a shared benefit: lower utility costs (if utilities are included) or a longer-lasting unit if you're paying your own electric bill. Even if a full appliance swap isn't approved, smaller additions like a portable induction burner or a compact microwave are usually fine.

## **Common Mistakes to Avoid**

* **Focusing only on the stove and ignoring the fridge.** Your refrigerator runs all day, every day, and is often the single biggest energy user in the kitchen, even though it's easy to overlook.
* **Cooking without lids out of habit.** This alone can meaningfully increase the time and energy needed to boil water or simmer a sauce.
* **Running the dishwasher or oven for small loads out of convenience.** Batching tasks together is one of the easiest, free ways to cut energy use.
* **Leaving appliances plugged in "just in case."** Small standby draws add up across a kitchen full of gadgets over a year.
* **Assuming you can't do anything as a renter.** Most of the fixes here require no installation, no landlord approval, and no new appliances at all.

## **The Bottom Line**

Reducing kitchen energy use in a small apartment doesn't require a renovation or a shopping spree. Start with your fridge, since it runs constantly and is often the biggest single factor, then layer in free habits like cooking with lids, matching pan size to burner size, and unplugging idle appliances. These changes cost nothing, work whether you own or rent, and add up to a real difference on your electric bill over time.
