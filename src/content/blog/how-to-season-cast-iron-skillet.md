A sticky, blotchy or gummy pan was not seasoned badly. It was seasoned with too much oil, the most common mistake people make when they season a cast iron skillet.

Here is how to get a finish that comes out hard, dry and black instead. We cover the oven method step by step, the oils that work, quick stovetop touch-ups, everyday cleaning, rust rescue and fixes for every common problem.

**Quick answer:** Wash and fully dry the skillet, then rub a little oil over every surface and wipe it off until the pan looks almost dry. Bake it upside down at 450 to 500°F for 1 hour and let it cool in the oven. Repeat 3 to 4 times. Use a cheap, neutral, high-smoke-point oil like canola or vegetable oil, and keep each coat very thin.

## What Seasoning Is, and Why Less Oil Wins

Seasoning is oil that has been heated until it turns into a hard, slick film bonded to the iron. That film protects the bare iron from rust and helps food release.

A good coat is made of many very thin layers, not one thick one. A thin layer of oil can harden all the way through in an hour. A thick layer only hardens on top, so the oil underneath stays soft. That soft middle is what feels sticky, collects dust and peels off later.

So the rule for every round is simple. Put oil on, then take nearly all of it off. If you can see shiny oil, streaks or a puddle in the corner, there is still too much.

Carbon steel seasons the same way; see our guide to [carbon steel on induction](/blog/carbon-steel-on-induction-does-it-work-will-it-warp/). Stainless steel does not hold a coat, which is why [seasoning stainless steel pans](/blog/how-to-season-stainless-steel-pans/) only gives a short-term boost.

## How to Season a Cast Iron Skillet in the Oven, Step by Step

This is the method for a new bare pan, a pan you have stripped, or one that has gone dull and patchy. Each round takes about an hour and a half, including cooling.

### Step 1: Clean and Dry the Pan Completely

Wash the skillet with warm water and a little dish soap, scrubbing off any stuck food or loose flakes.

Dry it with a towel, then set it on a burner over medium-low heat for 3 to 5 minutes. This drives out the water hiding in the pores of the iron. Water left in the metal causes spotty seasoning and fresh rust.

### Step 2: Preheat the Oven

Set the oven to 450 to 500°F. Put a sheet pan or a sheet of foil on the lower rack. Leave the upper rack in the middle.

Turn on your range hood or open a window now, before any smoke starts. If your kitchen holds smells for days, our guide to [range hood ventilation in a small kitchen](/blog/small-kitchen-guide-range-hood-ventilation-cooking-smell/) shows how to clear the air faster.

### Step 3: Apply a Thin Coat of Oil Everywhere

While the pan is still warm, add about 1 teaspoon of oil for a 10-inch skillet. Rub it over the cooking surface, the sides, the outside, the bottom and the handle. Warm iron takes oil more evenly than cold iron.

With a spray, hold the can about 8 inches away and give the pan a light, quick pass. Then rub it in with a cloth.

### Step 4: Wipe It Off As If You Made a Mistake

This is the step that decides everything. Take a clean, dry cloth and wipe the pan as though you want to remove all the oil. Keep going until it looks matte and almost dry, with no shine, streaks or pooled oil.

Enough oil stays in the surface to build a layer. You cannot wipe it all off.

### Step 5: Bake It Upside Down for 1 Hour

Place the skillet upside down on the middle rack. Upside down stops any extra oil from pooling in the cooking surface. The sheet pan below catches drips.

Bake for 1 hour. Expect some light smoke in the first 15 to 20 minutes. That is the oil changing into seasoning.

### Step 6: Let It Cool in the Oven

Turn the oven off and leave the pan inside until it cools, about 45 minutes to an hour. Slow cooling lets the coat finish hardening. It also protects the iron from a sudden temperature shock.

### Step 7: Repeat 3 to 4 Times

One round gives a thin, light brown coat. Three or four rounds build a darker, tougher finish that handles daily cooking. Repeat Steps 3 to 6 each time, starting with a warm pan.

Do all four rounds in one afternoon or spread them over a few days. The finished pan looks evenly dark, feels dry and has a soft satin shine.

## Cast Iron Seasoning Temp and Time Cheat Sheet

| Situation | Oven temp | Time | Rounds | Cooling |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| New bare or stripped pan | 450 to 500°F | 1 hour | 3 to 4 | In the oven |
| New pre-seasoned pan | 450 to 500°F | 1 hour | 1 to 2 (optional) | In the oven |
| Dull, patchy or after rust removal | 450 to 500°F | 1 hour | 2 to 4 | In the oven |
| Slightly sticky finish | 450 to 500°F | 1 hour, no new oil | 1 | In the oven |
| Avocado oil or other very high-smoke oil | 500°F | 1 hour | 3 to 4 | In the oven |

Always stay at or above 450°F. At lower temperatures the oil only half-cures, and you get the sticky, gummy finish we are trying to avoid.

If your only oven is a countertop model, check its top temperature first. Many reach 450°F, and our [small oven and toaster oven guide](/blog/best-small-ovens-and-toaster-ovens-for-baking-in-small-kitchens/) lists their limits. Make sure the skillet fits with a gap on every side and does not touch the heating elements.

## What Is the Best Oil to Season Cast Iron?

The best oil is the cheap one already in your pantry, as long as it is neutral and refined. The oven should be hotter than the oil's smoke point, which is the temperature where it starts to smoke. That is how the oil fully hardens.

| Oil | Smoke point (approx.) | How it seasons | Our view |
|---|---|---|---|
| Canola or vegetable oil | 400 to 450°F | Even, tough, slightly brown | Our first choice for most people |
| Vegetable shortening | About 360°F | Smooth, dark, very reliable | Excellent, easy to spread thin |
| Sunflower oil (refined) | About 450°F | Even and hard | Very good |
| Grapeseed oil | About 420°F | Hard, slick finish | Very good, costs more |
| Avocado oil (refined) | About 500 to 520°F | Hard, glossy | Good; bake at the top of the range |
| Bacon fat or lard | About 370°F | Works, can smell | Fine for touch-ups, not first coats |
| Extra virgin olive oil | About 350 to 410°F | Often soft or sticky | Skip for seasoning, fine for cooking |
| Flaxseed oil | About 225°F | Very hard but brittle | Not worth it; prone to flaking |

### Is Flaxseed Oil Good for Seasoning Cast Iron?

Flaxseed oil builds a very hard, dark coat that looks great at first. The trouble is that it is brittle. On a pan that heats and cools every day, that hard shell tends to crack and flake off in sheets. It also costs more and goes rancid quickly. Save yourself the trouble and use canola or shortening.

### Can You Season a Cast Iron Skillet With Olive Oil?

Cook with olive oil all you like. For oven seasoning, extra virgin olive oil often leaves a soft, tacky film, so use a neutral oil instead.

## How to Season a Cast Iron Skillet on the Stove

Stovetop seasoning is for touch-ups, not for building a full coat from bare iron. It is quick and does not heat the whole apartment. Use it after washing, after cooking something acidic, or when a spot looks dry or grey.

1. Wash and dry the pan, then set it over medium heat for 2 to 3 minutes.
2. Add about half a teaspoon of oil and rub it over the inside with a folded cloth held in tongs.
3. Wipe off all the extra until the surface looks nearly dry.
4. Keep heating for 5 to 10 minutes, until it smokes lightly and then stops smoking.
5. Turn off the heat and let it cool on the burner.

On a smooth glass cooktop, lift the pan on and off instead of sliding it. Our guide on [protecting a glass-top stove from cast iron](/blog/how-to-protect-glass-top-stove-from-cast-iron/) covers preheating and scratches. Coil burners have their own quirks, explained in [coil stove cookware: what works and what warps](/blog/coil-stove-cookware-what-works-what-warps/).

### How to Season Cast Iron Without an Oven

No oven? A covered outdoor grill works well, and it keeps the smoke out of your home. Heat the grill to 450 to 500°F, oil and wipe the pan as usual, set it upside down on the grates, close the lid and hold the heat for 1 hour. Let it cool with the lid down.

## How to Season a New Pre-Seasoned Skillet

Almost every cast iron pan sold today comes pre-seasoned, so you can cook in it the day it arrives. The factory coat is thin, though, and may look patchy or stick at first.

Here is what we would do with a new pan:

1. Rinse it in warm water with a drop of soap to remove dust from shipping.
2. Dry it on a burner for 3 to 5 minutes.
3. Give it 1 or 2 oven rounds at 450 to 500°F for a slicker start. This step is optional but makes the first month easier.
4. Cook fatty foods first: bacon, sausages, shallow-fried potatoes, cornbread. Save eggs and delicate fish for a few weeks later.

## How to Clean Cast Iron After Cooking

Most seasoning damage happens at the sink. A good cleaning routine takes about two minutes.

1. **Clean it while it is warm.** Food lifts off easily before it cools and sets.
2. **Scrape first.** A plastic tool like the [Lodge Pan Scrapers](https://www.amazon.com/Lodge-Scrapers-Handheld-Polycarbonate-Cleaners/dp/B0039UU9UO/?tag=kitchenpot-20) removes stuck bits without gouging the finish. The corner shape fits the curved wall of a skillet.
3. **Scrub with hot water.** For stubborn spots, a [Lancaster Chainmail Scrubber](https://www.awin1.com/cread.php?awinmid=95395&awinaffid=1956629&clickref=how-to-season-cast-iron-skillet&ued=https%3A%2F%2Flancastercastiron.com%2Fproducts%2Fchain-mail-scrubber) cuts through burnt-on food while leaving well-bonded seasoning in place. It rinses clean and never holds smells like a sponge.
4. **Use soap if you want.** A small squeeze of modern dish soap is fine. Old soaps contained lye, which did strip seasoning. Today's dish soap does not.
5. **Dry on the burner.** Towel it off, then heat it for 2 to 3 minutes until every drop is gone.
6. **Oil lightly.** While it is warm, wipe on a few drops of oil and buff it until it looks dry.

### Never Do These

- **Do not soak it.** An hour in the sink can leave rust spots.
- **Do not put it in the dishwasher.** The long hot wash and harsh detergent strip the coat and rust the pan.
- **Do not let it air dry in a rack.** If your [dish drying rack](/blog/dish-drying-rack-ideas-for-small-kitchens/) is crowded, the skillet goes straight to the stove instead.
- **Do not store food in it.** Acidic leftovers like tomato sauce eat into the seasoning overnight.

### How to Season a Cast Iron Skillet After Washing

After every wash, the burner-dry and light oil wipe in Steps 5 and 6 above is all the seasoning it needs. A full oven round is only for pans that look dull, grey or patchy. Cooking with fat does the rest. Every time you sear, fry or roast in the pan, you add a fresh, thin layer.

## How to Remove Rust From Cast Iron and Re-Season It

Rust looks alarming, but cast iron almost always recovers. Light orange spots come off in minutes.

### Light Surface Rust

1. Scrub the rust spots with a [Lodge Rust Eraser](https://www.amazon.com/Lodge-Rust-Eraser-Size-Black/dp/B0958H3TWC/?tag=kitchenpot-20) or fine steel wool until you see grey metal.
2. Wash with warm, soapy water to remove the rust dust. Rinse well.
3. Dry on the burner for 3 to 5 minutes.
4. Do 2 to 4 oven rounds at 450 to 500°F, following the step-by-step method above.

### Heavy Rust

For thick, flaky rust over the whole pan, mix equal parts white vinegar and water. Submerge the pan for 30 minutes to 1 hour, checking every 15 minutes. Pull it out as soon as the rust scrubs away easily. Vinegar eats bare iron too, so a long soak can pit the surface.

Rinse, scrub with steel wool, dry on the burner right away and oil it lightly, because bare iron starts to rust again within minutes. Then do 3 to 4 full oven rounds.

### When to Give Up on a Pan

Replace the pan if it has a crack, a hole or a warped bottom that rocks on the burner. A rocking pan heats unevenly and is risky on glass tops. Our guide to [fixing a warped pan](/blog/how-to-fix-a-warped-pan/) explains which pans can be saved, and [what your cookware sounds are telling you](/blog/what-your-cookware-sounds-are-telling-you/) helps you spot a hidden crack by its ring.

## Why Is My Cast Iron Sticky, Flaking or Leaving Black Residue?

Most problems come from too much oil, too little heat or harsh cleaning. Find yours here.

| Problem | Likely cause | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Sticky or tacky surface | Too much oil, or oven under 450°F | Bake again at 450 to 500°F for 1 hour without new oil |
| Brown, blotchy patches | Uneven or thick coats | Scrub lightly, thinner coats, 2 more oven rounds |
| Flaking or peeling | Thick layers, flaxseed oil, or seasoning over old gunk | Scrub off loose flakes, re-season with thin coats |
| Black specks in food | Loose carbon or flaking seasoning | Scrub with chainmail, wash, oil and bake |
| Black residue on the towel | Normal carbon from cooking | Wash, dry and oil after cooking; it fades with use |
| Dull grey spots | Seasoning worn by acid or scrubbing | Stovetop touch-up or 1 oven round |
| Orange spots | Water left on the iron | Remove rust, then oven season |
| Food sticking | New coat, low heat or too little fat | Preheat longer, use more fat, cook fatty foods |

### Why Is My Cast Iron Sticky After Seasoning?

There was more oil on the pan than the heat could cure, usually from a skipped wipe-off or an oven below 450°F.

Put the pan back in a 450 to 500°F oven for 1 hour with no added oil. That cures most mild stickiness. If it still feels tacky, scrub it with hot water and a chainmail scrubber, dry it and season again with much thinner coats.

### Why Is My Cast Iron Flaking or Peeling?

Flakes mean a layer did not bond to the one beneath it. Thick coats, flaxseed oil, old greasy buildup and cold water on a hot pan are the usual causes.

Scrub off every loose flake until the surface feels smooth. Small bare patches are fine. Then season with 2 to 3 thin oven coats. A flat-edged wooden tool like the [Lancaster Wooden Turner Spatula](https://www.awin1.com/cread.php?awinmid=95395&awinaffid=1956629&clickref=how-to-season-cast-iron-skillet&ued=https%3A%2F%2Flancastercastiron.com%2Fproducts%2Fwood-pancake-turner) is gentle on a fresh coat. It comes in left- and right-handed versions.

### What Is the Black Residue on My Cast Iron?

A little black on a towel is normal carbon from cooked food. It is harmless and fades as the coat matures.

Lots of black residue every time means the pan needs a proper clean. Scrub it with hot water and a drop of soap, dry it, oil it thinly and do one oven round. If it keeps coming back, too much oil is building up between washes, so wipe the oil off harder.

### Why Is Food Sticking to My Seasoned Pan?

Even a good coat needs preheating. Heat the pan on medium for 3 to 5 minutes before adding fat and food. Cold iron and cold food are the most common reasons eggs weld themselves down.

A young coat also needs time, so cook with a bit more fat for the first few months. Until then, a pan from our [nonstick pan guide](/blog/best-nonstick-pans-with-buying-guide/) can handle breakfast eggs.

## Why Is My Cast Iron Smoking So Much?

Light smoke in the first 15 to 20 minutes of oven seasoning is normal. Heavy smoke throughout means too much oil. Pull the pan out, let it cool a little, wipe off the excess and restart the round.

Smoke while cooking usually means leftover oil from the last wipe. Wipe thinner, and never preheat an empty pan on high.

In a small apartment, smoke reaches the smoke alarm quickly. Run the hood on high, and keep the filter clean so it actually pulls air; our guide on [how often to clean a range hood filter](/blog/how-often-to-clean-a-range-hood-filter/) has a simple schedule. If you cook in cast iron often and have no real hood, an [under-cabinet range hood](/blog/best-under-cabinet-range-hood/) makes a big difference.

## How Often Should You Season Cast Iron?

Season in small doses all the time, and in big doses rarely.

- **After every use:** dry on the burner and wipe on a few drops of oil.
- **Every few weeks:** a stovetop touch-up if spots look dry or grey.
- **Once or twice a year:** a full oven round or two for a pan in steady use.
- **Right away:** after rust removal, heavy flaking, or a dishwasher accident.

Cooking is seasoning. A skillet used for bacon, steak, potatoes and [one-pan meals](/blog/one-pan-meals-for-one/) several times a week may never need another oven round.

To save energy, run a round right after baking dinner, while the oven is hot. Our guide on [cutting kitchen energy use in a small apartment](/blog/how-to-reduce-kitchen-energy-use-in-a-small-apartment/) has more tricks like this.

## Small-Kitchen Habits That Protect Your Seasoning

These habits keep the coat in good shape when space is tight.

**Store it dry, with air around it.** Moisture trapped under stacked pans causes rust. Place a folded paper towel or flour sack towel inside the skillet before you stack anything on it. **Leave it on the stove.** In a tiny kitchen, the skillet can live on a back burner. It stays warm and dry after cooking, and it is always ready. This works well if you are [cooking a full meal on two burners](/blog/how-to-cook-a-full-meal-with-only-two-burners/).
