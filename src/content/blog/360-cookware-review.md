Some of that pitch holds up. Some of it is just good covered cooking with a new name. This 360 Cookware review sorts one from the other.

**The short version:** 360 is worth the money if you cook a lot of vegetables, grains and braises with the lid on and you want thick, USA-made stainless steel that lasts. If you mostly sear and want fast heat, you'll get more pan for less money from a 5-ply brand like Misen.

## What Is Vapor Cooking, Really?

Vapor cooking is covered cooking at low heat with little or no added water. 360 trademarks the name, but the idea is older. It's the same idea behind most [waterless cookware](/blog/best-waterless-cookware-reviews/).

Here's the method, as 360 lays it out on its [Vapor Cooking page](https://www.360cookware.com/pages/vapor-cooking):

1. Rinse the food and put it in the pan with little or no added water.
2. Put the lid on and heat over medium.
3. When vapor starts to escape from under the lid, give the lid a gentle spin.
4. If it spins freely, the seal has formed. Turn the heat to low.
5. Leave the lid on until the food is done.

The spin test is the clever part. As steam hits the cooler rim, it condenses into a thin ring of water between lid and pan. That ring acts like a gasket. The lid floats on it, which is why it spins.

### What Vapor Cooking Does Well

It keeps water-soluble vitamins in the pan. Vitamin C and several B vitamins dissolve into cooking water. When you boil broccoli and drain it, some of those vitamins go down the sink. With little water to drain, less is lost.

It saves energy. Once the seal forms, low heat keeps the food cooking. A thick pan also holds that heat, so the burner does less work. We cover this in [how to choose energy-efficient cookware](/blog/how-to-choose-energy-efficient-cookware-for-a-small-kitchen/).

It cuts the oil you need for vegetables. Food steams in its own moisture, so you don't need fat to stop it sticking.

### What Vapor Cooking Doesn't Do

It doesn't make every meal oil-free. Searing meat still needs a hot pan and a little fat. So does a crisp egg.

It isn't magic nutrition. 360's own page makes no numbers claim about nutrients and cites no studies. The benefit comes from using less water, and a steamer basket does much the same.

It isn't only possible in 360 pans. Any heavy pan with a snug, domed lid can form a similar seal. The 360 lids are just well matched to their pans.

It doesn't suit everything. Pasta needs a pot of boiling water. Rice needs a measured amount of liquid. Vapor cooking shines with vegetables, potatoes, chicken, fish and braises.

## How 360 Cookware Is Built

360 pans use three layers. The inside is surgical-grade stainless steel. The middle is aluminum. The outside is T-400 series stainless, which is magnetic.

Each layer has a job. Aluminum spreads heat fast and evenly. The inner steel is non-reactive, so tomatoes and wine don't pick up a metallic taste. The magnetic outer steel is what lets the pans work on [induction cooktops](/blog/best-cookware-for-induction-cooktop/).

The whole sandwich runs up the sides, not just the base. That's called fully clad. Our [guide to stainless steel cookware](/blog/what-is-stainless-steel-cookware/) explains why that matters for even browning.

### T-304 or T-316 Surgical Stainless?

360's current product and materials pages say T-316, described as 18/10 (18% chromium, 10% nickel). An older FAQ on the same site still says T304. Both are food-safe.

The difference is small but real. T-316 adds molybdenum, which helps the steel resist pitting from salt. "Surgical grade" is a marketing term, not a separate standard.

If you have a nickel sensitivity, note that both grades contain nickel.

### Is .110 Gauge Thick?

Yes. 360 says its bonded wall is about .110 inches, or 2.7 mm. Many tri-ply pans sit a bit thinner. In published third-party measurements, All-Clad D3 came in around .09 inches.

Thick walls hold heat and resist warping. The trade-off is weight and speed. The same third-party testing found a 360 pan took longer to boil water than All-Clad and Made In. If you want the full thickness story, see [All-Clad D3 vs D5](/blog/all-clad-d3-vs-d5/).

## Weight, Handles, Oven and Induction

**Weight.** 360 pans are heavy. The 7-inch fry pan weighs 2.5 pounds, per 360. The 3.5-quart sauté pan is closer to 7 pounds, per the published testing above.

**Handles.** Long handles are marketed as "stay cool." Larger pieces add a short helper handle on the opposite side. Short-handle versions of several pans exist, which fit better in the oven and in [tight cabinets](/blog/how-to-organize-pot-lids-in-a-small-kitchen/).

Lid knobs are steel, so they get hot on the stove. Keep a dry towel or mitt nearby.

**Oven.** 360 rates its cookware oven safe to 500°F. Its FAQ adds two limits: don't put pans in during preheating, and don't use them under the broiler.

**Induction.** Every 360 pan works on induction because of the T-400 outer layer. Our [induction cooktop guide](/blog/best-induction-cooktop-for-the-money/) covers the burners themselves.

## Warranty, Returns and Care

360 covers its cookware and bakeware against defects in workmanship and materials for life. The [warranty page](https://www.360cookware.com/pages/lifetime-warranty) excludes normal wear, misuse and failure to follow care instructions. Electrical parts, like the slow cooker base, get 2 years.

One detail trips people up. The FAQ calls 360 items dishwasher safe. But product pages recommend hand washing. And the warranty excludes damage from dishwasher detergents with surfactants, hydroxides or abrasives.

So wash by hand. Warm soapy water, a soft sponge and a quick dry keep the finish bright. For stuck-on food and water spots, our guide on [how to clean stainless steel pans](/blog/how-to-clean-stainless-steel-pans/) walks through each fix.

Blue or gold marks after high heat are normal. They're heat tint, and [why stainless steel turns rainbow](/blog/why-does-stainless-steel-turn-rainbow/) explains how to polish them off.

## Is 360 Cookware Worth the Money?

It depends on how you cook.

360 makes sense if you steam or braise vegetables most nights and like to set a lid and walk away. It also makes sense if buying USA-made matters to you and you'll keep the pans for decades.

It makes less sense if you mostly sear, stir-fry or want pans that heat fast. It also isn't a good fit if weight bothers your wrists. A 7-pound sauté pan full of food is a lot to lift.
