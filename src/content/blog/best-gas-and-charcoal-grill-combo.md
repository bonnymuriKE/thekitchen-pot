Yes, you can have a grill with a gas side and a charcoal side. Most combo grills split the two fuels into separate chambers under one frame.

That means you fire up propane on a Tuesday and light charcoal on a Saturday, without owning two grills.

That flexibility comes with a trade-off. A combo unit is almost never as strong at either job as a dedicated grill built for just one fuel.

This guide covers five combo grills worth considering, what "combo" actually means on the label, and the specs that matter before you buy one.

## Why Get a Combo Instead of Just One Fuel?

Gas and charcoal solve different problems, and a combo grill exists because most people run into both problems in the same month.

Gas lights in under a minute and holds a steady temperature with a knob turn. That's what you want on a Tuesday, when dinner needs to happen in 30 minutes, not after a bag of charcoal burns down to the right stage.

Charcoal takes longer to light and needs more attention, but it sears harder and adds a smoky character gas alone doesn't produce. That's what you want on a Saturday, when the cook is the plan, not a chore squeezed before dinner.

A combo grill puts both options under one frame so you're not choosing which need to sacrifice. The [difference between a skillet and a frying pan](/blog/difference-between-skillet-and-frying-pan/) is a similar kind of question, really: right tool, right job, and sometimes you want both tools within reach.

## Convertible vs. Two-Chamber: Read the Fine Print

"Combo grill" gets used for two truly different designs, and mixing them up leads to disappointment.

A **two-chamber combo**, like four of the five grills above, puts a full gas burner system on one side and a full charcoal firebox on the other. Each side usually gets its own lid and its own grates. That means you can run both at once, searing a burger on gas while chicken thighs go low and slow on charcoal.

A **convertible hybrid**, like the Gas2Coal, has one cooking surface that switches between fuels. You get one fuel at a time, never both together, in exchange for a smaller, often cheaper unit.

Neither design is wrong. The difference just needs to match what you actually want from the grill, so check which type you're looking at before you buy.

## How Much Cooking Space Do You Actually Get?

Total cooking area on the spec sheet doesn't tell the whole story, since that number usually includes a smaller warming rack that isn't meant for direct cooking.

What matters more is how the space splits between gas and charcoal. On the Char-Griller E5030, for example, that split leans toward the primary grate with less room for the charcoal side. On the 5050 Duo, the charcoal side actually gets more room than the gas side, at 580 square inches to 438.

## One Lid or Two?

Two lids means two independent cooking zones you can run at different temperatures at the same time. That's the setup on the Char-Griller, Dyna-Glo, and Royal Gourmet models above.

One lid, as on the Gas2Coal, means one cooking surface and one fuel active at a time. It's simpler and usually cheaper, but it removes the "run both at once" flexibility that draws people to combo grills in the first place.

If simultaneous dual-fuel cooking is the whole reason you want a combo, confirm the lid count before buying. Some listing photos make this hard to tell at a glance.

## Gas-Side BTU: What It Actually Buys You

BTU measures heat output, not how well a grill cooks, but it's still a useful number for comparing gas sides against each other.

More BTU generally means faster preheating and a higher ceiling for searing, though burner design and lid sealing affect real-world performance just as much. A grill with more BTU but a leaky lid can still underperform a lower-BTU grill that holds heat well.

Among these five, the 5050 Duo and the Gas2Coal both lead on raw BTU, at 40,800 and 40,000 respectively. The Char-Griller E5030 sits lowest at 24,000, which is enough for normal grilling but won't sear a thick steak as fast as the higher-BTU options. For comparison, a dedicated three-burner [gas grill under $500](/blog/best-gas-grills-under-500/) often runs 30,000 to 36,000 BTU on gas alone, since it isn't splitting the budget with a charcoal side.

## The Honest Downside: a Jack of Two Trades

Here's the trade-off worth saying plainly. A combo grill is almost always worse at gas grilling than a dedicated gas grill at the same price. It's also worse at charcoal grilling than a dedicated charcoal grill at the same price.

Splitting the budget and the frame between two fuel systems means less metal, fewer burners, or a smaller firebox than a single-fuel grill would get for the same money. The gas side often runs lower BTU than a dedicated gas grill, and the charcoal side is often smaller than a dedicated kettle or barrel grill.

That's not a reason to avoid combo grills. It's a reason to buy one for the right reason: you truly want both fuels in one footprint, not because you think you're getting two full-price grills for one price.

If you already know you'll use one fuel far more than the other, a dedicated grill in that fuel usually performs better for the same money. ## Assembly: What to Expect

Combo grills take longer to assemble than single-fuel grills, and it's worth planning for that rather than being surprised by it.

A dedicated gas grill usually has one burner system, one set of grates, and one cart to bolt together. A combo grill doubles most of that: two burner or firebox systems, often two lids, two thermometers, and two sets of grates, all attached to one shared frame.

Owner reviews across these models commonly mention assembly times of one and a half to two and a half hours, longer with one person than two. Having a second set of hands to hold panels steady while you bolt them speeds this up considerably.

A few tips that apply across brands:

1. Lay out every bag of hardware before starting, and sort by size.
2. Attach wheels and the base frame first, before anything heavier goes on top.
3. Leave bolts slightly loose until the whole frame is together, then tighten everything at the end.
4. Read through the full manual once before starting, since combo grills often assemble in a different order than a standard gas grill.

## Keeping a Combo Grill Running

A grill with two fuel systems needs a bit more upkeep than a single-fuel one, mostly because there's more to look after.

Empty and clean the ash pan on the charcoal side after every use, since leftover ash holds moisture and speeds up rust in the firebox. Wipe down grates while they're still warm, not hot, using a grill brush, and check gas burner ports for clogs if flames start burning unevenly.

If you use [a meat thermometer for smoking](/blog/best-meat-thermometer-for-smoking/) on the charcoal side, [clean it properly after each use](/blog/how-to-clean-a-meat-thermometer/). Wiping it on a towel isn't enough, since raw meat residue lingers in the probe grooves.

Heat-resistant gloves earn their keep on a combo grill more than on a single-fuel one, since you're handling both an open charcoal firebox and hot cast-iron grates. Our [roundup of heat-resistant gloves](/blog/10-best-heat-resistant-gloves-for-cooking/) covers options built for that kind of direct heat.

## Is a Combo Grill Worth the Extra Cost?

Combo grills usually cost more than a comparable single-fuel grill, so it's a fair question whether that premium earns its keep.

If you already own a dedicated gas grill and you're happy with it, adding a charcoal-only grill next to it may serve you better than replacing both with one combo. You'd get full performance from each fuel instead of the split-the-difference performance a combo delivers.

If you're buying your first grill and truly want both fuels without two separate footprints, a combo earns its price. It saves patio space and one purchase decision. Our piece on [whether expensive cookware is actually worth it](/blog/is-expensive-cookware-actually-worth-it/) covers a similar spend-more-or-not question for indoor gear. The same logic applies here: pay for what you'll actually use, not for a feature that sounds good on a spec sheet.
