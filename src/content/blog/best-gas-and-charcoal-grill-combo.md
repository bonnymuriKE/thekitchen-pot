## What "Combo Grill" Actually Means

The label covers two designs that behave nothing alike.

A **two-chamber combo** is a gas grill and a charcoal grill bolted to one cart. Each side has its own grates, its own lid and usually its own gauge. Both can run at once, so chicken can smoke on charcoal while burgers sear on gas.

A **convertible hybrid** has one chamber. A charcoal tray drops in over the gas burners when you want coals, and comes out when you want propane. One fuel at a time, always.

Neither design is wrong. The FlexFuel Plus, Texas Trio and E5030 are two-chamber. The Nexgrill is convertible. Count the lids in the product photo and you will know which one you are looking at.

## How the Charcoal Side Controls Heat

Charcoal has no knob, so heat comes from two things: how close the coals sit to the food, and how much air reaches them.

Distance is the blunt control. An adjustable tray, like the one on the FlexFuel Plus, cranks the coal bed up for searing or down for a slower cook. Moving coals two inches closer makes a bigger difference than most people expect.

Air is the fine control. Vents and dampers decide how much oxygen the fire gets, and a fire starved of air burns cooler. Open the bottom damper to climb, close it partway to settle.

Convertible hybrids give you less of both. The coals sit at a fixed height over the burners, so you are left adjusting fuel quantity and lid position. That is workable, just less precise.

## What Gas-Side BTU Actually Buys You

BTU measures fuel burned per hour, not how well a grill cooks. It is still useful for comparing grills of similar size.

What matters is BTU per square inch of grate. The Nexgrill puts 48,000 main-burner BTU under 461 square inches, so heat density is high and preheating is quick. The E5030 spreads 24,000 BTU over 600 square inches, so it climbs slower.

Lid sealing and burner layout then decide how much of that heat stays put. A leaky lid wastes output no matter how big the number is.

For thick steaks, high heat density beats raw BTU every time. That is the same argument behind infrared burners, and it is why a smaller, hotter grate often outperforms a larger, cooler one.

## The Honest Trade-Off of One Grill Instead of Two

A combo grill is usually a little worse at gas than a dedicated gas grill of the same price. It is also usually worse at charcoal than a dedicated charcoal grill of the same price.

The reason is simple arithmetic. One budget builds two fuel systems, so something gives: fewer burners, thinner steel, or a smaller firebox.

That is a fine deal if you truly want both fuels in one footprint. It is a poor deal if you are hoping to get two full grills for the price of one.

So be honest about your split. If nine cooks out of ten will be propane, buy a [gas grill under $500](/blog/best-gas-grills-under-500/) and skip the charcoal side. If most cooks are long and smoky, a [propane smoker](/blog/best-propane-smoker/) or an [electric smoker](/blog/how-to-use-an-electric-smoker/) serves you better.

## Assembly Takes Longer Than You Think

You are building two cooking systems on one frame, so plan for an afternoon rather than an hour.

Owner reviews across dual-fuel models commonly report one and a half to three hours. A second person helps most when the chambers go onto the cart, because those panels are awkward before the bolts are in.

1. Sort every bag of hardware by size before you open the frame parts.
2. Build the cart and fit the wheels first, so the heavy chambers land on something stable.
3. Leave bolts finger-tight until the whole frame is square, then torque everything.
4. Read the manual through once. Combo grills often assemble in a different order than a plain gas grill.
5. Do a leak check on the propane fittings with soapy water before the first cook.

Keep heavy gloves nearby from the first cook onward. You will be handling an open firebox and hot cast iron in the same session, and our roundup of [heat resistant gloves for cooking](/blog/10-best-heat-resistant-gloves-for-cooking/) covers gear rated for that.

## Keeping a Dual-Fuel Grill Alive

Two fuel systems means twice the maintenance, but none of it is difficult.

Empty the ash pan after every charcoal cook. Cold ash holds moisture against bare steel, and that is how fireboxes rust from the inside.

Brush the grates while they are warm rather than cold. Warm grease lifts; cold grease sets. Cast iron grates also want a thin wipe of oil after cleaning, for the same reason a skillet does.

Check the gas burner ports whenever flames look uneven or yellow. Spiders and grease block them, and a clogged port starves that section of the grate.

Cover the grill when it is not in use. Painted steel and stainless both stain in wet weather, and stainless is not immune, whatever the label suggests. Our explainer on [what stainless steel cookware is](/blog/what-is-stainless-steel-cookware/) covers why that thin protective layer needs oxygen to keep working.

Food safety does not change with the fuel. Steaks and chops need 145°F with a three-minute rest, ground beef 160°F, and poultry 165°F, per the [USDA safe temperature chart](https://www.foodsafety.gov/food-safety-charts/safe-minimum-internal-temperatures). Leftovers then follow the same clock as any other cooked meat, which our guide to [how long chicken lasts in the fridge](/blog/how-long-does-chicken-last-in-the-fridge/) spells out.

## Accessories That Earn Their Space

A few extras really do change how often you use each side of the grill.
