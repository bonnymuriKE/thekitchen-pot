Not owning the exact pan a recipe calls for is one of the most common reasons home bakers either skip a recipe entirely or wing it and hope for the best. Neither has to be the answer.

Pan substitution comes down to one number: volume. Once you know how much batter a pan actually holds, swapping it for a different shape or size is just math, not guesswork.

## The Short Answer

Compare the volume of your pan to the volume the recipe calls for using the chart below. If the volumes are close, the swap works with minor adjustments. If your pan is deeper than the original, lower the oven temperature slightly and bake longer. If it's shallower, raise the temperature slightly and bake for less time.

Fill any pan no more than about two-thirds full of batter, regardless of which size you're using.

## Why This Matters More in a Small Kitchen

Owning every pan size a recipe might ever call for isn't realistic in most kitchens, and it's especially unrealistic in a smaller one with limited cabinet space.

A well-stocked small kitchen usually means a handful of versatile pans rather than a full wall of specialty bakeware. ## Measuring a Pan's True Volume

Before you can substitute anything, you need to know what you're actually working with, since pan sizes vary slightly between manufacturers.

**To measure width and length**, measure across the top of the pan from inside edge to inside edge, not including the outer rim or lip.

**To measure volume**, pour water into the empty pan by the cupful, counting as you go, until the water reaches the very brim. That total is the pan's true capacity.

This second step matters more than it seems. Two pans labeled the same size by different brands can hold noticeably different amounts, so measuring your actual pan beats trusting the label alone.

## How to Use This Chart to Substitute Pans

Once you know your pan's volume and the recipe's intended pan volume, the substitution logic is straightforward.

**If the volumes are close**, within a cup or two, you can usually swap pans with little to no adjustment beyond watching the bake time.

**If your pan holds noticeably less**, split the batter between two pans, or reduce the recipe, rather than overfilling a single smaller pan.

**If your pan holds noticeably more**, the batter will spread thinner, which usually means a shorter bake time and a slightly higher temperature to compensate for the thinner layer.

A quick example: a recipe calling for two 9-inch round pans, roughly 16 cups combined, converts reasonably well to a single 9x13 pan at around 14 to 15 cups, since the totals are close enough that only the bake time needs real attention.

## Adjusting Temperature and Time

Volume gets you most of the way to a successful substitution, but depth changes things too.

**A deeper pan than the original** concentrates the same batter into a taller shape. Lower the oven temperature by about 25 degrees Fahrenheit and expect a longer bake time, since heat takes longer to reach the center.

**A shallower pan than the original** spreads the batter thinner. Raise the temperature by about 25 degrees Fahrenheit and expect a shorter bake time, since there's less batter for heat to penetrate.

**A same-depth pan with a different footprint**, like swapping a round pan for a square one of similar volume, usually needs little to no temperature change, just a few extra minutes of baking time to be safe.

Always rely on a toothpick test or visual doneness cues rather than the clock alone when you've changed pan sizes, since every oven and substitution combination behaves slightly differently.

## Never Fill a Pan More Than Two-Thirds Full

This rule matters regardless of which pan you end up using.

Batter needs room to rise without spilling over the sides as it bakes. Filling a pan all the way to the brim, even if the volume technically matches, risks overflow, smoke, and a genuinely difficult cleanup.

If your available pan is on the smaller side for the recipe's volume, it's safer to split the batter across two pans or bake in batches than to force it all into one pan that's too full.

## Does Material Affect the Substitution Too?

Yes, and it's worth considering alongside size when you're swapping pans.

Glass holds heat longer than metal and continues cooking food after it leaves the oven, while metal heats and releases heat quickly. Our full comparison of [glass vs. metal bakeware](/blog/glass-vs-metal-bakeware-which-should-you-use/) covers how to adjust temperature when you're changing material, not just size, since both factors stack if you're substituting both at once.

If you're swapping from a recipe written for metal into a glass dish of a similar volume, combine both adjustments: lower the temperature by about 25 degrees Fahrenheit for the material change, and apply the depth-based adjustment above if the shapes differ significantly too.

## Common Substitutions Worth Knowing

A few swaps come up often enough to be worth memorizing rather than calculating each time.

**Two 9-inch rounds for one 9x13.** A very common cake substitution, since the volumes land close together.

**One 8-inch square for one 9-inch round.** Close in volume, and a reasonable swap for brownies or bar cookies baked in either shape.

**A muffin tin for a cake recipe.** Cupcakes generally need 18 to 22 minutes at the same temperature as a full cake, since the smaller portions cook through faster.

**A loaf pan for a bundt pan**, when the volumes are close. Expect a notably longer bake time, since the loaf shape is narrower and deeper than most bundt pans, which affects how quickly heat reaches the center.

## When Substitution Isn't a Good Idea

A few situations call for sourcing the actual pan rather than improvising.

Delicate, structured bakes like a layered cake meant for stacking and frosting depend on consistent, predictable dimensions. A significant size mismatch can throw off both the bake and the final presentation more than it's worth troubleshooting.

Recipes with very specific, tested ratios, like a cheesecake in a springform pan, are harder to substitute successfully, since both the pan's removable sides and its exact volume matter to the final texture.

In these cases, it's often simpler to borrow the right pan, buy an inexpensive one, or choose a different recipe better suited to what you already own.

## Substituting a Pan Size Is Not the Same as Scaling a Recipe

It's worth separating two different problems that often get confused with each other.

Pan substitution means baking the same amount of batter in a different container. Recipe scaling means actually changing how much batter you make, doubling or halving ingredient quantities to fill a different amount of pan space entirely.

If you want to keep the same amount of batter and just change the pan, use the volume comparison and temperature adjustments in this guide. If you actually want more or less finished product, scale the ingredients first, then choose a pan sized for that new volume.

## Converting Between Cupcakes and a Full Cake

This specific substitution comes up often enough to deserve its own explanation.

A standard cake recipe generally converts to 18 to 24 cupcakes, depending on how full you fill each cup. Fill cupcake liners about two-thirds full, the same rule that applies to full-size pans.

Bake time drops significantly for the smaller portions, usually to somewhere between 18 and 22 minutes at the same temperature the cake recipe specifies. Start checking a few minutes early, since cupcakes go from perfect to overdone faster than a full cake does.

## Does a Convection Oven Change Any of This?

Convection ovens circulate air with a fan, which affects baking time independent of whatever pan substitution you're making.

As a general rule, convection baking runs about 25 degrees Fahrenheit hotter than a conventional oven would need for the same result, or finishes in less time at the same temperature. If you're already adjusting temperature for a pan substitution, layer the convection adjustment on top of it rather than treating them as separate problems.

Check your specific oven's manual, since convection adjustments vary somewhat by manufacturer and model.

## A Simple Formula If You Want to Scale the Recipe Too

If you genuinely want to make more or less of a recipe, not just rehouse the same batter, a simple ratio gets you there.

Divide your pan's volume by the original recipe's pan volume. That number is your scaling multiplier. A 9-inch round pan at 8 cups, divided into a 9x13 pan at roughly 14 cups, gives a multiplier of about 1.75, meaning you'd scale every ingredient in the recipe up by that amount to fill the larger pan properly.

This is a different calculation from simple pan substitution, and it's worth doing the math rather than eyeballing it, since baking is less forgiving of rough ingredient estimates than most other cooking.

## A Few Ingredient-Specific Notes

A couple of recipe types respond to pan substitution a little differently than a basic cake batter does.

Dense batters, like banana bread or pound cake, are more sensitive to pan depth changes than light, airy cakes are, since heat has to travel further through dense batter to fully cook the center. Lean toward a smaller temperature adjustment and a longer check-in window with these.

Yeasted doughs, like a focaccia baked in a sheet pan, care more about surface area than exact volume, since the dough spreads to fill whatever footprint you give it. A substitution here is more about matching the pan's general size than calculating precise volume.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

* **Trusting the pan's labeled size without measuring.** Actual volume varies between brands.
* **Filling a pan more than two-thirds full.** This risks overflow and a messy oven.
* **Ignoring depth when only matching volume.** A deeper or shallower pan still needs a temperature adjustment.
* **Using the original bake time without checking doneness.** Substituted pans almost always need a different bake time.
* **Forcing a substitution on a structured or precise recipe.** Some bakes genuinely need their specified pan.
