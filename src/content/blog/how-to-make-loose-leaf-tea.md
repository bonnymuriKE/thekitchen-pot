Use about 2 to 3 grams of leaves per 8 ounces of water, heat the water to suit the tea, and steep 1 to 5 minutes. That is how to make loose leaf tea that tastes right.

Almost every bad cup comes from one of those three numbers being off. Get them right and even a cheap tin of loose tea beats most tea bags. Below you will find the exact amounts, a temperature and time chart for each type of tea, and simple methods for a mug, a teapot, a jar or no gear at all.

**In a hurry?** Weigh 2 to 3 grams of tea (about 1 teaspoon of most black teas) for each 8-ounce cup. Heat water to 175°F for green tea, 195°F for oolong and a full boil for black and herbal tea. Steep 2 minutes for green, 3 for oolong, 4 for black and 5 or more for herbal, then lift the leaves out or pour the tea off them. Taste, and change one thing at a time next round.

## How Much Loose Leaf Tea per Cup?

Our standard is **2 to 3 grams of tea per 8 ounces (240 ml) of water**. Start at 2.5 grams. If the cup tastes thin, go up half a gram next time rather than steeping longer.

The trouble with spoons is that tea leaves come in very different shapes. Some are tiny and dense. Others are long, twisted and full of air. Here is what 2.5 grams looks like in spoons for common styles:

| Tea style | Leaf shape | About 2.5 g is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Most black teas (English Breakfast, Assam, Ceylon) | Small, broken or chopped | 1 level teaspoon |
| Rolled oolong, gunpowder green | Tight little balls | 1 scant teaspoon |
| Japanese sencha | Thin, needle-like | 1 level teaspoon |
| Chinese green (long, twisted leaves) | Loose and airy | 1 heaping teaspoon to 1 tablespoon |
| White tea (Bai Mu Dan, Silver Needle) | Large, fluffy | 1 to 2 tablespoons |
| Rooibos | Fine needles | 1 level teaspoon |
| Chamomile, mint, flower blends | Light and bulky | 1 to 2 tablespoons |

### Scaling Up: per Liter, Quart and Gallon

For bigger batches, keep the same ratio. A liter (about 34 ounces) takes about 10 grams of tea. A quart is close enough to a liter that the same 10 grams works. A gallon of hot tea at drinking strength takes 40 to 45 grams. If you need help with the volume side, our [ounces in a quart chart](/blog/how-many-ounces-in-a-quart/) covers the conversions.

For iced tea, brew double strength (5 grams per 8 ounces) and pour it over a full glass of ice. The melting ice brings it back to normal strength.

## Water Temperature and Steep Time for Each Type of Tea

This is the chart to stick inside a cabinet door. Temperatures are where the water should be when it hits the leaves.

| Tea type | Water temperature | Steep time | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Green (Chinese) | 175 to 180°F | 2 to 3 min | Hotter water turns it grassy and sharp |
| Green (Japanese sencha, gyokuro) | 160 to 175°F | 1 to 2 min | The most heat-sensitive tea you will brew |
| White | 170 to 185°F | 2 to 4 min | Forgiving; can go longer without much bitterness |
| Oolong (green, rolled) | 185 to 195°F | 2 to 3 min | Leaves unfurl a lot; give them room |
| Oolong (dark, roasted) | 195 to 205°F | 3 to 4 min | Can take hotter water than green oolong |
| Black | 200 to 212°F | 3 to 5 min | Full boil is fine; 4 minutes suits most |
| Pu-erh | 205 to 212°F | 2 to 4 min | Rinse first (see the gongfu section) |
| Herbal and rooibos | 212°F | 5 to 7 min | No true tea leaf, so little risk of bitterness |

### How to Get the Right Temperature Without a Thermometer

If your kettle only boils, let the water cool before you pour. With the lid open, water just off the boil drops to roughly 195°F after a minute or two. It reaches roughly 180°F after about 4 minutes and 170°F after about 6. Those times vary with how much water is in the kettle, so treat them as a starting point.

A faster trick: mix 4 parts just-boiled water with 1 part cold tap water. That lands close to 180°F, right for most green and white teas. Pour the cold water into the mug or pot first, then the hot.

### Which Water to Use

Use fresh, cold water every time. Water that has been boiled over and over goes flat, and the tea tastes dull. If your tap water smells of chlorine or leaves heavy white crust in the kettle, filter it. That crust, called limescale, is just minerals from hard water. Clean it out every few weeks with our [electric kettle cleaning steps](/blog/how-to-clean-an-electric-kettle/), or it starts to flavor every cup.

## How to Make Loose Leaf Tea With an Infuser Basket

For one mug at a time, a basket infuser is the easiest method and the one we recommend most. A good basket is wide and deep, so the leaves can open fully instead of being squeezed into a ball.

1. Heat your water to the temperature for your tea.
2. Set the basket in your mug and add 2 to 3 grams of tea.
3. Pour the water over the leaves until the mug is full and the leaves are covered.
4. Cover the mug with a saucer or the infuser lid to hold in heat.
5. Start a timer. When it goes off, lift the basket out and rest it on the lid or a saucer.
6. Taste. Adjust the amount or time next round, not the temperature.

## How to Make Loose Leaf Tea in a Teapot

A teapot is the better choice for two cups or more, or for one person who likes a refill. The method is the same as a mug, with two extra habits that make a big difference.

1. **Warm the pot.** Swirl a little hot water in it for 20 seconds, then pour it out. A cold pot steals heat from the brewing water.
2. **Add the leaves.** Use 2 to 3 grams for every 8 ounces of water the pot holds.
3. **Pour and time.** Fill with water at the right temperature, put the lid on and start the timer.
4. **Pour it all off.** When the time is up, remove the strainer or pour every drop into cups or a second jug. Tea left sitting on the leaves keeps brewing and turns bitter.

## How to Brew Loose Leaf Tea in a Mug With Nothing Else

You can drop the leaves straight into a mug and drink around them. Tea drinkers in China have done it this way for centuries, and it is often called "grandpa style."

It works best with large, whole leaves that sink, like Chinese green teas, white teas and big-leaf oolongs. Use a little less tea than usual, about 1.5 to 2 grams per mug, and slightly cooler water. The leaves keep brewing the whole time, so the light dose keeps the tea from getting harsh. When the mug is a third full, top it up with more hot water and keep going.

Avoid this with small, broken black teas. They float, end up in your mouth and get bitter fast.

## How to Make Loose Leaf Tea Without an Infuser or Strainer

No infuser? Brew the leaves loose, then separate them. Here are the methods that work, from best to last resort.

### Use a French Press

A coffee French press is a large tea infuser in disguise. Add the leaves, pour in the water, put the lid on with the plunger up, and time it. Press the plunger down slowly and pour everything out right away. Do not leave tea sitting in the press; the leaves keep brewing under the filter. Wash it well first, because old coffee oil carries into the tea.

### Pour Through a Sieve or Coffee Filter

Brew the leaves loose in a mug, jar or measuring cup. When the timer goes, pour the tea through any fine kitchen sieve into a second mug. No sieve? Set a paper coffee filter in a funnel or a pour-over cone and pour through it. It drains slowly, so start pouring 30 seconds early. If you already own a dripper from our [pour over coffee maker guide](/blog/best-pour-over-coffee-maker/), you have a ready-made tea strainer.

### Make It in a Saucepan

For milky black tea, a small saucepan works well. Bring the water to a boil, turn off the heat, add the leaves and cover for 4 minutes. Add milk and warm it gently without boiling, then pour through a sieve. This is the base for a quick chai. ## Gongfu Style: More Leaf, Less Water, Short Steeps

Gongfu is a Chinese way of brewing that uses a small pot, a lot of leaf and very short steeps. You drink many tiny cups from one serving of leaves, and each one tastes a little different. It suits oolong, pu-erh and dark teas best.

Here is the simple version:

1. Use about 5 grams of tea for 100 ml (about 3.5 ounces) of water. That is roughly double the usual ratio.
2. Rinse the leaves first. Pour hot water over them, wait 5 seconds and pour it out. This wakes up rolled leaves and washes dust off pu-erh.
3. Steep the first round for 10 to 15 seconds, then pour every drop out.
4. Add 5 to 10 seconds to each round after that.
5. Keep going until the tea tastes thin. Good oolong often gives 6 to 8 rounds.

A traditional lidded bowl called a gaiwan is common, but any small teapot works. The KINTO glass pot above handles gongfu fine if you only fill it partway. Keep the kettle on its keep-warm setting so every round starts at the same temperature.

## How to Make Iced Tea and Cold Brew Tea With Loose Leaves

There are two good ways to get cold tea from loose leaves. One is fast. The other is smoother.

### Hot-Brewed Iced Tea

Brew double strength, about 5 grams per 8 ounces, using normal temperature and time. Strain it straight over a tall glass packed with ice. The tea chills in seconds and the ice dilutes it to the right strength. This is the method for black tea with lemon. Our guide to [the juice in one lemon](/blog/how-much-juice-is-in-one-lemon/) helps if you are squeezing for a whole pitcher.

### Cold Brew Tea

Cold brew uses no heat at all. The tea steeps slowly in the fridge and comes out sweet and smooth, with almost no bitterness. It is very hard to ruin.

1. Add 12 to 15 grams of tea per liter of cold, filtered water.
2. Cover and refrigerate.
3. Steep green and white teas 4 to 8 hours. Steep black, oolong and herbal teas 8 to 12 hours.
4. Strain out the leaves and keep the tea cold. Drink it within 2 to 3 days.

## Can You Resteep Loose Leaf Tea?

Yes, and you should. Whole loose leaves still have plenty of flavor after the first cup. Each extra steep lowers the cost per cup and often brings out a different side of the tea.

| Tea type | Good steeps (mug or teapot) | Add each time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Green | 2 to 3 | 30 seconds |
| White | 2 to 3 | 1 minute |
| Oolong | 3 to 5 | 30 to 60 seconds |
| Black | 1 to 2 | 1 minute |
| Pu-erh | 4 or more | 30 seconds |
| Herbal | 1 to 2 | 2 minutes |

A few rules for resteeping:

- Drain the leaves fully between steeps. Leaves sitting in a puddle of tea keep brewing and turn the next cup bitter.
- Use wet leaves within a few hours. Do not leave them overnight at room temperature; they can grow mold.
- The second steep of a green or white tea is often the best one, once the leaves have fully opened.

## Is Loose Leaf Tea Better Than Tea Bags?

For flavor, usually yes. Most tea bags hold small broken pieces, sometimes called fannings or dust. They brew fast and strong but go bitter quickly. Loose tea is larger leaves, so it keeps more of its aroma and brews a rounder cup. The leaves also have room to open, which they cannot do inside a tight bag.

Loose tea also wins on cost per cup once you resteep. The trade-off is one more thing to rinse, which a basket infuser turns into a 10-second job.

Pyramid-shaped bags that hold whole leaves come close, so keep those for the office.

## How to Store Loose Leaf Tea So It Stays Fresh

Tea has four enemies: air, light, heat and smells. Keep it away from all four and it lasts a long time.

- **Airtight.** The bag it came in is rarely good enough once opened.
- **Dark.** Clear jars on a sunny shelf fade green tea in weeks.
- **Cool.** Not above the stove, not beside the kettle, not on top of the fridge.
- **Away from strong smells.** Tea soaks up odors. Keep it apart from spices, coffee and onions.

## Why Your Loose Leaf Tea Tastes Bitter (and Other Common Mistakes)

Bitterness is the number one complaint, and it almost always has one of four causes. Fix the cause, not the symptom. Adding sugar to bitter tea hides the problem instead of solving it.

**Water too hot.** Boiling water on green or white tea brings out harsh, grassy notes. Drop the temperature to 175°F before you change anything else.

**Steeped too long.** Every tea turns bitter if you leave it long enough. Use a timer, and remove the leaves or pour off the tea when it goes.

**Leaves left in the pot.** This is the most common mistake with teapots. The first cup tastes great; the second, poured 10 minutes later, is harsh. Pour every drop into cups or a second jug, or lift the strainer out.

**Too much tea.** A heaping spoon of a dense tea can be 5 grams. Weigh it once to see what your spoon actually holds.

Other mistakes that ruin good leaves:

- **Weak tea fixed with longer steeping.** If tea tastes thin, add more leaf next time. Longer steeping adds bitterness faster than flavor.
- **A cramped infuser.** Small tea balls stop the leaves from opening. Use a wide basket or brew loose.
- **Reboiled or stale water.** Start with fresh, cold water every time.
- **A dirty kettle or pot.** Scale and old tea film add a dull, metallic taste. Descale the kettle and give the teapot a soak in hot water with a spoon of baking soda now and then.
- **Old tea.** If a green tea has turned brown and smells like hay, it is past its best. Use it for cold brew, which is kinder to tired leaves, and buy smaller amounts next time.
