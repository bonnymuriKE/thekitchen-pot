A range hood filter doesn't announce when it's overdue for cleaning. It just quietly gets worse at its job until one day the kitchen smells like dinner for hours after you've finished cooking.

This guide covers how often to actually clean it, the signs you've let it go too long, and the method that gets built-up grease off without damaging the filter.

## The Short Answer

Clean your range hood filter about once a month with regular cooking, or every one to two weeks if you fry often. Soak it in hot water with degreasing dish soap and a little baking soda for 10 to 15 minutes, then scrub with a soft brush.

If the filter looks yellowed, feels sticky, or your kitchen holds onto smoke and smell longer than it used to, it's overdue regardless of how long it's actually been.

## Why Cleaning Frequency Depends on How You Cook

There isn't one universal schedule, since grease buildup tracks directly with how often and how you cook.

**Daily cooking with typical amounts of oil** usually calls for monthly cleaning. This covers most home kitchens running through normal weeknight meals.

**Frequent frying, wok cooking, or high-smoke methods** push grease into the air faster and call for cleaning every one to two weeks instead.

**Light or occasional cooking** can often stretch the interval to every couple of months, since there's simply less airborne grease being produced in the first place.

If you genuinely can't remember the last time you cleaned it, treat that as your answer. It's overdue.

## Signs Your Filter Is Overdue

A few clear signs tell you it's time, even if you've lost track of the calendar.

**Visible grease buildup** is the most obvious sign, especially a filter that's turned yellow, brown, or sticky to the touch rather than its original metallic color.

**Reduced airflow or suction** happens as grease clogs the mesh, restricting how much air the hood can actually pull through it, even on the highest fan setting.

**Cooking smell that lingers longer than usual** is a direct result of that reduced airflow, since a clogged filter simply can't move as much air out of the kitchen.

**A visibly slower or weaker fan sound** can also be a clue. Our guide to [what your cookware's sounds are telling you](/blog/what-your-cookware-sounds-are-telling-you/) covers how appliance and cookware sounds change as performance drops, and a straining, muffled hood fan follows a similar pattern.

## The Soak-and-Scrub Method

This is the most reliable way to clean a grease-caked filter without damaging the mesh underneath.

1. Remove the filter from the range hood, following your model's release mechanism.
2. Fill a sink or basin with hot water.
3. Add a squirt of degreasing dish soap and about a quarter cup of baking soda.
4. Submerge the filter fully and let it soak for 10 to 15 minutes. Longer for heavier buildup.
5. Scrub with a soft-bristled brush, paying attention to the mesh layers and edges.
6. Rinse thoroughly under hot water.
7. Air-dry completely before reinstalling, since a wet filter reduces airflow just as much as a greasy one.

For filters with years of built-up grease, a stronger degreaser speeds this up considerably. ## Should You Use the Dishwasher Instead?

Many aluminum mesh filters are labeled dishwasher safe, and it's a genuinely faster option if yours is one of them.

Run it on a normal cycle, ideally without other dishes, since heavy grease can transfer. Repeated dishwasher cycles can dull the finish over time, so alternating between hand-washing and the dishwasher is a reasonable middle ground.

Charcoal or carbon filters should never go in the dishwasher. They aren't washable at all and need full replacement once they've absorbed as much odor as they can hold.

## Cleaning the Range Hood Itself, Not Just the Filter

The filter isn't the only part that collects grease, and skipping the rest of the hood means the filter gets dirty again faster than it should.

Wipe down the hood's exterior and the area directly around the filter housing while the filter itself is soaking. This catches grease before it has a chance to drip back onto a freshly cleaned filter.

For stainless steel range hood exteriors, a dedicated cleanser removes buildup without scratching the finish. ## When to Replace Instead of Clean

Cleaning has limits, and a few situations call for a new filter rather than another soak.

An aluminum mesh filter that's visibly warped, torn, or bent no longer sits properly and won't filter as effectively even after cleaning. Replace it rather than continuing to wash a damaged filter.

A filter that no longer returns close to its original color after a proper soak has likely absorbed grease deep enough into the metal that cleaning won't fully reverse it.

Charcoal and carbon filters, found on recirculating range hoods, can't be washed at all and need replacing on a set schedule regardless of how they look. ## Why This Matters Beyond Just Appearance

A clean filter isn't just about how your kitchen looks. It directly affects air quality and energy use too.

A clogged filter forces the range hood's fan to work harder to move the same amount of air, which uses more electricity than a clean, properly functioning filter would. ## Building It Into Your Routine

The easiest way to stay on top of this is to attach it to something you already do regularly, rather than relying on memory alone.

Clean the filter on the same day each month, like when you take out the trash or do a deeper kitchen clean, so it becomes routine rather than something you notice only once it's visibly bad.

Keeping degreaser and a soft brush stored near the kitchen, rather than in a separate cleaning closet, removes the small friction that often turns a five-minute task into a months-long delay.

## Ducted vs. Recirculating: Does It Change the Schedule?

The type of range hood you have changes what "cleaning" actually involves, not just how often.

A ducted hood vents air and grease outside, so the mesh filter is the only thing that needs regular cleaning. A recirculating hood adds a second filter, usually charcoal or carbon, that absorbs odor rather than just trapping grease, and that second filter follows a completely different maintenance schedule.

The aluminum mesh grease filter on either type follows the same monthly guidance covered above. The charcoal layer on a recirculating hood, by contrast, can't be washed at all and needs full replacement every 3 to 6 months regardless of how it looks.

## Charcoal Filter Replacement, Specifically

If your range hood doesn't vent outside, this second filter matters just as much as the one you can wash.

Charcoal filters absorb odor through the surface area of the carbon inside them, and that capacity saturates over time whether or not the filter looks dirty. A filter that's been in place for six months or more is likely doing far less than it looks like it's doing.

Mark a replacement date when you install a new charcoal filter, since there's no visual cue telling you it's time the way there is with a greasy mesh filter. ## Cooking Methods That Keep Your Filter Cleaner Longer

Some changes in how you cook reduce how much grease reaches the filter in the first place, which stretches out your cleaning interval.

Covering pans while frying or sautéing keeps a meaningful amount of grease from becoming airborne to begin with. ## A Simple Seasonal Deep Clean

Beyond the monthly filter soak, a deeper seasonal check catches what regular cleaning misses.

Every few months, wipe down the underside of the hood itself, not just the filter, since grease settles on the housing around the filter too. Check the fan blades if your model allows easy access, since buildup there can affect airflow independent of the filter's condition.

If you notice the fan running louder or straining more than it used to even with a clean filter, that's worth investigating further rather than assuming it's just the appliance aging. The same early-diagnostic principle covered earlier in this guide applies here: sound and airflow both signal a problem well before a filter looks visibly bad.

## Reinstalling the Filter Correctly

A clean filter that goes back in wrong can perform almost as poorly as a dirty one, so it's worth double-checking the fit.

Most mesh filters have a directional airflow arrow stamped somewhere on the frame. Installing it backward restricts airflow even when the filter itself is spotless.

Make sure the filter clicks or slides fully into place rather than sitting loosely in the housing. A gap around the edges lets unfiltered air and grease bypass the filter entirely, defeating the purpose of cleaning it in the first place.

## What a Truly Neglected Filter Looks Like

It's worth knowing what the far end of "overdue" actually looks like, since a lot of people underestimate how bad buildup can get before it becomes an obvious problem.

A filter that's gone a year or more without cleaning often looks less like metal mesh and more like a solid, amber-colored sheet, with individual holes barely visible under a layer of hardened grease. At that point, a single soak usually isn't enough, and it may take two or three cycles of soaking and scrubbing to work back down to bare metal.

This level of buildup is also a genuine fire risk, since hardened grease close to a heat source is exactly the kind of fuel a kitchen fire needs. If your filter has reached this state, treat cleaning it as a priority task rather than something to fit in eventually.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

* **Using abrasive scrubbers or steel wool.** These can scratch and damage aluminum mesh filters.
* **Skipping the soak step.** Scrubbing dry, caked-on grease is far harder than loosening it first.
* **Reinstalling a wet filter.** Standing moisture reduces airflow just as much as grease does.
* **Washing a charcoal filter.** These need full replacement, not cleaning.
* **Ignoring reduced airflow as just normal aging.** It's almost always a cleaning or replacement issue, not something you have to live with.

## **The Bottom Line**

Clean your range hood filter monthly. Fry often? Clean it every couple of weeks instead. Soak it in hot, soapy water with baking soda, then scrub gently. Watch for grease buildup, weak airflow, and lingering smoke. Those are your signs it's overdue. Replace it once it's warped, torn, or a spent charcoal filter.
