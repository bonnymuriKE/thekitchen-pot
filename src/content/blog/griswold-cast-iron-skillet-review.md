## Why Do People Still Want a Griswold Cast Iron Skillet?

Griswold pans were cast thin and then machined smooth on the inside. Most modern mass-market cast iron skips that step. So a Griswold is lighter, and its cooking surface feels like glass.

That smooth surface matters because seasoning is only a thin film of baked-on oil. On rough iron, the film has to fill tiny valleys before it feels slick. On smooth iron, it sits flat from the first few layers. Food has less to grab.

The light weight matters too. A pan you can lift with one hand is a pan you actually use. It also heats up faster, since there is less metal to warm. The flip side is that it cools down faster when you add food.

Then there is history. Griswold began in Erie, Pennsylvania in 1865. It became one of the best known cast iron names in the country. Owning one feels like owning a piece of that story. The question of whether a premium pan earns its price is one we look at in [is expensive cookware actually worth it](/blog/is-expensive-cookware-actually-worth-it/).

## Is Griswold Still Made? What Happened in 1957

No. Griswold is not made today, and it has not been for decades.

The Griswold family sold the company in 1946. In 1957 McGraw-Edison bought it and passed it to the Wagner Manufacturing Company. Production in Erie ended.

Wagner's owners kept using the Griswold name for a while. Those later pans were made at the Wagner plant in Sidney, Ohio, often on Wagner patterns. The name was dropped by 1973.

Collectors treat these post-1957 pieces differently. They usually lack "Erie" on the bottom, and they sell for much less. If you want the real thing, look for Erie markings.

## How to Identify a Real Vintage Griswold

Flip the pan over. Nearly everything you need is cast into the bottom.

### Read the Logo

Griswold changed its logo several times. The Cast Iron Collector's [history of the Griswold trademark](https://www.castironcollector.com/griswoldtm.php) is the best free reference. Here is the short version.

| Logo | Rough dates | Heat ring? | What collectors say |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| "Griswold's Erie" | 1905–1909 | Yes | Early and scarce |
| Slant logo, "Erie" | c. 1906–1916 | Yes | The classic italic logo |
| Slant logo, "Erie PA USA" (Slant EPU) | c. 1909–1929 | Yes | Highly sought after |
| Large block logo | c. 1920–1940 | Some yes, some smooth | Bold block letters, "Cast Iron Skillet" text |
| Small block logo | c. 1939–1957 | No (sizes 12 and up still had one) | The most common Griswold today |
| Post-1957 marks | 1957–1973 | No | Made in Sidney, Ohio, lower value |

Dates overlap because Griswold phased logos in and out. Treat them as rough windows, not exact years.

### Check the Pattern Number

Most Griswold skillets carry a pattern number near the handle. A No. 8 skillet uses the 704 series. A No. 10 uses 716, and a No. 12 uses 719. A letter after the number marks the mold it came from.

A missing or odd pattern number is a warning sign. It does not prove a fake, but it means you should look harder.

### Look at the Heat Ring

A heat ring is a raised ring around the outer edge of the bottom. It was made for old wood and coal stoves. Early Griswolds have one. Most small block pans do not.

Heat ring pans still cook well on gas. On a flat glass or induction top, the ring touches first. That leaves a small gap under the center. See our guide to [using non-induction cookware on an induction cooktop](/blog/how-to-use-non-induction-cookware-on-induction-cooktop/) if you plan to use vintage iron on induction.

### Spot the Fakes

Reproductions exist, mostly of small, valuable pieces. The Cast Iron Collector's guide to [reproductions and counterfeits](https://www.castironcollector.com/fakes.php) lists the common tells. These are the big ones:

- **Rough, grainy casting.** Real Griswolds are clean and smooth. Fakes often feel like sandpaper.
- **Blurry or wrong letters.** Soft edges, wrong fonts or uneven spacing under the name are red flags.
- **Odd numbers.** Pattern numbers that do not match the size, or extra mold numbers on the bottom.
- **Wrong weight.** A recast pan is often heavier or a slightly different size than a real one.

## How to Check a Used Griswold Before You Pay

A vintage pan is a used-market purchase. There is no warranty and usually no return. So check it in person if you can, or ask the seller for clear photos.

1. **Do the spin test.** Set the pan upside down on a flat counter and press one edge. If it spins or rocks, it is warped. Our guide on [how to fix a warped pan](/blog/how-to-fix-a-warped-pan/) explains why cast iron rarely comes back.
2. **Look for cracks.** Check the walls, the base and where the handle meets the pan. Tap it gently with a spoon. A clear ring is good. A dull thud can mean a crack.
3. **Check for pitting.** Deep pits on the cooking surface usually come from long rust. Light surface rust is fine. Deep pits rarely smooth out fully.
4. **Ask about the history.** A pan that was put in a fire to strip it can warp or turn red. Ask how it was cleaned.
5. **Match the logo to the size.** Use the table above. A logo that doesn't fit the size or era is worth questioning.

Cracks and deep pits are deal-breakers. Warping is almost always one too, especially on a glass top.

## What Should You Pay for a Griswold Skillet?

It depends on the logo, the size and the condition. Common pans are cheaper than many people expect. Rare ones are not.

A small block No. 8 is the most common Griswold, and it is usually the most affordable.Unrestored pans at yard sales or online often go for less.

Early slant logos, large block pans and odd sizes sell for more. Sizes like No. 13 and No. 20 are collector territory. One collector's [Griswold price list](https://griswoldcookware.com/griswold-vintage-cast-iron-skillet-price-list/) notes a single skillet sale of $17,000.

## How to Restore a Rusty Griswold

Many vintage Griswolds turn up rusty or crusted with old grease. Most clean up well.

1. **Strip it.** Old crud comes off with a lye-based oven cleaner and time, or with electrolysis. Avoid a fire. Heat that high can warp or crack the pan.
2. **Remove the rust.** Soak it in a 50/50 mix of white vinegar and water. Check every 30 minutes or so, because vinegar also eats bare iron if left too long.
3. **Wash and dry right away.** Rinse, scrub, and dry on a warm burner. Bare iron rusts in minutes.
4. **Season it.** Wipe on a very thin coat of oil, then wipe most of it off. Bake it upside down in a hot oven for about an hour. Repeat a few times.

Our guide on [how to season stainless steel pans](/blog/how-to-season-stainless-steel-pans/) covers how baked-on oil works on another metal. The idea is the same. A thin layer baked hard is better than a thick, sticky one.

Once it is seasoned, keep it that way. Wash with a little soap and hot water, dry it on the stove, and add a light wipe of oil. The same wash-and-dry-fast habit shows up in our guide to [how to clean stainless steel pans](/blog/how-to-clean-stainless-steel-pans/).

## Using Vintage or Modern Cast Iron on Different Stoves

Cast iron works on every common stove. How well it works depends on the bottom.

On a glass top, a flat, smooth bottom is key. Lift the pan instead of dragging it, since cast iron can scratch the glass. Warped vintage pans can also rock and heat unevenly on glass.

On an electric coil, heat comes up in a spiral. That can leave hot rings in the pan. Our guide to [coil stove cookware](/blog/coil-stove-cookware-what-works-what-warps/) explains why a thick base helps there.

Cast iron works on induction, since iron is magnetic. Heat it slowly, though. Blasting a cold pan on high can crack it. Our explainer on [carbon steel on induction](/blog/carbon-steel-on-induction-does-it-work-will-it-warp/) covers the same slow-heat rule for another iron pan.
