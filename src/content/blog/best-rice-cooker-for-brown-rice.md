Brown rice is not white rice with a tan. It still has its bran layer and germ, and that intact bran is a barrier: water has to work through it before the starch inside can soften. Press the white rice button on brown rice and you get the predictable result, grains that are chewy in the middle and a pot that smells faintly scorched at the bottom.

> **Quick answer:** the best rice cooker for brown rice for most people is the Zojirushi NS-ZCC10 Neuro Fuzzy, which soaks the grain before it cooks. If you want brown rice in half the time, buy the Cuckoo CRP-P0609S pressure cooker. If your counter is tiny and you cook for one or two, buy the 3-cup Zojirushi NS-LGC05.

That soak stage is the whole game. A dedicated brown rice program is not a marketing badge; it changes the sequence of what the machine does. Below are six cookers that have one, compared on capacity, heating type and how much counter they occupy, which in a small kitchen is the spec people regret ignoring. ## Why Brown Rice Needs Its Own Setting

Three things change when the bran stays on.

**Water absorption is slower.** The bran resists water, so brown rice needs both a longer wet phase and slightly more liquid than white rice. That is why cookers with marked inner pots have a separate brown rice line.

**The cooking window is longer.** A white rice cycle on a fuzzy logic machine typically runs around 40 minutes. The same machine's brown rice cycle often runs 80 to 110 minutes, most of it at lower temperatures.

**Texture punishes shortcuts.** Cook brown rice too fast and the outside of each grain turns pasty while the core stays hard. Gentle heat is what fixes it, which is why induction and pressure models handle brown rice better than cheap heating plates.

A brown rice program handles all three automatically. ## Fuzzy Logic, Induction or Pressure?

The heating method decides how good the brown rice is and how long you wait for it.

| Heating type | Brown rice time | Texture | Typical price | Trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Basic on-off plate | 45 to 60 min | Uneven, no soak | Lowest | No adjustment during cooking |
| Fuzzy logic heater | 80 to 110 min | Even and reliable | Mid to high | Slow |
| Induction heating | 80 to 110 min | Most even of all | Highest | Slow and expensive |
| Electric pressure | 30 to 40 min | Soft, slightly wetter | Mid to high | Gasket care, less precise |

Induction is the step up from fuzzy logic: instead of heating a plate under the pot, it heats the pot itself, which gives the most even result over a long brown rice cycle. ## What Size Should You Buy?

Rice cooker capacity is quoted in uncooked cups, and the cooker's own cup is 180ml, smaller than a US measuring cup. Ignore the "cooked cups" number on the box, which roughly doubles.

| Uncooked cups | Cooked yield | Suits | Footprint |

Two rules keep people out of trouble. First, most cookers do not work well below about one third of their rated capacity, so a big machine is a poor choice if you usually cook one cup. ## Getting Better Brown Rice From Any Cooker

These steps improve the result on a $40 machine and a $300 one alike.

1. **Measure with the cup that came with the cooker.** It holds 180ml, not 240ml. Using a US measuring cup throws the water ratio off by 25% before you start.
2. **Soak if your cooker does not.** Thirty to sixty minutes in cool water softens the bran and evens out the cook. Drain the soaking water and add fresh.
3. **Use the brown rice line in the pot.** If your pot has marked lines, fill to the brown line for the number of cups you added rather than measuring water separately.
4. **Add a pinch of salt, not oil.** Salt improves flavor throughout the grain. Oil mostly coats the pot.
5. **Let it rest ten minutes before opening.** The steam redistributes and the grains firm up. Opening early is the most common cause of wet rice.
6. **Fluff from the edges inward with the paddle.** Stirring from the middle compacts the grains and breaks them.

If grains are still firm after all that, add two extra tablespoons of water per cup next time and keep the change if it works. Different harvests absorb differently.

## Storing and Reheating Cooked Brown Rice

Cooked rice needs a little care. Cool it quickly and refrigerate within two hours, because rice left at room temperature can support bacterial growth that reheating does not fix. FoodSafety.gov's [cold food storage chart](https://www.foodsafety.gov/food-safety-charts/cold-food-storage-charts) puts cooked rice and grains at three to four days in the fridge.

Spread the rice in a shallow layer rather than leaving it in a deep container, so it cools through instead of staying warm in the middle. Airtight containers keep it from drying out and picking up fridge smells, and our roundup of [airtight food storage containers](/blog/best-airtight-food-storage-containers/) covers sets that stack in a small fridge.

To reheat, add a tablespoon of water per cup and cover. A nonstick pan works if you keep the heat moderate; our explainer on [what nonstick cookware is](/blog/what-is-nonstick-cookware/) covers the temperature limits, and our guide to [cleaning a ceramic pan](/blog/how-to-clean-ceramic-pan/) helps if rice sticks. Leftover brown rice is also the backbone of a fast fried rice, and it works in the rolls covered in our [sushi making kit guide](/blog/best-sushi-making-kit/), though short-grain white is traditional there.

Cooling rice in a compact fridge fills shelves fast, so shallow containers matter more than usual.
