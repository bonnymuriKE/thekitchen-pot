Simmer a tomato sauce for three hours in bare cast iron and it can taste faintly of coins. Do it in enameled cast iron and nothing happens.

That one difference explains most of the enameled cast iron vs cast iron choice. Both start as the same heavy iron. One gets a coat of glass, the other gets a coat of baked-on oil. Those two skins decide what you can cook, how you clean, how the pan fails and what you pay.

**Bottom line:** Buy enameled cast iron for a Dutch oven. It handles soups, braises, chili and tomato dishes for hours, needs no seasoning, and washes with soap. Buy bare cast iron for a skillet. It sears harder, gets slicker with use, takes any heat you throw at it, costs less and cannot chip. If you bake bread every week or cook over fire, a bare Dutch oven is the tougher tool. Most kitchens end up best served by one of each: an enameled pot and a bare pan.

| --- | --- | --- |

## What Enamel Changes, in Plain Words

Enamel is powdered glass melted onto the iron at very high heat. Once it cools, the glass is part of the pot. It seals the metal away from food, water and air. Our [Le Creuset cast iron skillet](/blog/le-creuset-cast-iron-skillet/) explainer covers how the glass is made. Here we stick to what it changes when you cook.

Three things change. First, food touches glass instead of iron, so acid has nothing to react with. Second, there is no porous metal for oil to soak into, so seasoning does nothing and is never needed. Third, glass and iron expand at different rates when heated. That is why enamel has heat rules that bare iron does not.

Bare cast iron is the opposite. The cooking surface is iron covered by thin layers of oil that were baked until they turned hard and dark. That coating is called seasoning. It protects the iron from rust and gives the pan its slick feel. It also wears, repairs and thickens as you cook. If you want the details of building it, our guide on [how to season a cast iron skillet](/blog/how-to-season-cast-iron-skillet/) walks through the oven method.

## Seasoning: The Upkeep You Sign Up For

With bare cast iron, care is a short habit after every wash. Rinse, scrub, dry the pan fully, warm it on the burner for a minute, then wipe on a few drops of oil. That takes about three minutes. Skip the drying step and you will see orange rust spots by morning.

A [Lodge 10.25-inch cast iron skillet](https://www.amazon.com/Lodge-L8SK3-Skillet-Pre-Seasoned-10-25-inch/dp/B00006JSUA/?tag=kitchenpot-20) arrives pre-seasoned, so you can cook in it the day it lands. The surface is a little pebbly at first. It gets smoother and slicker over the first few months of frying and searing.

With enameled cast iron, there is no oiling, no warming to dry and no rust watch on the cooking surface. You wash it like any pot and put it away. The one exception is the rim. Many enameled pots leave the top edge of the pot and lid as bare or matte black iron. Dry that edge well, and wipe a drop of oil on it now and then if you see rust freckles.

## Tomato Sauce, Wine and Long Braises

This is where enamel wins outright, and it is why most Dutch ovens are enameled.

Acids like tomato, wine, vinegar, citrus and buttermilk slowly eat into seasoning. On bare iron, a quick pan sauce or a splash of wine to lift the browned bits is no problem. A well-seasoned pan handles about 30 minutes of acidic cooking without trouble. Past that, the acid starts pulling iron into the food. You get a dull gray color, a metallic taste and a patch of stripped seasoning to rebuild.

Enamel has no such clock. A three-hour bolognese, a wine-braised short rib, a pot of chili with canned tomatoes or a vinegar-heavy adobo can sit in enameled cast iron all afternoon. You can also store leftovers in the pot in the fridge overnight, which you should never do with bare iron.

For stews, soups and sauces, an enameled pot like the [Le Creuset Signature 5.5-quart round Dutch oven](https://www.amazon.com/Le-Creuset-Signature-Enameled-Cast-Iron-Round-Dutch-Oven-5-5-Quart/dp/B0076NOGPY/?tag=kitchenpot-20) will do more of your cooking than any other single pot. The 5.5-quart size holds a whole chicken, a big batch of chili or a braise for five or six people. For one or two people, our guide to [cookware sizes for cooking for one](/blog/best-cookware-sizes-for-cooking-for-one/) explains why a 3 to 4-quart pot is often enough.

For tomato-heavy cooking in a skillet, our [stainless steel vs cast iron skillet](/blog/stainless-steel-vs-cast-iron-skillet/) comparison covers the other acid-proof option.

## Searing, Browning and Why Food Sticks

Both types hold heat the same way, because the iron underneath is the same. Drop a cold steak into either, and the pan barely cools. That stored heat is what builds a deep crust. The difference is in how hot you can safely push the surface and how food releases from it.

### Searing in Bare Cast Iron

Bare iron is the searing champion. You can preheat it empty over medium-high for 5 to 8 minutes until a drop of water skitters and vanishes. Add a thin layer of high smoke point oil and lay the meat in. Our guide on [how to cook steak in a cast iron skillet](/blog/how-to-cook-steak-in-cast-iron-skillet/) gives the exact timing for a thick cut.

Lancaster pans are cast thinner and machined smooth, which makes them heat faster and release food earlier in their life. The [Lancaster No. 10 cast iron skillet](https://www.awin1.com/cread.php?awinmid=95395&awinaffid=1956629&clickref=enameled-cast-iron-vs-cast-iron&ued=https%3A%2F%2Flancastercastiron.com%2Fproducts%2Flancaster-no-10-cast-iron-skillet) has a 10-inch cooking surface and weighs just over 6 pounds. That is enough room for two steaks or four chicken thighs without crowding.

### Searing in Enameled Cast Iron

Enamel sears well, but it needs a gentler start. Never heat it empty on high. Instead, put the pot on medium, add the oil right away, and let both heat together for 5 to 7 minutes. When the oil shimmers, the surface is hot enough to brown meat. Push the burner to medium-high only once there is food or fat in the pot. Brown stew meat in two or three rounds, because tall sides trap steam and a crowded pot boils the meat gray.

### Is Enameled Cast Iron Nonstick?

No. Enamel is smooth, but smooth is not the same as nonstick. Eggs, fish skin and potatoes will grab onto a light enamel surface unless you preheat properly and use enough fat. A well-seasoned bare skillet releases a fried egg far more easily. If eggs are your daily breakfast, a bare skillet or a dedicated pan from our [best nonstick pans](/blog/best-nonstick-pans-with-buying-guide/) guide is the better tool.

## Heat Limits: Empty Pans, Oven Temps and Open Flame

Bare cast iron has no practical upper limit in a home kitchen. Broiler, 550°F oven, charcoal grill, campfire coals: it handles all of them. The only risk is a sudden shock, like pouring cold water into a red-hot pan, which can crack any cast iron.

Enameled cast iron has three rules.

1. **No empty heating on the stove.** An empty pot on a high burner can get hot enough to crack the glass or leave a fine web of cracks. Always add fat or liquid before going past medium.
2. **Check the knob.** Most enameled Dutch ovens are oven-safe to about 500°F, and the knob is usually the weak point. Black plastic knobs on older or cheaper pots are often rated lower. A stainless steel knob removes that worry.
3. **No grill grates or campfires.** Open flame heats one spot far faster than the rest. Glass hates that uneven heat, and soot stains the outside for good.

Thermal shock is the other enamel killer. Let a hot pot cool on the stove for 10 to 15 minutes before it meets water. A hot pot set in a cold, wet sink can chip or craze in seconds. Use a pair of [heat-resistant cooking gloves](/blog/10-best-heat-resistant-gloves-for-cooking/) when you move a full pot out of a 450°F oven; a loaded Dutch oven weighs 15 pounds or more.

## Chipping and Crazing: How Enamel Fails

Bare cast iron fails slowly and can always be brought back. Rust scrubs off, flaking seasoning gets stripped and redone, and even a pan from a yard sale can be restored. Our [Griswold cast iron skillet review](/blog/griswold-cast-iron-skillet-review/) shows how old bare pans come back to life after decades.

Enamel fails suddenly and cannot be repaired at home. There are two kinds of damage.

**Chipping** is a missing flake of glass, usually from a knock or a drop. Chips on the rim or outside come from knocks, a spoon tapped on the edge or a lid set down hard. They are cosmetic. Dry the exposed iron and rub a drop of oil on it.

**Crazing** is a fine web of hairline cracks in the interior glass. It comes from overheating an empty pot or from thermal shock. You often notice it first as brown lines where food stains seep into the cracks. Light crazing may stay stable for years, but it can lead to flaking.

**A chip on the cooking surface is the line.** Once glass breaks away inside the pot, the edges around it can keep flaking. Stop cooking in it. Established brands back their interior enamel with long warranties, so contact the maker before you give up on it.

To prevent chips in a small kitchen, never stack an enameled pot inside another pot without a cloth or pan protector between them. Our guide to [storing pots and pans in a small kitchen](/blog/store-pots-and-pans-in-a-small-kitchen/) has space-saving ways to keep heavy pots apart, and [organizing pot lids](/blog/how-to-organize-pot-lids-in-a-small-kitchen/) stops a heavy lid from rattling against the rim.

## Dishwasher, Metal Utensils and Daily Cleaning

### Can Enameled Cast Iron Go in the Dishwasher?

Some brands say yes. We still recommend hand washing. Dishwasher detergent is strongly alkaline and slowly dulls the glass, so a glossy interior turns chalky over the years. The exposed iron rim can also rust in a long, wet cycle. Warm water, dish soap and a soft sponge are all it needs.

For stuck-on bits in enamel, fill the pot with warm water and a spoonful of baking soda, simmer for 10 minutes and let it sit. Then wipe. For gray marks and tan stains, a paste of [Bar Keepers Friend Cookware Cleanser](https://www.amazon.com/Bar-Keepers-Friend-Cookware-Cleanser/dp/B000V6YLNA/?tag=kitchenpot-20) and a damp cloth lifts them without scratching the glass. Never use steel wool on enamel.

Bare cast iron never goes in the dishwasher. One cycle strips the seasoning and leaves the pan rusty. Wash it by hand with a little soap, which is fine on a seasoned pan. For stuck food, a pair of [Lodge polycarbonate pan scrapers](https://www.amazon.com/Lodge-Scrapers-Handheld-Polycarbonate-Cleaners/dp/B0039UU9UO/?tag=kitchenpot-20) clears the bottom and corners without hurting the seasoning.

### Can You Use Metal Utensils on Each?

On bare cast iron, yes. A flat metal spatula actually helps by scraping off loose bits and smoothing the surface over time.

On enamel, avoid them. Metal spoons and whisks leave gray marks on light enamel and can chip the rim if you tap them on the edge. Use wood, silicone or nylon. The gray marks are metal left on the glass, not scratches, and the cleanser above removes them.

## Is Enameled Cast Iron Safe and Healthier?

Both are safe, coating-free choices with no plastic-based nonstick layer to wear off. If you want the difference between enamel and polymer coatings, our explainer on [what nonstick cookware is](/blog/what-is-nonstick-cookware/) lays it out.

Enamel is glass, so it does not react with food or add anything to it. That makes it a good fit for people who cook a lot of acidic dishes or who need to limit iron. Buy from an established brand, since the safety of the glaze depends on the maker.

Bare cast iron adds a little iron to food. That is harmless for most people, though anyone with an iron overload condition should limit it.

## Weight, Price and How Long Each Lasts

### Weight

The weight gap is smaller than most people expect. Enamel adds only a little. A 5 to 6-quart Dutch oven weighs about 11 to 14 pounds whether it is enameled or bare. A 10 to 12-inch skillet weighs about 5 to 8 pounds either way.

What changes the weight is how the iron is cast. Thin, machined bare pans are the lightest cast iron you can buy. ### Lifespan

Bare cast iron lasts for generations. Nothing on it can wear out for good, because seasoning can always be stripped and rebuilt.

Enameled cast iron lasts decades with gentle handling, but one hard drop or a hot pot in cold water can end it. Think of a good enameled pot as a 20 to 30-year pot, and a bare skillet as a forever pan.

## Skillet vs Dutch Oven: Where Each Type Wins

### For Skillets, Go Bare

A skillet's main jobs are searing, frying, shallow frying, cornbread and eggs. All of those want high heat and a slick surface. Bare iron gives you both, and its seasoning improves fastest in a skillet because you cook with fat in it so often.

An enameled skillet is a fine pan for someone who refuses to season anything. Our look at the [Le Creuset cast iron skillet](/blog/le-creuset-cast-iron-skillet/) covers that case. But it costs several times more than a [Lodge skillet](/blog/lodge-cast-iron-skillet-review/) and does the main skillet jobs a little worse. For pan sauces with wine or tomato, a stainless pan is a better partner for bare iron than an enameled skillet.

### For Dutch Ovens, Go Enameled

A Dutch oven spends its life full of liquid for hours: stock, braises, beans, chili, soup and sauce. Long, wet, often acidic cooking is the exact thing that strips seasoning from bare iron. Enamel shrugs it off.

A budget enameled pot does this job well. The [Lodge Essential Enamel 6-quart Dutch oven](https://www.amazon.com/Lodge-Essential-Enamel-Cast-Iron-Dutch-Oven-6-Quart/dp/B000N4WN08/?tag=kitchenpot-20) is a roomy, low-cost way into enamel with a light interior that shows browning clearly. One step up, the [Goldilocks 6-quart Dutch oven](https://www.awin1.com/cread.php?awinmid=90353&awinaffid=1956629&clickref=enameled-cast-iron-vs-cast-iron&ued=https%3A%2F%2Fcookgoldilocks.com%2Fproducts%2F6-quart-dutch-oven) is a mid-priced enameled pot with a cream interior and an oven-safe stainless steel knob. That knob means no worry about a plastic knob in a hot oven.

Our [best Dutch oven](/blog/best-dutch-oven/) roundup compares more models by size and budget, and the [Milo Dutch oven review](/blog/milo-dutch-oven-review/) looks at another direct-to-buyer option.

### When a Bare Dutch Oven Makes Sense

Pick bare for a Dutch oven if you bake bread most weeks, deep-fry, cook outdoors or camp. The [Lodge 5-quart cast iron Dutch oven](https://www.amazon.com/Lodge-5-Quart-Cast-Iron-Dutch-Oven-Iron-Lid/dp/B00004S9HE/?tag=kitchenpot-20) is cheap, nearly indestructible and goes straight onto coals. For a smoother, heirloom-quality pot, the [Lancaster 5-quart Dutch oven](https://www.awin1.com/cread.php?awinmid=95395&awinaffid=1956629&clickref=enameled-cast-iron-vs-cast-iron&ued=https%3A%2F%2Flancastercastiron.com%2Fproducts%2F5-quart-dutch-oven) has a polished cooking surface and a self-basting lid. Keep long tomato braises in an enameled pot or a [stockpot with a lid](/blog/best-stockpot-with-a-lid/) instead.

## Black Matte Enamel vs Light Sand Interior

Not all enamel interiors cook the same. There are two main styles.

**Light interiors** (cream, sand or white) are smooth and glossy. Their big strength is visibility. You can see exactly how dark the browned bits on the bottom are getting, which helps you avoid burning a sauce base. The downside is that they show every stain, and they can darken or craze if you abuse them with empty heat. Le Creuset, Lodge and Goldilocks all use light interiors.

**Black matte interiors** are slightly rough, almost like fine sandpaper under your finger. That texture gives oil more to grip, so it builds a light patina and browns meat a little more deeply. It also hides stains and handles high heat and empty oven preheats with less visible wear. The trade is that you cannot see the browned bits as easily. The [Staub 5.5-quart round cocotte in black matte](https://www.amazon.com/Staub-Cast-Iron-Round-Cocotte-5-5-Quart-Black-Matte/dp/B000AXQADI/?tag=kitchenpot-20) is the classic example. Its lid also has small spikes underneath that drip condensed steam back onto the food during a braise.

| | Light sand or cream interior | Black matte interior |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Seeing the fond | Easy | Harder |
| Browning meat | Very good | Slightly better |
| Stains | Show clearly | Hidden |
| Release over time | Small improvement | Builds a patina, improves more |
| Best for | Sauces, soups, new cooks | Searing, braising, bread |

## Enameled vs Cast Iron Dutch Oven for Sourdough Bread

Bread is the one Dutch oven job where bare iron often makes more sense. Most sourdough recipes call for preheating the empty pot at 450 to 500°F for 30 to 60 minutes, then baking inside it. That is easy for bare iron and hard on enamel.

Enameled pots can do it, especially those with metal knobs and black interiors. But weekly empty preheats slowly darken and dull a light interior.

A bare Dutch oven costs a fraction of a premium enameled one and does not care how hot it gets. If you bake bread most weeks, keep a bare pot for bread and an enameled one for stews. A good [bread proofing basket](/blog/best-bread-proofing-basket-reviews/) is the other tool that makes the biggest difference to the loaf's shape.

## Induction, Glass Top and Gas Stoves

Both types work on every stove type, including induction, because the iron underneath is magnetic. Our [best cookware for induction cooktop](/blog/best-cookware-for-induction-cooktop/) guide covers what else works there.

On glass and ceramic tops, enameled pots have a small edge. Their bottoms are usually smooth enamel, so they are less likely to scratch the glass. Bare cast iron bottoms can be rough, especially on budget pans. Lift either one rather than sliding it. Our guide on [how to protect a glass top stove from cast iron](/blog/how-to-protect-glass-top-stove-from-cast-iron/) covers the habits that keep the glass safe.

Match the pot base to the burner size, since a big pot on a small burner heats unevenly. On a [coil stove](/blog/coil-stove-cookware-what-works-what-warps/), give cast iron a few extra minutes to even out. On gas, keep the flame under the base so it does not scorch the enamel sides.

## The Verdict: Which One Should You Buy?

**Buy a bare cast iron skillet if** you sear, fry, make eggs and cornbread, or want a pan that lasts forever and costs little. Start with a classic Lodge, or choose a lighter, smoother Lancaster if weight or a glass top worries you.

**Buy an enameled Dutch oven if** you make stews, soups, chili, braises or sauces. A budget Lodge or a mid-priced Goldilocks does the job. A Le Creuset or Staub brings better enamel and a longer warranty.

**Buy a bare Dutch oven if** you bake bread most weeks, deep-fry or cook outdoors.

**Buy an enameled skillet only if** you never want to season or oil anything and mostly cook at medium heat.

For a small kitchen that has space for just two heavy pieces, the winning pair is a 10-inch bare skillet and a 5 to 6-quart enameled Dutch oven. Together they cover searing, frying, baking, braising and soup.
