A medium lemon gives about 3 tablespoons of juice. That is the number most recipes assume when they say "juice of one lemon."

Lemons vary a lot, though. Size, ripeness and how you squeeze all change the yield.

**The short version:** One medium lemon yields about 3 tablespoons (1.5 ounces) of juice and 1 tablespoon of zest. You need about 6 medium lemons for 1 cup of juice. To get the most, use room-temperature lemons, roll them first and squeeze with a press like the [Chef'n FreshForce Citrus Juicer](https://www.amazon.com/Chefn-FreshForce-Citrus-Juicer-Lemon/dp/B002XOB0P0/?tag=kitchenpot-20).

## Lemon Juice Conversion Chart

| Lemons | Juice (Tablespoons) | Juice (Cups) | Fluid Ounces |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 small | 1 to 2 tbsp | About 1/8 cup | 0.5 to 1 oz |
| 1 medium | 3 tbsp | About 1/5 cup | 1.5 oz |
| 1 large | 4 tbsp | 1/4 cup | 2 oz |
| 3 medium | 9 tbsp | About 1/2 cup | 4.5 oz |
| 6 medium | 18 tbsp | About 1 cup | 9 oz |
| 4 large | 16 tbsp | 1 cup | 8 oz |

Yields for medium and large lemons, and the 1/2 cup and 1 cup counts, follow [A Couple Cooks' lemon yield guide](https://www.acouplecooks.com/how-much-juice-in-one-lemon/). Small-lemon figures are an estimate; buy extra when a recipe needs an exact amount.

## Quick Reference

- **Juice of 1 lemon:** About 3 tablespoons.
- **Juice of 1/2 lemon:** About 1 1/2 tablespoons.
- **Zest of 1 lemon:** About 1 tablespoon.
- **1/2 cup of juice:** About 3 medium or 2 large lemons.
- **1 cup of juice:** About 6 medium or 4 large lemons.
- **1 tablespoon of juice:** About 1/3 of a medium lemon.

## Why Lemons Give Different Amounts

**Size.** Bigger lemons hold more juice, but a heavy small lemon can out-juice a light large one.

**Skin thickness.** Thick, bumpy skins usually mean more pith and less juice. Thin, smooth skins are juicier.

**Ripeness.** Fully yellow, slightly soft lemons give more juice than hard, green-tinged ones.

**Temperature.** Cold lemons release less juice. Let them come to room temperature first.

**Variety.** Meyer lemons are often juicier and sweeter than standard Eureka or Lisbon lemons.

## How to Pick Juicy Lemons

1. **Feel the weight.** A lemon that feels heavy for its size has more juice.
2. **Check the skin.** Choose thin, smooth, glossy skin over thick and bumpy.
3. **Give a gentle squeeze.** It should yield slightly. Rock-hard lemons are dry.
4. **Look at the color.** Bright yellow with no green patches.
5. **Avoid soft spots and mold.** These lemons are past their best.

## How to Get the Most Juice From a Lemon

1. **Bring it to room temperature.** Leave it out for an hour, or soak it in warm water for a few minutes.
2. **Roll it.** Press firmly and roll the lemon on the counter with your palm. This breaks the juice sacs inside.
3. **Warm it briefly.** A cold lemon can go in the microwave for 10 to 20 seconds. Let it cool slightly before cutting.
4. **Cut it lengthwise.** Cutting from tip to tip exposes more of the flesh than cutting across.
5. **Use a tool.** A lever press, reamer or electric juicer gets far more juice than bare hands.
6. **Squeeze over a strainer.** It catches seeds while you work.

## Lime, Meyer Lemon and Orange Swaps

| Fruit | Typical Juice | Swap Tip |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lime | Less than a lemon | Use about 1 1/2 limes per lemon |
| Meyer lemon | Similar or more | Sweeter and less sharp |
| Orange | Much more | Sweeter; not a direct swap in savory dishes |

Lime juice works in most recipes that call for lemon juice, though the flavor is more floral. Meyer lemons are less sour, so you may want to add a splash of regular lemon or vinegar for brightness.

## Fresh vs Bottled Lemon Juice

**Fresh juice** tastes brighter and more aromatic. Use it in dressings, drinks, sauces and anything served raw.

**Bottled juice** is convenient and consistent. It works well in baking, marinades and long-cooked dishes where the fresh aroma fades anyway.

Swap them one for one by volume.

## How to Store Lemons and Lemon Juice

**Whole lemons:** Keep at room temperature for about a week. In the fridge, in a sealed bag, they last much longer.

**Cut lemons:** Wrap the cut side and refrigerate. Use within a few days.

**Fresh juice:** Refrigerate in a sealed jar for a few days. Fresh flavor fades quickly.

**Frozen juice:** Freeze juice in ice cube trays, then move the cubes to a freezer bag. One standard cube is roughly 1 to 2 tablespoons. Label the bag.

**Zest:** Freeze grated zest flat in a small bag and break off what you need.

[Airtight food storage containers](/blog/best-airtight-food-storage-containers/) keep juice and zest from absorbing fridge smells.

## Simple Homemade Lemonade

A classic ratio is 1 part lemon juice, 1 part sugar and about 4 parts water.

1. **Make a syrup.** Heat 1 cup sugar with 1 cup water until dissolved. Let it cool.
2. **Juice the lemons.** You need about 1 cup of juice, roughly 6 medium lemons.
3. **Mix.** Combine the syrup and juice in a pitcher.
4. **Dilute.** Add about 3 cups of cold water, then taste and adjust.
5. **Chill.** Serve over ice with lemon slices.

Making syrup first means the sugar fully dissolves, so the lemonade is never gritty. For blended frozen lemonade, see the [best blenders for smoothies](/blog/best-blenders-for-smoothies/).

## Quick Lemon Vinaigrette

Whisk together 3 tablespoons lemon juice (one medium lemon), 1/2 teaspoon Dijon mustard, a pinch of salt and pepper, and 1/3 cup olive oil. Add a little honey if the lemons are very sour.

The juice of one lemon is just right for a salad for four. Store leftovers in a jar in the fridge for up to a week and shake before using.

## Lemon Juice in Baking

Baking is less forgiving than cooking. Measure juice exactly with spoons or a measuring cup rather than counting lemons.

Lemon juice reacts with baking soda to help cakes and muffins rise. If you swap fresh juice for bottled, use the same amount; the acidity is similar enough for baking.

Add zest for stronger lemon flavor without extra liquid. Zest carries most of the fragrant oils, while juice brings sourness.

## Lemon Juice for Canning and Preserving

Home canning guidance generally calls for bottled lemon juice rather than fresh when adding acid to tomatoes and some other foods. Bottled juice has a consistent acidity, while fresh lemons vary from fruit to fruit.

Follow a tested canning recipe from a trusted source, and do not reduce the lemon juice it calls for. The acid is part of what makes the jar safe.

## Lemon Juice Nutrition Basics

Lemon juice is low in calories and a good source of vitamin C. It adds bright flavor, so it can help you use less salt in dressings and sauces.

Its acid can wear tooth enamel over time. If you drink lemon water often, use a straw and rinse your mouth with plain water afterward.

## Freezing a Lemon Haul

When lemons are cheap, buy in bulk and freeze.

1. **Juice them all at once** with an electric juicer.
2. **Pour into ice cube trays.** Note how much one cube holds by filling one with a measuring spoon.
3. **Freeze, then transfer** the cubes to a labeled freezer bag.
4. **Zest first** and freeze the zest separately in a flat bag.
5. **Use within a few months** for the best flavor.

Frozen cubes melt quickly in sauces, marinades and drinks.

## Troubleshooting Lemon Juice

**The juice tastes bitter.** You probably squeezed too hard and pressed oils and pith compounds from the peel, or seeds were crushed. Strain the juice and squeeze less forcefully.

**The lemon gives almost no juice.** It may be old, cold or dry. Warm it, roll it and try a lever press. If it is still dry, use it for zest instead.

**The juice is cloudy and pulpy.** Pour it through a fine strainer. Electric reamers with pulp control let you choose how much pulp gets through.

**The juice turned brown in the fridge.** Fresh juice oxidizes over a few days. Freeze it in cubes if you will not use it within two or three days.

**The recipe tastes too sour.** Lemons vary. Balance with a pinch of sugar or honey, or a little more fat in a dressing.

## Lemon Juice vs Lemon Zest vs Lemon Extract

**Juice** brings sourness and some fruity flavor. It adds liquid, which matters in baking.

**Zest** carries the fragrant oils from the peel. It gives strong lemon flavor with little sourness and no extra liquid.

**Extract** is concentrated lemon oil in alcohol. A little goes a long way and it adds no acid.

For the brightest lemon flavor in baked goods, use both zest and juice.

## Kid-Friendly Lemon Jobs

Juicing lemons is a great first kitchen job for children. A handheld press is safe, simple and satisfying to use.

Let kids roll the lemons on the counter, squeeze them with a press and count how many tablespoons each lemon gives. It turns a recipe into a small math lesson. Keep knives with adults and supervise any cutting.

## Peeling and Slicing Lemons for Garnish

For neat lemon wheels, use a sharp knife and slice across the fruit. For very thin, even slices, a [mandoline slicer](/blog/best-mandoline-slicers/) with a hand guard is fastest.

For twists in drinks, peel a strip with a vegetable peeler, trim the edges straight and twist it over the glass to release the oils.

Store garnish slices in a sealed container in the fridge for a day or two. They dry out quickly when left uncovered.

## Planning Lemons for a Week of Cooking

If you cook with lemon often, buy a bag of six to eight and keep them in the fridge crisper. They last much longer chilled than on the counter.

Plan meals that share the lemons: roast chicken with lemon on Sunday, a lemony grain salad midweek, and fish with lemon butter at the end of the week. Use the zest early and the juice later, since zest dries out once the lemon is cut. ## Cleaning With Lemon Juice

Lemon's natural acid cuts grease and mineral deposits.

- **Kettles:** Boil water with lemon juice to loosen scale. See [how to clean an electric kettle](/blog/how-to-clean-an-electric-kettle/).
- **Dishwashers:** Lemon helps freshen the interior; see [how to clean a dishwasher](/blog/how-to-clean-a-dishwasher/).
- **Cutting boards:** Rub with salt and half a lemon to lift stains and smells.
- **Microwaves:** Heat a bowl of water with lemon slices for a few minutes, then wipe.

Do not use lemon on natural stone counters like marble; the acid can etch them.

## Lemon Juice in Recipes: Tips

- **Add lemon juice at the end** of cooking for the brightest flavor.
- **Zest before you juice.** It is much harder to zest a squeezed lemon.
- **Taste as you go.** Lemons vary in sourness.
- **Strain seeds.** Bitter seeds can spoil a sauce or drink.
- **Use a small bowl.** Squeeze into a bowl first, not straight into the dish, so you can catch seeds and measure.

## How to Zest a Lemon

1. Wash and dry the lemon.
2. Use a fine grater or microplane.
3. Grate only the yellow layer; the white pith is bitter.
4. Rotate the lemon as you go.
5. One medium lemon gives about a tablespoon.

For thin strips of peel for drinks, use a vegetable peeler, then slice the strips thinly with a sharp knife. Keep knives sharp with the [best knife sharpener](/blog/best-knife-sharpener/).
