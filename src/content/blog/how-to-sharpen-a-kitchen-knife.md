Hold a sheet of printer paper by one corner and slice down with your chef's knife. If it snags or tears, the edge is dull. Below is how to sharpen a kitchen knife at home.

No paper handy? Rest the blade on a ripe tomato and pull it back gently. A sharp knife bites through the skin on its own weight. A dull one skates across the top, which is exactly how knives slip and cut fingers.

**Quick answer:** Hone your knife on a rod every few uses to keep the edge straight. When honing stops working, sharpen it. Grind on a 1000 grit whetstone until you feel a small rough lip (the burr) along the whole edge, then polish on 6000 grit. Hold about 15 degrees for Japanese and modern knives and 20 degrees for older German ones. Most home cooks need a real sharpening every two to four months.

## Is Your Knife Dull? Three Checks That Take Ten Seconds

Before you reach for a stone, find out what kind of dull you have. The fix depends on it.

1. **The paper test.** Slice down through a hanging sheet of paper. A sharp edge makes a clean, quiet cut. A rough tear at one spot means a damaged patch. Tearing along the whole length means the knife needs a full sharpening.
2. **The tomato test.** Lay the blade on the skin and draw it back lightly. If it catches straight away, you are fine. If it slides, the edge is either bent or worn.
3. **The light test.** Hold the knife edge up under a ceiling light and look straight down at it. A sharp edge is so thin it reflects almost nothing. Shiny dots or a bright line show flat, worn spots or small dents.

If the knife fails the tomato test but shows no bright line, the edge is just bent over, and a minute on a honing rod brings it back. A bright line means honing will not help; it is time to sharpen.

Everything here applies to steel knives. Ceramic blades need diamond, which we cover in [how to sharpen ceramic knives](/blog/how-to-sharpen-ceramic-knives/).

## Honing vs Sharpening: Which One Does Your Knife Need?

These two jobs get mixed up all the time.

**Honing** straightens the edge. Every cut on a board bends the thin edge a little to one side. After a week, it rolls over like the top of a wave, still there but pointing the wrong way. A honing rod pushes it back upright. You remove almost no steel.

**Sharpening** grinds new steel. When the edge has worn round, there is nothing left to straighten. You have to grind both sides of the blade until they meet in a fresh, thin edge again. That takes a stone, a guided sharpener or a machine.

| | Honing | Sharpening |
|---|---|---|
| What it does | Straightens a bent edge | Grinds a new edge |
| Steel removed | Almost none | A small amount |
| Tool | Steel or ceramic rod | Whetstone, guided or electric sharpener |
| How often | Every 2 to 5 uses | Every 2 to 4 months for daily cooks |
| Time | 30 seconds | 10 to 20 minutes on a stone |
| Fixes chips or a rounded edge? | No | Yes |

Hone often and sharpen rarely. A knife honed twice a week can go months between sharpenings, and every sharpening you skip adds to its life.

## What Angle Should You Sharpen a Kitchen Knife At?

The angle is how far you tilt the blade off the stone. It is measured per side. A knife sharpened at 15 degrees per side has a 30-degree edge in total.

A lower angle makes a thinner edge. It slides through food more easily but wears and dents faster. A higher angle makes a sturdier edge that lasts longer but takes more push.

| Knife type | Angle per side | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Japanese gyuto, santoku, nakiri | 12 to 15 degrees | Hard steel holds a thin edge well |
| Modern Western chef's knife (most made since 2010) | 14 to 15 degrees | Many brands moved to thinner edges |
| Older German chef's knife | 20 degrees | Softer steel, built for a tougher edge |
| Cleaver, heavy boning knife | 20 to 25 degrees | Hits bone and joints |
| Pocket or outdoor knife used in the kitchen | 20 degrees | Toughness over glide |
| Not sure | 17 to 18 degrees | A safe middle for any steel knife |

To find the angle without a protractor, hold the blade straight up at 90 degrees, halve it to 45, then halve again to about 22. Lower the spine a little more and you are near 15.

Japanese knives are where the low angle really pays off. A hard VG-10 blade like the [Hoshanho KAGE 8-inch Damascus chef knife](https://www.awin1.com/cread.php?awinmid=124972&awinaffid=1956629&clickref=how-to-sharpen-a-kitchen-knife&ued=https%3A%2F%2Fhoshanho.com%2Fproducts%2Fkage-8-damascus-chef-knife) keeps a 15-degree edge for a long time because the steel resists rolling. Sharpen it at 20 degrees and you throw away the reason you bought it. If you are curious how steel and build change the way a blade wears, our piece on [forged vs stamped knives](/blog/forged-vs-stamped-knives/) explains it.

The one rule that matters more than the number: **hold the same angle on every stroke.** A steady 18 degrees beats a wobbly 15.

## How to Sharpen a Kitchen Knife With a Whetstone

A whetstone (also called a water stone) is a flat block of abrasive that you slide the knife across. It is the cheapest way to get a truly sharp edge, it works on almost every steel knife, and it takes up less room than a box of foil.

For a first stone, get a two-sided one with a 1000 grit side and a 6000 grit side. The [King KDS 1000/6000 Combination Whetstone](https://www.amazon.com/King-KDS-Combination-Whetstone-1000-6000/dp/B01BB1ZDVW/?tag=kitchenpot-20) is the classic Japanese starter stone and has been on kitchen counters for decades. For a full kit, the [Sharp Pebble 1000/6000 Whetstone Knife Sharpener](https://www.amazon.com/Sharp-Pebble-Whetstone-Knife-Sharpener/dp/B01FZZUL30/?tag=kitchenpot-20) adds a bamboo base and a clip-on angle guide. That guide helps a lot in your first few sessions.

### Step 1: Soak the Stone

Drop the stone in a bowl or a sink of water for 10 to 15 minutes, until the bubbles stop. A dry stone grabs the steel and clogs fast. Splash-and-go stones only need a quick wetting; the label on the stone will tell you which kind you have. Keep a cup of water next to you and splash the surface whenever it starts to look dry.

### Step 2: Set Up a Steady, Wet-Proof Spot

Put the stone on its base, coarse side (1000) up, with the long side facing you. Lay a damp kitchen towel under the base so it cannot slide. In a small kitchen, the sink is the best place to sharpen. A [Naniwa Sink Bridge Whetstone Holder](https://www.amazon.com/Naniwa-Sink-Bridge-Whetstone-Holder/dp/B01D4QYKWU/?tag=kitchenpot-20) stretches across the basin and clamps the stone, so the gray slurry runs down the drain instead of over your counter. If your sink area is already crowded, our tips on [organizing under the kitchen sink](/blog/organize-under-the-kitchen-sink/) help make room to store the stone and bridge there.

### Step 3: Find Your Angle

Hold the handle in your strong hand with your index finger along the spine or your thumb on the flat near the heel. Put two or three fingertips of your other hand flat on the blade, near the edge. Tilt the spine up to 15 or 20 degrees. Your fingers hold the edge on the stone; your wrist holds the angle.

### Step 4: Grind Until You Raise a Burr

Push the blade across the stone, edge leading, as if you were slicing a thin layer off the top. Sweep from heel to tip on each stroke so the whole edge touches. Use light, firm pressure, about what you would use to rub a stain out of a shirt.

After 10 to 15 strokes, carefully run your thumb from the spine *across* the edge on the other side (never along it). You are feeling for a tiny rough lip of bent steel. That lip is the **burr**, and it is the most important sign in sharpening. It means you ground the first side all the way to the edge.

Keep going until you can feel the burr along the full length, from heel to tip. On a very dull knife this can take 50 strokes or more. Do not move on until it is there. No burr means no new edge.

### Step 5: Flip and Repeat on the Second Side

Turn the knife over and do the same on the other side at the same angle. Grind until the burr flips back to the first side along the whole length. Most people need fewer strokes here.

### Step 6: Polish on the Fine Side

Turn the stone to the 6000 side and wet it. Now make lighter strokes, alternating sides: five per side, then three, then two, then one. This smooths the scratch marks from the coarse side and shrinks the burr. The edge will start to look brighter and cut more cleanly.

### Step 7: Remove the Burr

A tiny burr can still cling to the edge. Draw the edge lightly through a cork, then give it two gentle passes per side on a ceramic rod. Wash and dry the knife, and run the paper test again.

### Step 8: Clean Up

Rinse the stone and let it air dry for a day before you put it away. A wet stone in a closed drawer can grow mold and crack.

## Which Whetstone Grit Do You Need?

Grit is how coarse the stone is. Lower numbers cut faster and leave a rougher edge. Higher numbers cut slowly and polish.

| Grit | What it is for | Who needs it |
|---|---|---|
| 200 to 400 | Fixing chips, broken tips, very dull knives | Only if you rescue damaged knives |
| 800 to 1000 | Normal sharpening, raising the burr | Everyone; this is the working side |
| 3000 to 4000 | A smooth, toothy edge for meat and bread crust | Optional middle step |
| 6000 to 8000 | Polishing for clean cuts in herbs, fish, tomatoes | Most home cooks; the finishing side |
| 10000 and up | Mirror finish | Not needed in a home kitchen |

A 1000/6000 stone covers about 90 percent of home sharpening. Only buy a coarse stone if your knife has chips or a broken tip. Treat it like a repair tool, not a weekly one.

## How to Sharpen a Kitchen Knife With a Rod

This is one of the most searched questions, and the honest answer is that a rod mostly hones. It keeps a sharp knife sharp. Here is how to do it right.

1. Hold the rod straight down with its tip resting on a folded towel on the counter.
2. Place the heel of the knife against the top of the rod at your sharpening angle.
3. Draw the knife down and toward you, sweeping from heel to tip, with very light pressure. Think of the weight of the knife plus a little more.
4. Switch sides on every stroke. Five to eight strokes per side is plenty.
5. Wipe the blade and do the tomato test.

Pointing the rod down into a towel is far safer than the in-the-air flourish you see on cooking shows. The blade always moves away from your hand.

Steel rods with fine ridges are fine for soft German knives. For anything else, choose ceramic. The [Idahone Fine Ceramic Sharpening Rod](https://www.amazon.com/Idahone-Ceramic-Sharpening-Black-Handle/dp/B01C9L1PC4/?tag=kitchenpot-20) is a 10-inch fine ceramic rod made in the USA. It is gentle enough for hard Japanese steel and removes just a whisper of metal, so it straightens and lightly refreshes the edge in one go. Give the rod a scrub with a damp scouring pad when it turns gray with steel.

## Pull-Through and Electric Sharpeners: When They Make Sense

A whetstone is a skill. Not everyone wants to learn one, and that is fine.

### Pull-Through Sharpeners

These have slots with abrasive set in a V that grinds both sides at once. They are quick and fit in a drawer. But the angle is fixed, they remove more steel than needed, and cheap carbide slots leave a ragged edge.

If you go this route, choose one with a ceramic stage rather than carbide alone. The [Work Sharp Kitchen Edge Knife Sharpener](https://www.amazon.com/Work-Sharp-WSKTNKES-ATL-Kitchen-Edge/dp/B09SQFV9FT/?tag=kitchenpot-20) is compact, has a ceramic honing wheel and also works on serrated bread knives. Keep pull-throughs for everyday, inexpensive knives such as the ones in our [best knife set under 100](/blog/best-knife-set-under-100/) roundup. We would not run a thin Japanese blade through one.

### Electric Sharpeners

Most electric sharpeners spin small grinding wheels inside fixed slots. They are fast but aggressive, and a few careless passes can grind away years of blade life.

A slower, gentler kind exists. The [Tormek T-1 Kitchen Knife Sharpener](https://www.awin1.com/cread.php?awinmid=108632&awinaffid=1956629&clickref=how-to-sharpen-a-kitchen-knife&ued=https%3A%2F%2Fshop.tormek.com%2Fen%2Fproducts%2Ftormek-t-1-kitchen-knife-sharpener) clamps the knife in an angle guide and grinds it on a fine diamond wheel, then polishes it on a separate honing wheel. The guide holds the exact same angle every time, which is the hardest part of using a stone. It suits people with several good knives who want whetstone-quality edges without learning the hand skill. Our full [Tormek T-1 review](/blog/tormek-t1-review/) covers how it works and how it compares with the larger models.

## How to Sharpen a Kitchen Knife Without a Sharpener

Guests are coming, the knife is dull and there is no stone in the house. You have options.

**The mug trick.** Turn a ceramic mug or plate upside down. The unglazed ring on the bottom is rough enough to act like a fine stone. Hold the knife at about 15 degrees and draw each side across the ring five or six times, alternating sides. Wipe the gray streaks off the mug and the blade. This works surprisingly well on a knife that is only a little dull.

**Sandpaper on glass.** Tape a sheet of 1000 grit wet-dry sandpaper to a flat piece of glass or a smooth countertop. Wet it and use it exactly like a whetstone. Finish on 2000 grit if you have it. This is a fair stand-in when you want to try stone sharpening before buying a stone.

**A file.** A metal file cuts fast and leaves a rough edge. Save it for garden tools, not a chef's knife.

None of these fix a knife with chips or a rounded edge. They are first aid, not a cure.

## How to Sharpen Serrated Knives

Serrated knives, such as bread knives and many steak knives, stay sharp for years because the points do most of the work and the scallops are protected. When a bread knife starts to tear soft bread instead of slicing it, sharpen only the scalloped side.

1. Find the beveled side. Serrations are ground on one side only; the other side is flat.
2. Use a thin, tapered rod. Slide it into one scallop at the same angle as the existing bevel. The [DMT Diafold Serrated Knife Sharpener](https://www.amazon.com/DMT-Diafold-Serrated-Knife-Sharpener/dp/B00004WFTY/?tag=kitchenpot-20) is a tapered diamond rod that folds into its own handle and fits large and small scallops.
3. Make three or four light strokes, pushing the rod outward and twisting it slightly so the wider part fills the scallop.
4. Move to the next scallop. Yes, every one.
5. Flip the knife and lay the flat side on a fine stone or ceramic rod. One or two flat strokes take off the burr.

Never run a bread knife flat across a whetstone or through a basic V-slot. That flattens the points. If you slice enough bread or deli meat to wear out serrations often, a [meat slicer for home use](/blog/best-meat-slicer-for-home-use/) saves the knife.

## How Often Should You Sharpen a Kitchen Knife?

There is no fixed calendar. It depends on how much you cook, what you cut and what you cut on.

| How you cook | Hone | Sharpen |
|---|---|---|
| Cook every day, lots of vegetables | 2 to 3 times a week | Every 2 to 3 months |
| Cook 3 to 4 times a week | Once a week | Every 4 to 6 months |
| Cook on weekends only | Every 2 weeks | Once or twice a year |
| Heavy meat prep, breaking down chickens | Before each session | Every 1 to 2 months |

What wears an edge fastest is the board. Glass, marble, slate and ceramic boards ruin an edge in a few cuts. Wood and soft plastic are kind to it. If you are still chopping on something hard, start there; our guides to the [best cutting board](/blog/best-cutting-board/) and the [best over-the-sink cutting board](/blog/best-over-the-sink-cutting-board/) cover materials that save your edge. Dishwashers are the other big killer: the blade bangs against racks and the heat and detergent dull it. Wash good knives by hand.

## How to Tell When the Knife Is Sharp

You will know the job is done when:

- The paper test gives a clean, quiet cut along the whole edge, heel to tip.
- The knife bites into tomato skin under its own weight.
- An onion's layers stay intact instead of being crushed. Your eyes water less, because a sharp edge breaks fewer cells.
- You see no bright line when you look straight down the edge under a light.

Check the tip and the heel, not just the middle. Beginners most often miss steel at those two ends.

A sharp edge makes thin slices and fine dice easy, which speeds up [meal prep for one person in a small kitchen](/blog/easy-meal-prep-ideas-for-one-person-in-a-small-kitchen/). It also makes a [vegetable chopper](/blog/best-vegetable-choppers/) less necessary.

## Sharpening Safety in a Small Kitchen

Sharpening is safe if you set up well. Most cuts happen when a stone slides or a hand is in the wrong place.

- **Lock the stone down.** Use its base, a damp towel underneath, or a sink bridge. A stone that moves is a stone that cuts you.
- **Protect your guide hand.** Your fingertips sit close to the edge on the stone. A cut-resistant glove like the [NoCry Cut Resistant Gloves](https://www.amazon.com/NoCry-resistant-cutting-protection-ambidextrous/dp/B0BLHJ1SF9/?tag=kitchenpot-20) on that hand takes the worry out of the first few sessions. Wear it on the hand that touches the blade, not the one on the handle.
- **Feel for the burr across the edge, never along it.** Thumb moves from spine toward edge, sideways.
- **Clear the counter.** Move food, kids, pets and the coffee cup away. In a tight galley you need at least 24 inches of clear counter or the sink. If space is the issue, [countertop organization ideas for a small kitchen](/blog/countertop-organization-ideas-for-a-small-kitchen/) help free up a working strip.
- **Wipe the blade from spine to edge** with a folded towel, never by pinching the edge.
- **Store the sharp knife safely.** A freshly sharpened knife loose in a drawer is how fingers get cut. Hang it on a [magnetic knife strip](/blog/best-magnetic-knife-strip/) or see our guide to [storing knives without a knife block](/blog/store-knives-without-a-knife-block/).

## Looking After Your Whetstone

A stone wears down in the middle with use. After a few months it develops a shallow dip, called dishing. A dished stone puts a curve on your edge and makes the tip hard to sharpen.

To check, lay a steel ruler on its edge across the stone. If you see light under the middle, it needs flattening.

To flatten it, draw a pencil grid on the wet stone, then rub it on a flattening stone in figure eights until the grid is gone. The [Nordstrand Flattening Stone](https://www.amazon.com/Nordstrand-Flattening-Stone-Re-Leveling-Waterstones/dp/B077QCYMNX/?tag=kitchenpot-20) is a coarse grooved block made for this job and costs little. If you sharpen often, the [DMT Dia-Flat Diamond Lapping Plate](https://www.amazon.com/DMT-Dia-Flat-Diamond-Lapping-Plate/dp/B005FUHTDG/?tag=kitchenpot-20) is a 4 by 10 inch diamond plate that stays flat for good and flattens a stone in a minute or two.

Flatten after every three or four sessions and store the stone dry, flat in a drawer. Our [guide to organizing kitchen drawers in a small kitchen](/blog/how-to-organize-kitchen-drawers-in-a-small-kitchen/) shows how to fit a stone, rod and guards into one shallow drawer.

## Sharpening Mistakes That Ruin a Good Edge

- **Moving on before the burr forms.** This is the number one mistake. Without a burr along the whole edge, the second side and the fine stone are just polishing a dull edge.
- **Changing the angle mid-stroke.** Rocking the wrist rounds the edge. Lock your wrist and move from the shoulder.
- **Pressing too hard.** Heavy pressure on a fine stone gouges it and on a rod chips hard steel. Light and steady wins.
- **Skipping the tip.** As you near the tip, lift the handle slightly so the curve stays on the stone.
- **Using the coarse stone for every session.** A 1000 grit side is enough for routine sharpening. A 400 grit stone every time wears a knife out years early.
- **Sharpening at the wrong angle for the knife.** Grinding a 15-degree Japanese edge to 20 wastes steel. Grinding a soft German knife to 12 makes an edge that folds on carrots.

If a knife loses its edge in days, the steel is too soft to hold one, and a better blade is the fix. Our [best chef knife](/blog/best-chef-knife/) guide covers what to look for. For vegetable-heavy cooks, a hard, flat Japanese vegetable knife such as the [Hoshanho 6.5-inch Damascus Nakiri Knife](https://www.awin1.com/cread.php?awinmid=124972&awinaffid=1956629&clickref=how-to-sharpen-a-kitchen-knife&ued=https%3A%2F%2Fhoshanho.com%2Fproducts%2F6-5-inch-damascus-nakiri-knife-vg-10-japanese-cleaver-vegetable-knife-with-olive-wood-handle) comes with a 15-degree edge on a VG-10 core. Its straight edge is also one of the easiest shapes to sharpen on a stone, because there is no curve to follow.

## A Simple Sharpening Routine for Busy Home Cooks

Put it all together and the routine is short:

1. **Every few meals:** five to eight strokes per side on a ceramic rod.
2. **When the tomato test fails after honing:** a full session on the 1000 grit side, then the 6000 side.
3. **Every three or four sessions:** flatten the stone.
4. **Once a year, or after a chip:** start on a coarse stone, or take the knife to a professional.

Keep the kit small. A combination stone, one rod and a towel fit in a single drawer, which matters when you are [stocking a small kitchen for solo cooking](/blog/how-to-stock-a-small-kitchen-for-solo-cooking/) or trying to [declutter a small kitchen](/blog/declutter-a-small-kitchen/). A stone and rod belong on any list of [small kitchen gadgets worth buying when you cook for one](/blog/small-kitchen-gadgets-worth-buying-when-you-cook-for-one/).

A sharp knife also makes tricky jobs safer, from [cracking open a coconut](/blog/how-to-crack-open-a-coconut/) to slicing fish for a [sushi kit](/blog/best-sushi-making-kit/).
