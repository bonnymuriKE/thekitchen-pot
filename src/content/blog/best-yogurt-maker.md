## How Does a Yogurt Maker Work?

A yogurt maker is a small, low-power heater with a thermostat. Its only job is to keep milk warm and steady for hours.

The cultures in yogurt are bacteria that eat lactose, the natural sugar in milk. As they eat, they make lactic acid. The acid makes the milk proteins tighten and set into a gel, and it also gives yogurt its tang.

Those bacteria work fastest between about 100°F and 115°F. Below that they slow down. Much above it they start to die. So the machine holds the milk in that window for 6 to 12 hours, and the cultures do the rest.

That is why any steady warm spot can work, including a [food dehydrator](/blog/best-food-dehydrator/) set low. A dedicated maker just makes it repeatable.

## How to Make Yogurt in a Yogurt Maker

The steps are the same in almost every machine:

1. **Heat the milk to about 180°F.** Use a heavy pot over medium heat, or a [double boiler](/blog/how-to-use-a-double-boiler/) if you don't want to stir constantly. Hold it there for a few minutes.
2. **Cool it to about 110°F.** Set the pot in a sink of cold water to speed this up. Check with a thermometer.
3. **Add the starter.** Stir a few spoonfuls of plain yogurt with live cultures into a cup of the warm milk, then stir that back into the pot. A freeze-dried starter works too.
4. **Pour into the jar or jars and incubate.** Six to twelve hours is typical. Longer gives a tangier, firmer yogurt.
5. **Chill it.** Move the yogurt to the fridge for at least a few hours. It firms up more as it cools.

A [stockpot with a good lid](/blog/best-stockpot-with-a-lid/) that is heavy on the bottom makes step one much less likely to scorch.

### Why Heat the Milk First?

Heating to about 180°F does two things. It kills stray bacteria that would compete with your starter. It also changes the milk proteins so they hold more water, which gives you a thicker, smoother yogurt.

Some people skip this step with ultra-pasteurized milk. The yogurt still sets, but it is usually thinner.

Stirring in a little nonfat dry milk before heating also helps. More milk solids mean a thicker yogurt with no other changes.

### Jars or One Big Container?

Jars give you portions. You can flavor each one, and you never contaminate the batch with a used spoon. The downside is washing seven small jars and lids.

One big container is faster to fill, pour and strain. It suits Greek yogurt, since you can tip the whole batch into a strainer. If you [meal prep for one in a small kitchen](/blog/easy-meal-prep-ideas-for-one-person-in-a-small-kitchen/), you can always portion a big batch into [airtight food storage containers](/blog/best-airtight-food-storage-containers/) afterward.

### Do You Need Temperature Control?

For plain dairy yogurt, no. A fixed setting around 110°F works fine.

You do need it for three things. Specialty probiotic cultures like L. reuteri often call for around 100°F for 36 hours. Some plant-based starters prefer lower heat. And if you want to fine-tune the tang, a few degrees up or down makes a real difference.

### Timer and Auto Shut-Off

A timer is the feature that lets you sleep. Without one, a batch started at 10 p.m. keeps fermenting until you wake up, and it can get very sour.

Shut-off is not cooling, though. Plan batches so they finish when you are home.

## How to Make Greek Yogurt at Home

Greek yogurt is regular yogurt with much of the whey drained off. No special culture is needed.

Spoon chilled, set yogurt into a fine strainer, cover it, and leave it in the fridge. Two to four hours gives a thick Greek-style yogurt. Overnight gives labneh, a tangy spread. Either way, expect to lose a good share of the volume as whey.

Save the whey for smoothies, soaked oats and bread dough. A blender from our [best blenders for smoothies](/blog/best-blenders-for-smoothies/) list will mix it in without a trace.

Very thick strained yogurt can stand in for cream cheese in dips. Like cream cheese, it turns grainy after freezing, as covered in [can you freeze cream cheese](/blog/can-you-freeze-cream-cheese/).

## Can You Use Plant-Based Milk in a Yogurt Maker?

Yes, but expect a different result. Dairy cultures feed on lactose and set by tightening milk proteins. Most plant milks have little of either.

Soy milk is the easiest, since it has the most protein. Almond, oat, cashew and coconut milks usually need help. Use a starter made for plant milks, and add a thickener like agar, tapioca starch or pectin before heating. Our guide to [what to use instead of cornstarch](/blog/what-to-use-instead-of-cornstarch/) explains how those thickeners behave.

Plain, unsweetened milk with few additives cultures best. If you are choosing a dairy-free milk, our lists of the [best substitutes for whole milk](/blog/best-substitutes-for-whole-milk/) and the [best coconut milk substitute](/blog/best-coconut-milk-substitute/) are a good start.

A maker with adjustable temperature, like the YM260 or the Luvele, gives plant milks the best chance.

## Is Homemade Yogurt Safe?

Yes, with basic care. Yogurt's own acid protects it once it has set. The risky part is the hours before that, when the milk is warm and still mild.

A few habits keep it safe:

- **Start clean.** Wash jars, lids and spoons in hot, soapy water, then rinse and let them dry.
- **Use fresh starter.** A starter yogurt that is near its date or has been opened for a while may be weak. Weak starters set slowly, which gives other bacteria time to grow.
- **Don't add the starter to hot milk.** Above about 115°F, you risk killing it.
- **Chill it promptly.** Move set yogurt to a fridge at 40°F or colder.
- **Eat it within about one to two weeks.** Like any [leftovers in the fridge](/blog/how-long-do-leftovers-last/), it doesn't last forever.

If a batch never sets, throw it away and start again with fresh starter. Do the same with any batch that grows mold, shows pink or orange spots, or smells yeasty or off.

You can also freeze yogurt in small portions for smoothies. The texture changes, but the flavor holds. Our guide on [how to freeze single portions](/blog/how-to-freeze-single-portions/) covers the containers and labels that work best.
