A round-bottom wok on an electric stove touches the burner in about one spot. The rest of the pan just sits there, waiting for heat that never really arrives.

That single point of contact is the whole problem. Fix it and everything else about wok cooking on electric gets easier.

**The short version:** get a flat-bottom wok, 13 to 14 inches across, with a base wide enough to cover your biggest element. Carbon steel is the best all-round choice. On a glass top, make sure the base is smooth, and lift the pan instead of sliding it.

## Why a Round-Bottom Wok Fails on Electric

A traditional wok is round underneath for a reason. It sits in a ring above a gas flame, and the flame climbs the sides. The whole bowl gets hot at once, which is what makes real stir-frying work.

An electric stove heats the opposite way. There is no flame to wrap around anything. Heat moves by contact, straight up through whatever metal is pressed against the coil or the glass.

Put a round wok on that surface and you get a coin-sized contact patch. The sides stay cool, food releases water and steams in its own juice, and the pan rocks every time you stir. GE's guidance for smooth glass cooktops says it plainly: [flat pans are needed](https://products.geappliances.com/appliance/gea-support-search-content?contentId=16259) because the bottom of the pan has to touch the glass for heat to transfer.

A wok ring does not rescue this. On gas a ring holds the pan in the flame, but on electric it lifts the base away from the element and traps heat under the pan. That is bad for the cooktop and worse for your food.

### How Wide Should the Flat Base Be?

Most flat-bottom woks have a base between 5 and 7 inches across. Measure your largest burner and match it. A 14-inch wok with a 6-inch base on an 8-inch element still works, because the sloped sides catch the spill-over heat.

What you want to avoid is a wok so wide that the rim hangs far past the burner. The middle scorches while the edges stay lukewarm, which is the same sizing mistake people make with an oversized [skillet](/blog/difference-between-skillet-and-frying-pan/).

## The Heat Problem Nobody Mentions

Electric burners do not hold a steady output. They cycle on and off to average out to the setting you picked, and that is normal behavior rather than a fault. GE says the cycling happens even on the highest setting, and that it happens more often when the pan is not flat or is the wrong size.

Radiant glass cooktops add a second limit. A [temperature limiter](https://products.geappliances.com/appliance/gea-support-search-content?contentId=22880) under each element cuts power when the glass gets near 880°F, then resets once it cools. Cook long and hot in one spot and you will feel the burner drop out from under you.

So recovery is the real skill on electric. When cold vegetables hit a hot pan, the metal dumps heat into them fast, and the element cannot surge to catch up. Three habits fix most of it:

1. **Preheat longer.** Give the wok four or five minutes on medium-high before any oil goes in. A drop of water should skitter across the base.
2. **Cook in batches.** Half a pound of protein at a time, not a pound and a half. Crowding is what turns a stir-fry into a stew.
3. **Rest between batches.** Sixty seconds with the pan empty lets the element and the metal both catch up.

Thin metal helps here too, which is why carbon steel outperforms thick cast iron on electric. It gives heat to the food quickly and takes it back from the element quickly. If you want the opposite experience, where the burner responds instantly, that is the case for an [induction cooktop](/blog/best-induction-cooktop-for-the-money/) and [cookware built for induction](/blog/best-cookware-for-induction-cooktop/).

## Matching the Material to Your Cooktop

Your stove narrows the field faster than your budget does. Here is how the four materials behave on each surface.

| Material | Coil element | Radiant glass | Induction | Main trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Carbon steel | Excellent | Excellent if the base is smooth | Excellent | Needs seasoning and hand washing |
| Clad stainless | Very good | Excellent | Excellent | Food sticks until you learn the preheat |
| Hard-anodized nonstick | Good | Good | Usually no | Coating wears out, low heat ceiling |
| Cast iron | Excellent | Not recommended | Excellent | Heavy, and rough on glass |

A few extra notes worth knowing:

- **On glass, lift, never slide.** Dragging a loaded pan is what puts the fine arc-shaped scratches in a cooktop. The same rule applies to every heavy pan you own.
- **Check the magnet if induction is in your future.** Carbon steel, cast iron and clad stainless all pass. Plain aluminum does not, and our guide on [using non-induction cookware on an induction cooktop](/blog/how-to-use-non-induction-cookware-on-induction-cooktop/) explains the workaround and its limits.
- **Aluminum-only woks warp.** Thin aluminum expands unevenly over a hot ring, and once the base bows it will never sit flat again.
- **If you are replacing more than the wok**, a [tri-ply set under $200](/blog/best-cookware-set-under-200/) covers the pots and pans around it for less than you would expect.

## How to Season a Carbon Steel Wok

Seasoning sounds mysterious and is not. You are heating a very thin film of oil past its smoke point so it breaks down and bonds into a hard, slick layer. That layer is what makes carbon steel release food, and it is why it gets better with age.

1. **Scrub off the factory coating.** New unseasoned woks ship with an oil or lacquer film. Use hot water, dish soap and a scouring pad, and keep going until the rinse water runs clean. This is the only time soap and a scrubber are welcome.
2. **Dry it on the burner.** Put the empty wok on medium heat for a couple of minutes until every drop is gone.
3. **Heat until the colour shifts.** Keep the empty wok on medium-high. The steel turns straw yellow, then blue-grey, starting at the centre. Tilt it so the colour spreads up the sides.
4. **Wipe on a very thin coat of oil.** Use a neutral high-smoke-point oil such as grapeseed or canola. Apply it with a wadded paper towel held in tongs, then wipe most of it back off. Thick oil goes sticky rather than hard.
5. **Heat until the smoke stops.** Let it smoke and then stop smoking, which is the oil finishing its change into a solid layer. Turn on the extractor first.
6. **Repeat two or three times.** Each round darkens the surface a little more.

After that, cooking is the seasoning. Fry a batch of onions or ginger in a generous amount of oil for the first meal. The mechanism is the same one behind [seasoning a stainless steel pan](/blog/how-to-season-stainless-steel-pans/) and behind the patina on a [cast iron skillet](/blog/lodge-cast-iron-skillet-review/).

### Keeping the Seasoning Alive

- Wash with hot water and a soft brush, not soap, and never in the dishwasher.
- Dry it on a warm burner, not in the rack.
- Rub a few drops of oil over the inside while it is still warm.
- Cook acidic sauces in something else. Tomato, vinegar and wine strip a young seasoning fast, and that is a job for [clad stainless](/blog/what-is-stainless-steel-cookware/) or a [sauté pan](/blog/best-saute-pan/).

If rust does appear, scrub the spot back to bare metal and re-season just that area. Nothing is ruined.

## Getting More Heat Out of an Electric Burner

Electric will never match a restaurant wok range. It can still turn out a good stir-fry if you work with the burner instead of against it.

- **Use the biggest element.** More contact area means more heat into the base, and less time cycling.
- **Preheat empty.** Four to five minutes on medium-high. Oil goes in after the pan is hot, not before.
- **Dry your ingredients.** Water on the surface of vegetables boils off before anything can brown, and that steam is what makes home stir-fry soggy.
- **Prep everything first.** Once the pan is hot there is no time to chop. Line up bowls in cooking order, then go.
- **Cook protein and vegetables separately.** Sear the meat, lift it out, cook the vegetables, then return the meat at the end.
- **Add sauce last and let it bubble.** Cornstarch-thickened sauces need a hard boil to turn glossy. If you are out of cornstarch, our list of [substitutes for cornstarch](/blog/what-to-use-instead-of-cornstarch/) covers what else thickens.

Serve stir-fry with rice you started before you began cooking. A [rice cooker for brown rice](/blog/best-rice-cooker-for-brown-rice/) handles it unattended, or follow our steps for [cooking rice in a rice cooker](/blog/how-to-cook-rice-in-a-rice-cooker/).
