## How We Chose

No physical testing happens here, so the picks come from things that can be checked and compared. For every cooktop on this list we pulled the manufacturer's own spec sheet for wattage, element sizes, dimensions and safety certifications, then cross-checked the same numbers against retailer listings. We weighted three things heavily:

- **Real power on a real circuit.** A 1800-watt portable burner is the practical ceiling on a 120-volt, 15-amp outlet. Anything claiming more is either rounding up or expecting a 20-amp circuit.
- **Safety hardware that's actually listed.** Pan detection, child lock, overheat shutoff and a residual heat indicator are the four that matter, and they show up in the specs below only where the maker lists them.
- **Longevity signals.** Warranty length, ETL or NSF certification, and repeated owner complaints about fan noise, error codes or glass cracking.

Prices move constantly on this category, so treat any figure below as a starting point rather than a promise.

The decision usually comes down to wiring and permanence rather than cooking quality. A 1800-watt portable burner and a 2000-watt built-in element behave almost identically under a saucepan.

## What Power and Wiring You Actually Need

This is where people run into trouble, so the numbers are worth spelling out.

A standard North American kitchen outlet is 120 volts on a 15-amp circuit, which tops out at 1,800 watts. Electrical code treats anything running three hours or more as a continuous load and caps that at 80% of the circuit, about 1,440 watts, so a 1,800-watt burner is meant for shorter bursts. That ceiling is why full-size portable induction burners stop at 1,800 watts. Plug one in alongside a toaster or a kettle on the same circuit and the breaker will trip. Give it its own outlet.

Built-ins are a different animal. Both 30-inch cooktops above run on 208-240 volts and want a 40-amp breaker. GE lists a 7.7 kW maximum connected load at 240 volts for the PHP9030DTBB; Empava lists 7200 watts for the 30EC04 and 7700 watts for its IDC30 model. That's range-level wiring, not cooktop-level.

One practical note on efficiency: the [U.S. Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/articles/making-switch-induction-stoves-or-cooktops) reports that induction appliances are up to three times more efficient than gas and up to 10% more efficient than smooth-top electric. Duxtop rates the 9600LS at 83% efficiency, and Nuwave claims 85% to 90% energy transfer. The practical effect is less wasted heat in the room, which matters most in a small kitchen with poor ventilation. Our guide to [energy-efficient cookware for a small kitchen](/blog/how-to-choose-energy-efficient-cookware-for-a-small-kitchen/) covers the other half of that equation, and our list of [compact Energy Star appliances](/blog/best-compact-energy-star-appliances-for-small-kitchens/) covers the rest of the room.

## Will Your Pans Work on Induction?

Induction heats the pan, not the surface, by inducing a current in magnetic metal. So the rule is simple: if a magnet sticks firmly to the base, it heats. If it slides off, nothing happens.

**Works on induction:**

- Cast iron and enameled cast iron
- Carbon steel, including most woks sold for induction
- Magnetic stainless steel, including most tri-ply sets
- Nonstick and ceramic pans built on a magnetic steel base

**Doesn't work:**

- Plain aluminum
- Copper without a steel disc
- Glass, ceramic and stoneware
- Many hard-anodized nonstick pans

Two extra things worth knowing. Not all stainless steel is magnetic, so test the actual pan rather than trusting the label. And the base has to be flat: a warped pan sits off the coil and heats unevenly or triggers an error. If your current pans fail the magnet test, you have two options. Replace them, starting with our roundup of the [best cookware for induction cooktops](/blog/best-cookware-for-induction-cooktop/) or the budget-friendly sets in our [cookware sets under $200](/blog/best-cookware-set-under-200/) guide. Or use a converter disc, which our guide on [using non-induction cookware on an induction cooktop](/blog/how-to-use-non-induction-cookware-on-induction-cooktop/) explains in plain terms, including why it undoes most of the speed advantage.

Cast iron works beautifully on induction but is heavy enough to chip glass if you drag it. ## Fitting Induction Into a Small Kitchen

A portable burner solves a specific small-kitchen problem: it gives you cooking capacity that disappears when you're done. That changes how you plan the room.

- **Store it vertically.** A 2-inch-thick burner slides into the gap beside a baking sheet. Our [vertical storage ideas for small kitchens](/blog/vertical-storage-ideas-for-small-kitchens/) show where that gap usually is.
- **Cook where you have outlets, not where the stove is.** A portable burner on a stable side table doubles your cooking surface during a big meal.
- **Match the pan to the coil.** On a 7-inch coil, a 12-inch skillet will have cool edges. Smaller pans really do cook better here, which is the argument our [stackable pots and pans](/blog/7-best-stackable-pots-and-pans/) roundup makes for a different reason.
- **Keep the vents clear.** Two to four inches of air on the vented sides. Don't box it in against a backsplash.
- **Plan the landing zone.** You need somewhere to put a hot pan. Our guide on [organizing a small kitchen with no pantry](/blog/how-to-organize-a-small-kitchen-with-no-pantry/) covers making that space appear.

Induction also runs cooler than gas or radiant electric because the heat stays in the pan, which is a real comfort gain in a studio. It doesn't remove the need for extraction, though: steam and frying smoke still need somewhere to go. ## Cleaning and Caring for the Glass

The good news is that induction glass stays far cooler than a radiant element, so spills rarely bake on. A damp cloth and a drop of dish soap handles almost everything, as long as you do it once the surface has cooled.

For the stubborn cases:

| Problem | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Boiled-over starch or sugar | Cooktop cream and a plastic scraper at a low angle, while the glass is barely warm |
| Greasy film after frying | Warm soapy water, then a dry microfiber cloth to stop streaks |
| White mineral haze | A little white vinegar on a cloth, then rinse and dry |
| Fine scratches from sliding pans | Not removable. Lift pans instead of dragging them |
| Error code with a pan on | Check the base is flat and magnetic, and at least 5 inches across |

Never use steel wool, scouring powder or a melamine sponge on induction glass. And keep sugar syrup off it while it's hot: sugar can pit ceramic glass permanently. If a pan has a rough or rusty base, deal with that first. Our guide to [cleaning stainless steel pans](/blog/how-to-clean-stainless-steel-pans/) and our walkthrough on [cleaning a ceramic pan](/blog/how-to-clean-ceramic-pan/) both cover getting bases smooth again.

One more habit: wipe under the pan as well as under the cooktop. Grit trapped between a pan base and the glass is what causes most of the scratching people blame on the cookware itself. Our roundup of the [best nonstick pans](/blog/best-nonstick-pans-with-buying-guide/) flags which bases stay smooth longest.
