## How Pour Over Works, and Why Cone Shape Matters

Pour over is gravity brewing. You pour hot water over ground coffee in a filter. The water dissolves flavor from the grounds as it passes through, then drips into the cup below.

| Design | How Water Moves | What It Tastes Like | Forgiveness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Open cone, one big hole (V60) | Fast; your pour sets the speed | Bright, clear, easy to change | Low |
| Thick-paper cone (Chemex) | Slow, held back by heavy filter | Very clean and light | Medium |
| Flat bottom, small holes (Kalita Wave) | Slow and even | Balanced and steady | High |
| Wedge cone, small exit (Melitta) | Slow; one or two big pours work | Simple, drip-style cup | High |
| Metal mesh cone (Kinto) | Fairly fast; oils pass through | Fuller body, some sediment | Medium |

## Paper vs Metal Filter: Which Is Better?

**Paper filters** catch the coffee oils and the finest grounds. The result is clean, bright and clear. Paper also catches cafestol, a natural compound in coffee oil that can raise LDL cholesterol. If you drink several cups a day and keep an eye on cholesterol, paper is the sensible choice. Brown or white paper both work, but rinse either one with hot water before brewing.

**Metal filters** let the oils and a few tiny particles pass through. The coffee feels thicker and richer, and there is some sediment in the last sip. You never buy filters again, which cuts waste. You do need to rinse the mesh well each time, because old oils turn stale and taste rancid.

If you like a clean cup, choose a V60, Chemex or Kalita with paper. If you like body, start with the [Kinto carafe set with a steel filter](https://www.awin1.com/cread.php?awinmid=104879&awinaffid=1956629&clickref=best-pour-over-coffee-maker&ued=https%3A%2F%2Fkinto-usa.com%2Fproducts%2F27621). ## Pour Over vs Drip: Is It Worth the Effort?

A drip machine simply does the pouring for you. The catch is heat and control. Coffee brews best at 195 to 205°F. Many budget drip machines never reach that range, and their shower heads often wet the grounds unevenly. Pour over lets you set the water temperature and wet every bit of coffee yourself.

So pour over usually tastes cleaner and sweeter. It also takes four or five minutes of your attention. If you need a full pot with no effort, a machine still makes sense. For iced coffee, slow-steeped [cold brew](/blog/best-cold-brew-coffee-maker/) is a different method entirely, and it does not need hot water at all.

## The Pour Over Coffee Ratio, Grind and Temperature

### Coffee to Water Ratio

Use 1:15 to 1:17 by weight. That means 1 gram of coffee for every 15 to 17 grams of water. Start at 1:16 and adjust from there.

| Cups | Coffee | Water (1:16) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 mug (about 10 oz) | 18 to 20 g | 290 to 320 g |
| 2 mugs | 36 to 40 g | 580 to 640 g |
| 4 cups (Chemex or Kinto server) | 40 to 45 g | 640 to 720 g |

A gram of water weighs the same as a milliliter, so a kitchen scale with a timer is all you need. If you think in cups and quarts, our [quick guide to ounces in a quart](/blog/how-many-ounces-in-a-quart/) helps with conversions.

### Grind Size

Pour over wants a medium-fine to medium grind, about like table salt. Go a little finer for the V60 and Melitta, medium for the Kalita, and a little coarser for the Chemex and metal filters.

The grinder matters more than the brewer. Blade grinders chop coffee into dust and boulders, which brew unevenly. A burr grinder crushes it to an even size. The [OXO Brew Conical Burr Coffee Grinder](https://www.amazon.com/OXO-BREW-Conical-Coffee-Grinder/dp/B07CSKGLMM/?tag=kitchenpot-20) is a reliable choice with a wide range of settings.

### Water Temperature

Aim for 195 to 205°F. If you have no thermometer, boil the water and let it sit for about 30 seconds. Use the hotter end for light roasts and the cooler end for dark roasts, which turn bitter more easily.

## How to Make Pour Over Coffee, Step by Step

This method works for a V60, Kalita Wave and the Kinto brewers. For a Chemex, use a coarser grind and expect a longer drain.

1. **Heat the water.** Bring it to 195 to 205°F, or boil and wait 30 seconds.
2. **Weigh and grind.** Use 20 g of coffee for one large mug and grind it medium-fine.
3. **Rinse the filter.** Pour hot water through the paper, then throw that water away. This warms the brewer and removes paper taste.
4. **Add the coffee and level it.** Give the brewer a gentle shake so the bed is flat.
5. **Bloom.** Start a timer and pour about 40 to 50 g of water, enough to wet all the grounds. Wait 30 to 45 seconds. Fresh coffee will bubble as trapped gas escapes.
6. **Pour in stages.** Add the rest of the water slowly in small circles, keeping the level steady. Avoid pouring straight onto the paper.
7. **Let it drain.** One mug should finish in about 2.5 to 3.5 minutes. Swirl the brewer once near the end to flatten the bed.
8. **Taste and adjust.** Sour means grind finer. Bitter means grind coarser.

Once you have it right, a [Kinto travel tumbler](/blog/kinto-travel-tumbler-review/) keeps a fresh pour over hot for the commute. If you like a milky cup, a [milk frother](/blog/best-milk-frother/) turns pour over into a simple cafe au lait.

## The Coffee Matters More Than the Cone

Even the best pour over coffee maker cannot fix stale beans. Pour over shows off the coffee itself, so fresh beans make the biggest difference you will notice.

Buy whole beans and grind them just before brewing. Use them within a few weeks of the roast date. Store them in a sealed, opaque container away from heat and light, not in the fridge. A dark [corner cabinet](/blog/organize-a-corner-kitchen-cabinet/) is better than a sunny shelf by the stove.

## How to Clean a Pour Over Coffee Maker

Rinse the brewer with hot water right after each use, because coffee oils turn stale fast. Once a week, soak metal filters in hot soapy water. If you have hard water, descale your kettle every month or two, as shown in [how to clean an electric kettle](/blog/how-to-clean-an-electric-kettle/).
