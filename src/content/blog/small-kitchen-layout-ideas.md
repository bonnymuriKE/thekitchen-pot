Picking the right small kitchen layout ideas comes down to inches. A few inches of aisle or landing space decide whether a tiny kitchen feels easy or cramped.

This guide compares the four layouts that work in small rooms: one-wall, galley, L-shape and U-shape. We give you the clearance numbers, the landing space rules and who each layout suits, so you can plan your kitchen or rearrange the one you rent.

**The short version:** An L-shape is the best all-round layout for most small kitchens. A galley packs the most counter into a long, narrow room. A one-wall layout is the only real choice in most studios. A U-shape works only if the room is at least about 8 feet wide. Whatever you pick, keep the aisle at 42 inches, give every appliance a landing spot, and keep the sink, stove and fridge within a few steps of each other.

## The Four Small Kitchen Layouts Compared

Every small kitchen fits one of four basic shapes. The walls, doors and windows set the shape, not taste.

| Layout | Best room shape | Minimum room width | Counter you get | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| One-wall | Studio or open plan | About 6 feet deep (counter plus walkway) | Lowest | Solo cooks, studios, renters |
| Galley | Long and narrow | About 7.5 feet wall to wall | High for the floor area | One serious cook |
| L-shape | Square or near-square corner | About 7 by 7 feet | Medium to high | Couples, open plans, adding a peninsula |
| U-shape | Square room, one open side | About 8 feet wall to wall | Highest | Keen cooks who want storage |

## Small Kitchen Layout Dimensions You Need to Know

| Element | Standard size or clearance | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Base counter depth | 24 to 25 inches | Fits standard base cabinets and appliances |
| Counter height | 36 inches | Comfortable working height for most adults |
| Upper cabinet depth | 12 inches | Leaves headroom over the counter |
| Gap between counter and uppers | 18 inches | Room for a coffee maker or toaster underneath |
| Work aisle, one cook | 42 inches | You can open an oven or dishwasher and still stand in front of it |
| Work aisle, two cooks | 48 inches | Two people can pass behind each other |
| Walkway with no work zone | 36 inches | Enough for walking, not for working |
| Main prep space | 36 inches wide by 24 inches deep | Enough for a cutting board and a bowl |
| Standing room by an open dishwasher | 21 inches | You can load it without stepping sideways |
| Counter seat width | 24 inches per stool | Elbow room for each person |

### What These Numbers Mean in Practice

The aisle number is the one people get wrong most. The 42 inches is measured from counter edge to counter edge, not wall to wall. A standard counter takes 24 to 25 inches from each wall. So a galley with counters on both sides needs about 90 inches, or 7.5 feet, from wall to wall.

The 36-inch prep space is the other big one. That is the single clear stretch of counter where you chop and mix. Most small kitchens have enough total counter but no single 36-inch run. ## Is the Kitchen Work Triangle Still Relevant?

Yes, but treat it as a check, not a law. The work triangle is the imaginary line that joins the sink, the stove and the fridge.

Our rule of thumb for the work triangle:

- Each leg should be 4 to 9 feet long.
- The three legs together should total 13 to 26 feet.
- No leg should cross through an island or a cabinet by more than 12 inches.
- No main walkway or doorway should cut through the triangle.

In a small kitchen, the legs are rarely too long. The real problem is legs that are too short. If the sink sits right against the stove, you have nowhere to rest a hot pan.

### Think in Zones, Too

Most kitchens also have a microwave, a dishwasher and a trash can. So we also plan in zones: a prep zone, a cooking zone, a cleanup zone and a storage zone.

The prep zone sits between the sink and the stove. The cleanup zone keeps the sink, dishwasher and trash together. The storage zone keeps the fridge near the door so people can grab a drink without walking through the cooking area. If you cook on a small stove, our guide to [cooking a full meal with only two burners](/blog/how-to-cook-a-full-meal-with-only-two-burners/) shows how to plan that zone for real dinners.

## Landing Space: The Rule Most Small Kitchens Break

Landing space is the clear counter next to an appliance, where you set things down. Without it, you end up holding a hot tray with nowhere to put it.

| Appliance | Landing space we recommend | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Fridge | 15 inches | On the handle side, or across the aisle within 48 inches |
| Stove or cooktop | 12 inches on one side, 15 inches on the other | Both sides, same height as the cooktop |
| Sink | 24 inches on one side, 18 inches on the other | One side for dirty dishes, one for clean |
| Wall oven | 15 inches | Beside it, or across the aisle within 48 inches |
| Microwave | 15 inches | Above, below or beside it |

These spaces can overlap. If the sink and stove sit side by side, one 24-inch stretch between them can serve both. Small kitchens depend on that overlap.

Never put a stove right at the end of a counter run with no landing on that side. It is a burn risk, and handles that hang past the edge get knocked. If there is no counter beside the fridge, park a cart on the handle side as a landing spot. Its wood top is a solid place to set down milk and leftovers, and the wheels lock.

## The One-Wall Kitchen Layout

A one-wall kitchen puts everything on a single wall: fridge, sink, stove and cabinets. It is standard in studios and basement suites.

### How to Set Up a One-Wall Kitchen

Put the sink in the middle. Put the fridge at one end and the stove at the other. This gives you two counter runs, one on each side of the sink. One becomes your prep zone and the other your cleanup zone.

A one-wall kitchen needs at least 8 feet of wall to work well. Under that, you will have a fridge, a 24-inch range and a sink with almost no counter. At 10 to 12 feet, it becomes a very usable kitchen.

The work triangle here is a straight line, which is fine. Just keep the fridge and stove from sitting side by side.

When the run is short, borrow counter from the stove. Lift it off before you turn on any burner.

### Who It Suits

One-wall layouts suit solo cooks and couples who cook simple meals. They leave the most floor open, which matters in a studio. Their weak point is counter space and storage. Make up for it by going up: use the wall above the uppers and the ends of the run. ## The Galley Kitchen Layout

A galley kitchen has two parallel counter runs with an aisle between them.

### Galley Kitchen Measurements

Keep the aisle at 42 inches. With 24-inch counters on both sides, that means about 7.5 feet from wall to wall. If your galley is narrower, 36 inches is the floor. Below that, you cannot open the oven and stand in front of it at the same time.

If the galley is wider than about 10 feet, you walk across a wide gap with every pan. At that width, an L-shape with a peninsula works better.

### Where to Put Each Appliance

Put the sink and stove on the same side if you can. Then you never carry a full pot of boiling water across the aisle. Put the fridge on the other side, near the entrance. That keeps traffic out of the cooking zone.

Do not put the dishwasher directly across from the oven. If both doors open at once, the aisle is blocked. Offset them by at least one cabinet width. ### Who It Suits

A galley is the most efficient small kitchen for one cook. Everything is within a step or two. It gets crowded with two cooks unless the aisle is 48 inches. If two doors open into the galley, keep the walkway clear and store nothing on the floor. The one thing that belongs there is a low runner. ## The Small L-Shaped Kitchen Layout

An L-shaped kitchen uses two walls that meet at a corner. It is the most flexible layout for small rooms.

### Small Kitchen Layout L Shape Tips

Put the sink on one leg and the stove on the other. Keep the fridge at the open end of one leg. This gives you a clean triangle with no traffic running through it.

Keep each leg at least 6 to 7 feet long. A leg shorter than that leaves almost no counter after you fit an appliance. Avoid putting the sink or stove right in the corner. That corner counter is hard to reach and becomes a dumping ground.

### Deal With the Corner

The corner is the weak spot of any L-shape. The base cabinet there is deep and dark. A blind corner cabinet loses a lot of usable space unless you fit a pull-out or a lazy Susan. ### Who It Suits

An L-shape suits couples, open-plan rooms and anyone who wants to add a peninsula or a small table later. It leaves one side open and lets two people work, one on each leg.

## The Small U-Shaped Kitchen Layout

A U-shaped kitchen has counters on three walls. It gives you the most counter and storage of any layout. In a small room, it is also the easiest to get wrong.

### U-Shaped Kitchen Measurements

You need at least 42 inches of clear floor between the two facing legs. Add 24 inches of counter on each side, and the room must be about 8 feet wide. If your room is narrower, skip the U. You will have a box you cannot turn around in.

A U-shape has two corners, so it has two blind corner cabinets. Plan both before you buy cabinets. Lose them, and the U gives you little more storage than an L. Its two shelves swing and slide out, so you never kneel to reach the back.

### Who It Suits

A U-shape suits a keen cook in a small, square room. It is a poor fit for two cooks unless the room is about 9 feet or wider.

## L-Shaped or Galley: Which Is Better for a Small Space?

This is the most common choice in small homes. The room shape usually decides it.

| Question | Choose a galley | Choose an L-shape |
|---|---|---|
| Room shape | Long and narrow | Square or near-square |
| Number of cooks | One | One or two |
| Need seating? | Hard to add | Easy to add with a peninsula |
| Open to a living area? | Feels closed in | Feels open |
| Walking distance | Shortest | Short |
| Corner cabinets | None | One |

Our view: if the room is less than 8 feet wide, choose the galley. If it is close to square, choose the L.

## Can You Fit an Island in a Small Kitchen?

Sometimes, but less often than people hope. An island needs clear floor on every side where you work or walk.

### Island Clearance Rules

Keep 42 inches of clear floor on each working side, and 36 inches on any side that is only a walkway. A small island is at least 2 feet deep and 4 feet long. Add the clearances, and you need about 9 feet of clear floor in front of the counter. With counters on one side only, that means a room about 10.5 feet wide. With counters on both sides, it means about 13 feet.

If you add stools, allow 24 inches of counter width per seat. Give each seat a counter overhang of about 15 inches for knee room at 36-inch counter height. Pick backless stools so they slide fully under the edge. ## Small Kitchen Layout with Peninsula: Is It Better Than an Island?

In most small kitchens, yes. A peninsula is a counter that sticks out from a wall or the end of a cabinet run. It is open on three sides instead of four.

### Why a Peninsula Works in Small Rooms

A peninsula needs clearance on fewer sides. That alone can save 3 to 4 feet of floor compared with an island. It also adds base cabinets underneath, so you gain storage as well as counter.

Seating works well along the outer edge, giving you a breakfast bar. The same backless counter stools from the island section tuck under it. In a small L-shape, adding a peninsula turns it into a G-shape and gives you a clear line between the kitchen and the living area.

### Peninsula Measurements

Keep 42 inches between the peninsula and the opposite counter. Make it at least 24 inches deep for prep. Go 36 inches or deeper if you want seating on the far side.

The one catch: a peninsula can block traffic. If it cuts off the path to the fridge or a door, shorten it.

## What Is the Best Layout for a Small Kitchen?

There is no single best layout, only the best one for your room. Use this order to decide:

1. **Measure wall to wall in both directions.** Under 7.5 feet wide rules out a galley with two full runs. Under 8 feet rules out a U-shape.
2. **Mark the doors and windows.** Every doorway that cuts a wall shortens your counter. A window over the sink is fine. A window where you want uppers is not.
3. **Count your cooks.** One cook can work in a 42-inch aisle. Two cooks need 48 inches.
4. **Decide if you need seating.** If yes, an L-shape with a peninsula is usually the answer.
5. **Check the landing space.** Every appliance needs its clear counter. If a layout cannot give that, reject it.

## How to Plan a Small Kitchen Layout in 6 Steps

1. **Draw the room to scale.** Use graph paper with one square per 6 inches. Mark walls, doors, windows, outlets, and the water and gas lines.
2. **Place the sink first.** Plumbing is the hardest thing to move. Every other choice works around it.
3. **Place the stove.** Keep it 12 to 15 inches of counter away from walls and corners on each side. Make sure a vent can reach it. Our [small kitchen range hood ventilation guide](/blog/small-kitchen-guide-range-hood-ventilation-cooking-smell/) explains your options.
4. **Place the fridge.** Put it at the end of a run, near the entrance. Check that its door swings fully open without hitting a wall.
5. **Find your 36-inch prep space.** It should sit between the sink and stove. If it does not exist, you need to move something or add a cart.
6. **Walk it.** Tape the outlines on the floor with painter's tape. Open imaginary doors. Walk from the fridge to the sink to the stove. Fix anything that feels tight before you buy.

## Common Small Kitchen Layout Mistakes

### An Aisle That Is Too Narrow

A 30-inch aisle looks fine on paper, but an open dishwasher fills it. Hold to 42 inches and never go below 36.

### No Landing Space Beside the Stove

A stove pushed against a wall or tall cabinet has no landing on that side. You will set hot pans on the burners or the floor. Leave at least 12 inches.

### Fridge and Stove Side by Side

The heat makes the fridge work harder. Put at least one cabinet width between them.

### Doors That Collide

Oven, dishwasher, fridge and cabinet doors can hit each other in a small room. Check every swing on your plan.

### Forcing an Island

An island with 30 inches of clearance makes the kitchen feel smaller, not bigger. Use a cart or a peninsula instead.

### Ignoring the Corners

Dead corner cabinets in an L or U waste space you paid for. Plan a pull-out, a lazy Susan or deep drawers from the start.

### Planning Storage Last

Layout and storage go together. Decide where pots, pans, lids and pantry food will live while you plan. Our guides to [organizing pot lids](/blog/how-to-organize-pot-lids-in-a-small-kitchen/) and [organizing pots and pans](/blog/how-to-organize-pots-and-pans/) help you size the cabinets you need.

### Gaps Where Pests Hide

Gaps behind the fridge and under the stove collect crumbs. In a tight layout, you cannot reach them. Leave enough room to pull each appliance out to clean. Our guide on how to [keep pests out of a small kitchen](/blog/how-to-keep-pests-out-of-a-small-kitchen/) explains why it matters.
