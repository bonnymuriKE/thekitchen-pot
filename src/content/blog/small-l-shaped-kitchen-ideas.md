Good small L-shaped kitchen ideas are about more than looks. They decide where the sink, stove and fridge go, what happens in the corner and how you use the open side.

An L-shape is the most forgiving layout for a small room. It still has one weak spot and a few easy traps. Below is our full plan, with the measurements, the work zones, the corner fixes and the point where a U-shape makes more sense.

**The short version:** Put the sink on one leg, the stove on the other and the fridge at the open end of a leg. Keep both the sink and the stove out of the corner, and leave a 42-inch aisle in front of the counters. Fit the corner base cabinet with a lazy Susan or a pull-out, and use the open side for a peninsula, a cart or a small table instead of a full island. Switch to a U-shape only if the room is at least 8 feet wide and the third wall is free.

## Is an L-Shaped Layout Good for a Small Kitchen?

Yes. For most small, square-ish rooms, the L is the layout we recommend first. An L-shaped kitchen runs cabinets and counters along two walls that meet at a corner. The other two sides stay open.

That open side is the big win. It gives you:

- **Short walks.** The sink, stove and fridge sit within a few steps of each other.
- **Room for two.** One person can prep on one leg while the other cooks on the other.
- **Flexible floor.** You can add a peninsula, a cart or a small table later.
- **An open feel.** The kitchen flows into the next room instead of boxing you in.

The L is the wrong choice in two cases. In a long, narrow room under about 7 feet wide, a galley gives you more counter per foot of floor. Our [galley kitchen ideas](/blog/galley-kitchen-ideas/) cover that shape. If you only have one usable wall, as in many studios, read our [one-wall kitchen ideas](/blog/one-wall-kitchen-ideas/) instead. For a side-by-side look at all four shapes, start with our [small kitchen layout ideas](/blog/small-kitchen-layout-ideas/).

## What's the Minimum Size for an L-Shaped Kitchen?

Plan on a room about 7 by 7 feet, with each leg at least 6 to 7 feet long. Smaller than that, and the corner and the appliances eat almost all of your counter.

Here are the numbers that shape every small L:

| Element | Measurement | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Room size | About 7 x 7 ft or more | Leaves a working floor in front of both legs |
| Leg length | 6 to 7 ft minimum each | The corner uses about 2 ft of each leg |
| Counter height and depth | 36 in high, 24 to 25 in deep | Fits standard cabinets and appliances |
| Upper cabinets | 12 in deep, about 18 in above the counter | Room for small appliances below |
| Work aisle | 42 in for one cook, 48 in for two | You can open the oven and still stand in front of it |
| Walkway only | 36 in minimum | Fine for walking past, not for working |
| Landing space | 15 in by the fridge and on one side of the stove, 24 in by the sink | A place to set down hot pans and groceries |
| Main prep run | 36 in wide, clear | Fits a cutting board and a bowl |

### Do the Corner Math First

The corner is a 24-by-24-inch square that belongs to both legs. So subtract 2 feet from each leg before you plan anything.

Here is a worked example. Take a 7-foot leg, which is 84 inches. Subtract 24 inches for the corner, and you have 60 inches left. Put a 30-inch range on it, and only 30 inches of counter remain. That is enough for landing space on one side of the stove, but not for a full prep run.

This is why leg length matters more than room size. If one leg is short, put only one appliance on it, or none at all.

## Small L-Shaped Kitchen Layout: Where the Sink, Stove and Fridge Go

Our rule for a small L-shaped kitchen layout is simple. Put the sink on one leg, the stove on the other, and the fridge at the open end of a leg. This keeps the work triangle tight and keeps people walking through the room out of it.

The work triangle is the imaginary line that joins the sink, the stove and the fridge. Each side should be 4 to 9 feet long, and the three sides together should total 13 to 26 feet. In a small L, the sides are rarely too long. The usual problem is a sink and stove crammed together with no counter between them.

| Your room | Sink | Stove | Fridge |
|---|---|---|---|
| Window on one wall | Under the window | Middle of the other leg | Open end of the stove leg |
| One long leg, one short leg | Short leg | Long leg, with counter on both sides | Open end of the long leg |
| Door near one leg | Leg away from the door | Other leg | End nearest the door, so people grab drinks without crossing your path |
| Two cooks | Middle of one leg | Middle of the other leg | Open end of either leg |

### Keep Both Wet and Hot Out of the Corner

Do not put the sink or the stove tight against the corner. Two people at the corner bump elbows, pot handles hit the other leg, and the corner counter turns into a dumping ground.

Leave at least 15 inches of counter between the corner and the stove. Leave at least 24 inches between the corner and the sink. That counter becomes your landing space.

### Place the Fridge So the Door Helps You

A fridge at the end of a leg should open toward the counter. Set the hinge on the side away from the counter. Then you can turn from the open fridge and set things straight down on the 15-inch landing spot beside it.

### Put the Dishwasher Beside the Sink, Not the Corner

Keep the dishwasher right next to the sink, on the side away from the corner if you can. A dishwasher tight against the corner blocks the corner cabinet door when it is open. If space is tight, an 18-inch model such as the [Midea 18-Inch Built-In Dishwasher](https://www.amazon.com/Midea-MDF18A1AST-Dishwasher-Installation-Compression/dp/B0CYC85BBS/?tag=kitchenpot-20) gives back 6 inches of base cabinet compared with a standard 24-inch one.

## Plan Your Work Zones Along the L

Think of the L as one long counter that bends. Then split it into zones that follow the order you cook in: storage, prep, cooking, cleanup.

1. **Storage zone at the open end.** The fridge and your food cabinets go here, near the door.
2. **Prep zone across the corner.** The counter between the sink and the stove is your main chopping spot. In a small L, that run usually wraps around the corner.
3. **Cooking zone on the stove leg.** Pots, pans, oils and spatulas live within arm's reach of the burners.
4. **Cleanup zone on the sink leg.** Sink, dishwasher, trash and the dish rack stay together.

### Prep at the Front of the Corner

The front edge of the corner counter is a fine place to chop. Only the deep back of the corner is hard to reach. Set your cutting board at the front and keep the back for storage, as we explain below.

Every L has three corners stacked on top of each other: the base cabinet, the counter and the upper cabinet. Each needs a different fix. We keep this short, because the rule is the same for all three: bring the back of the corner out to you.

## Small L-Shaped Kitchen Design With a Window

Many small L-shaped kitchens have a window on one leg. That is good news. A window brings free light and makes the room feel bigger. It also changes the plan.

### Put the Sink Under the Window

The sink is the best fit under a window. You stand there often, and a sink needs no upper cabinets or hood above it. The stove should never go under a window, since curtains and drafts do not mix with flames.

Check two things before you set the sink there. Make sure the faucet clears the window when it opens. Then make sure the window frame leaves room for a faucet with a pull-down spray.

### Make Up for the Lost Upper Cabinets

The window wall loses upper cabinets. Win that storage back on the other leg, with uppers that run all the way to the ceiling. Store the things you use least on the top shelf.

Keep the window clear on the sink side. A tall dish rack blocks the light, so pick a low one. The [OXO Good Grips Extendable Over-the-Sink Aluminum Dish Rack](https://www.amazon.com/OXO-Good-Grips-Extendable-Over-Sink-Aluminum-Dish-Rack/dp/B09C7YML48/?tag=kitchenpot-20) stretches across the sink and lets drips fall straight into the basin. It keeps your counter free and leaves the window open. An [over-the-sink cutting board](/blog/best-over-the-sink-cutting-board/) adds a second prep surface in the same spot.

If you are replacing the sink, a single large bowl does more in a small L than two small bowls. Our guide to the [best white undermount kitchen sink](/blog/best-white-undermount-kitchen-sink/) covers sizes that fit a standard base cabinet. A light sink under a bright window also helps [make a small kitchen look bigger](/blog/make-a-small-kitchen-look-bigger/).

### Window at the End of a Leg

If the window sits at the end of a leg instead, keep tall things away from it. Do not put the fridge or a tall pantry cabinet right beside it. Put the fridge at the other end, so daylight runs down the whole counter.

## Can a Small L-Shaped Kitchen Have an Island?

Only if the room is bigger than it looks. An island needs about 9 feet of clear floor in front of the counter. That breaks down into a 42-inch work aisle, a 24-inch island and 36 to 42 inches of walkway behind it.

Add the 2-foot counter, and you need about 11 feet from the back wall of that leg to the opposite wall. Most small L-shaped kitchens do not have that. If you squeeze an island in anyway, every drawer and door hits it.

Here is what fits instead:

| Option | Clear floor needed | Seating | Renter-safe | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Island | About 9 ft in front of the counter | Yes | Only if freestanding | Larger L-shapes with a wide open side |
| Peninsula | 42 in aisle around it | Yes, on the outside | No, it is built in | Owners who want seats and counter |
| Rolling cart | About 7 to 8 ft | Small drop leaf only | Yes | Most small L-shapes and rentals |
| Wall table or gate-leg table | About 3 ft behind the chairs | Yes | Gate-leg yes, wall-mounted needs drilling | Eating more than prepping |

### A Peninsula Is the Small-Kitchen Island

A peninsula is a short counter that sticks out from the end of one leg. It turns the L into a near-U, with one side still open. It needs clearance on fewer sides than an island, adds a counter and base cabinets, and gives you seats on the outside.

Keep the aisle between the peninsula and the opposite leg at 42 inches. Run the peninsula no longer than you need for a prep run and two stools, usually about 4 to 5 feet.

### A Cart Is the Renter's Island

A rolling cart gives you a small island that moves out of the way. ## Small L-Shaped Kitchen Ideas for Renters

You cannot move the sink in a rental, but you can change almost everything around it.

1. **Fix the corner without tools.** Put a turntable in the base corner and a tiered shelf on the corner counter.
2. **Add an island that rolls.** A cart with locking wheels adds counter and storage, and it moves with you.
3. **Light both legs.** Plug-in bars go up with screws or adhesive and come down at move-out.
4. **Use the ends of the legs.** Adhesive hooks hold towels, mitts and a small broom.
5. **Fold your table.** A gate-leg table gives you a dining spot only when you need it.

## L-Shaped vs U-Shaped: Which Suits a Small Kitchen?

A U-shape adds counters on a third wall. It gives you the most counter and storage of any layout. In a small room, it can also feel like a box.

The math is the deciding factor. Two 24-inch counters plus a 42-inch aisle add up to 90 inches. So a U needs at least 7.5 feet from wall to wall, and we plan for about 8 feet.

| Question | Stay with an L | Switch to a U |
|---|---|---|
| Room width | Under about 8 ft | 8 ft or more |
| Third wall | Has a door, window or walkway | Clear and usable |
| Number of cooks | Two | One main cook |
| Seating | Needed in the kitchen | Can go elsewhere |
| Open to a living area | Yes | No, or a short peninsula is enough |
| Corner cabinets | One | Two |

### When to Make the Switch

Switch to a U-shape when three things are true. The room is at least 8 feet wide. The third wall has no door or window in the way. And you are short on counter, not floor. If only one of those is true, stay with the L and add a peninsula or a cart instead.

### Small U-Shaped Kitchen Ideas

If you do go U-shaped, plan both corners before you buy anything. Lose them, and the U gives you little more storage than an L. For a narrow corner door, the [Rev-A-Shelf 31 Inch Half Moon 2-Shelf Pullout](https://www.amazon.com/Rev-A-Shelf-Half-Moon-Blind-Corner-Shelves/dp/B0009EJXHI/?tag=kitchenpot-20) fits openings from 13.25 inches. Its two shelves swing and slide out to you.

Put the sink at the bottom of the U, with the stove and fridge on the two sides. Do not put the dishwasher right across from the oven, or the two open doors will block the aisle. Keep the third wall lighter, with open shelves or shorter uppers, so the room does not close in. ## Common Small L-Shaped Kitchen Mistakes

- **Putting the sink or stove in the corner.** Two people collide and the corner counter fills with clutter. Keep 15 to 24 inches of counter between the corner and each one.
- **Ignoring the corner math.** Each leg loses 2 feet to the corner. Plan appliances on what is left.
- **Forcing an island.** Without about 9 feet of clear floor, it blocks every door. Use a peninsula or a cart.
- **Leaving the corner cabinet empty.** Fit a lazy Susan or a pull-out, or at least a turntable.
- **Running heat appliances under the uppers.** Keep toaster ovens and air fryers on open counter, with air around them.
- **Fridge door opening the wrong way.** Hinge it on the side away from the counter.
- **Lighting only the ceiling.** The corner stays dark. Run under-cabinet light into it.
- **Blocking the window.** Keep tall racks and tall appliances away from it.
