The first time you use a tortilla press, it feels like magic. You drop a little ball of dough in, pull the handle down, and out comes a perfect round tortilla in two seconds. The second time, the dough cracks at the edges, sticks to the plastic, and tears when you try to peel it off.

The press itself is almost never the problem. Nearly every tortilla fail comes down to three things: how wet the dough is, how hard you press, and how hot your pan is. Get those right and homemade corn tortillas taste far better than anything in a plastic bag from the store.

This guide shows you how to use a tortilla press from start to finish, including how to mix the masa, how to line and press, how to cook tortillas so they puff, and how to fix the problems everyone runs into at first.

> **Quick answer:** Mix masa harina with warm water until it feels like soft Play-Doh, then let it rest 10 to 20 minutes. Roll it into balls about the size of a golf ball, place one slightly toward the hinge side of a press lined with two sheets of plastic, and press gently to about 1/8 inch thick. Peel off the plastic and cook the tortilla on a hot, dry skillet for about 30 seconds per side, flipping it twice until it puffs.

## What Is a Tortilla Press?

A tortilla press is two flat, round plates joined by a hinge, with a lever handle that pushes the top plate down on the bottom one. You put a ball of dough between the plates, press, and the dough flattens into an even circle.

It's built for corn tortillas, which are made from masa, a dough of corn that has been cooked in an alkaline solution and ground. Masa has no gluten, so it doesn't stretch like wheat dough. That's why it's hard to roll out neatly with a pin but flattens beautifully under steady pressure.

Tortilla presses usually come in three materials: cast iron, cast aluminum and wood. They're also sold in different plate sizes. Victoria, a Colombian maker of cast iron cookware, sells presses in 6.5-inch, 8-inch and 10-inch sizes. An 8-inch press is the most versatile for home kitchens, since it handles standard taco-size tortillas with room to spare.

Beyond tortillas, a press is handy for flattening arepas, empanada dough, patacones (smashed green plantains) and small flatbreads.

## How to Make Masa Dough for a Tortilla Press

Good dough is most of the job. Recipes vary a little, but they all land in the same place: roughly equal volumes of masa harina and water, give or take.

Here are three reliable starting ratios from trusted sources:

- **Maseca's package recipe:** 2 cups of masa harina and 1 1/2 cups of water, kneaded for about 2 minutes. It makes about 19 tortillas from 1-ounce balls.
- **Rick Bayless:** 1 3/4 cups (7.5 ounces) of masa harina and 1 cup plus 2 tablespoons of hot tap water, rested 15 minutes, for 15 tortillas.
- **Masienda (by weight):** 120 grams of masa harina to 200 grams of warm water, with an optional 1/2 teaspoon of salt.

Different brands absorb water differently, which is why no single ratio is exact. Use the numbers to start, then go by feel.

### How to mix it

1. Put the masa harina and salt in a bowl.
2. Pour in most of the warm water and mix with your hand.
3. Knead for 2 to 3 minutes until smooth and evenly moist. Masienda suggests about 3 minutes of mixing so the water absorbs evenly.
4. Cover the bowl with a damp towel and let it rest for 10 to 20 minutes so the masa fully hydrates.
5. Check the texture. It should feel like soft cookie dough or fresh Play-Doh: moist, not sticky, and not crumbly.

### The squish test

America's Test Kitchen suggests a simple check: flatten a golf ball-size piece of dough between your palms. If lots of big cracks form around the edge, the dough needs more water. Knead in warm water 1 tablespoon at a time and test again. If the dough sticks to your hands, add a little more masa harina.

Dough can dry out as you work, especially in a warm kitchen. Re-test halfway through your batch and knead in a splash more water if the edges start cracking again.

## How to Use a Tortilla Press Step by Step

Once your dough is ready, set up the press next to the stove so you can press and cook in one smooth rhythm.

### 1. Heat the pan first

Put your skillet or comal on the stove over medium-high heat before you press anything. Masienda recommends a cooking surface of about 400 to 500°F, which is medium-high to high on most stoves. A properly hot pan is what makes tortillas puff.

Rick Bayless takes it a step further and uses two heat zones: one skillet (or one end of a long griddle) on medium, and a second on medium-high. The tortilla starts on the cooler side and finishes on the hotter one.

### 2. Portion the dough into balls

Roll the dough into balls about the size of a golf ball or a ping-pong ball. Masienda uses about 40 grams per ball, America's Test Kitchen uses about 1 1/2 ounces, and Victoria suggests balls about 1 1/4 inches across for its 8-inch press. Keep the balls under a damp towel while you work.

### 3. Line the press

Cut the side seams off a heavy zip-top freezer bag, leaving the bottom fold intact, so it opens like a book. Lay it on the bottom plate with the fold at the hinge. Thicker freezer bags peel away from the dough more cleanly than thin sandwich bags.

### 4. Place the dough slightly toward the hinge

Put a ball on the plastic just off center, a little closer to the hinge than the handle, which is where Victoria's instructions say to place it. Starting there tends to give you a rounder tortilla. Fold the top sheet of plastic over the ball.

### 5. Press gently

Close the top plate and pull the handle down with steady, moderate pressure. You don't need to lean your whole body on it. Rick Bayless says to press just enough to make a disc about 1/8 inch thick and about 5 inches across.

For more even thickness, Masienda suggests pressing once, then turning the tortilla 180 degrees in its plastic and pressing again.

### 6. Peel it off

Open the press and lift out the tortilla in its plastic. Peel off the top sheet. Flip the tortilla onto your fingers, dough side down, and slowly peel the other sheet away from the tortilla, rather than pulling the tortilla off the plastic. Bayless recommends practicing this step on the counter a few times before you do it next to a hot pan.

### 7. Cook it with two flips

Lay the tortilla flat on the hot pan. Then:

1. **First side, about 30 seconds.** Cook until the edges look dry and the tortilla releases from the pan on its own. Don't let it go too long, or it dries out.
2. **Flip and cook the second side, about 30 seconds**, until you see browned spots underneath.
3. **Flip back to the first side for another 30 seconds or so.** This is when it should puff. If you're lucky, it balloons up like pita bread.

If it only puffs a little, gently press around the edge with a folded, damp towel or your fingertips. Masienda says pressing around the rim helps push the steam toward the center.

### 8. Keep them warm

Stack the cooked tortillas in a towel-lined basket or a tortilla warmer. They'll steam each other and turn soft and flexible as they sit. America's Test Kitchen wraps its hot tortillas in a damp dish towel for the same reason.

## Tortilla Press Cooking Times at a Glance

Here's how the timing compares across the recipes above, so you can see the range.

| Source | Ball size | Pressed size | Pan heat | Cook time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Maseca | About 1 ounce | 5 inches | Medium-high | 30 seconds per side, turned 3 times |
| Rick Bayless | 1/15 of the batch | 5 inches, 1/8 inch thick | Medium, then medium-high | About 30 seconds for each of 3 turns |

| America's Test Kitchen | About 1 1/2 ounces | 5 1/2 inches | Medium, until just smoking | About 1 minute, 45 seconds, then 15 seconds |

The takeaway: small balls, 1/8-inch thick, a hot dry pan, and roughly a minute and a half of total cooking with two flips.

## Tortilla Press Problems and How to Fix Them

Almost every beginner runs into at least one of these. The fix is usually a small change to the dough or the heat.

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Edges crack or split | Dough too dry | Knead in warm water 1 tablespoon at a time |
| Dough sticks to the plastic | Dough too wet, or thin plastic | Add a little masa harina, switch to a heavy freezer bag |
| Tortilla tears when peeling | Pressed too thin | Press more gently or stop partway |
| Tortilla is lopsided or oval | Ball placed in the center or on the handle side | Place the ball slightly toward the hinge, press, rotate, press again |
| Tortilla doesn't puff | Pan not hot enough, dough dry, or overcooked first side | Heat the pan longer, rehydrate dough, shorten the first side |
| Tortilla is stiff and dry | Cooked too long or left uncovered | Cook less, stack in a towel right away |
| Tortilla sticks to the pan | Pan not hot enough or not seasoned | Preheat longer, use well-seasoned cast iron or nonstick |

If you're getting more than one of these at once, start with the dough. Most issues vanish when the masa has the right moisture.

## Can You Use a Tortilla Press for Flour Tortillas?

You can, but it's less forgiving. Flour tortillas have gluten, which is elastic. Press a ball of flour dough and it often springs back into a smaller, thicker circle. That's why Tasting Table recommends a rolling pin for flour tortillas, which stretches the gluten gradually.

If you want to try the press anyway, rest time is the secret. Masienda's flour tortilla method lets the dough rest 30 minutes after mixing, then another 15 minutes once it's portioned into balls. It presses each ball with full pressure, flips the lined tortilla over, and presses again. The tortillas cook fast on a medium-high griddle, about 15 to 30 seconds per side, until bubbles form and light brown spots appear.

A common compromise: press the flour dough to start the circle, then finish it with a few passes of a rolling pin.

### What about electric tortilla presses?

Electric tortilla makers press and cook at the same time between two heated plates, a bit like a flat [panini press](/blog/best-panini-press-for-home-use/). They're mostly used for flour tortillas. They're quick, but the tortillas tend to come out flatter and less puffy than ones cooked on a comal. For corn tortillas, a manual press and a hot skillet give you more control.

## How to Clean and Care for a Tortilla Press

Because the dough sits on plastic, a press rarely gets very dirty. Care depends on what it's made of.

- **Cast iron.** Victoria's press comes pre-seasoned with flaxseed oil. The company says to wipe it with a dry or oiled cloth after each use. For heavier soiling, hand wash it with warm water and a soft brush, dry it right away, and rub on a thin layer of cooking oil, wiping off the excess. Store it somewhere dry.
- **Cast aluminum.** Wash by hand with warm, soapy water and dry it. Aluminum won't rust, so it's lower maintenance than cast iron.
- **Wood.** Wipe with a damp cloth and dry immediately. Don't soak it, since standing water can make wood swell or crack.

Wipe down your plastic liner between batches if dough builds up. You can reuse the same bag several times if you rinse and dry it.

## Making Tortillas When You Cook for One

You don't need to make 19 tortillas at a time. Half or quarter the recipe, and use the Masienda ratio by weight if you have a kitchen scale, since it scales down cleanly. One cup (120 grams) of masa harina with 200 grams of water makes about eight 40-gram balls, a comfortable batch for one or two people.

Masienda says leftover dough keeps in an airtight container in the fridge for a day or two, so you can press a few fresh tortillas at breakfast and a few more at dinner. ## The Bottom Line on Using a Tortilla Press

Learning how to use a tortilla press takes one or two batches, not years. Mix the masa until it feels like soft Play-Doh, rest it, press small balls gently to about 1/8 inch between plastic, and cook them on a hot, dry pan with two flips. When the edges crack, add water. When they stick, add masa. When they won't puff, turn up the heat.

Once it clicks, a press turns a bag of masa harina into warm, fresh tortillas in about 20 minutes.
