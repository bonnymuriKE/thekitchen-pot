If you live in an apartment, condo, or a house with a tiny outdoor footprint, you've probably assumed real grilling wasn't in the cards for you. It is. You just need to shop differently than someone with a big backyard and a six-burner gas monster.

The best small grills for balconies and patios aren't scaled-down versions of full-size grills. They're built from the ground up for tight footprints, quick cleanup, and, in a lot of cases, rules that don't allow an open flame at all. Getting this right means understanding not just size, but grill type, because that's what determines whether you're even allowed to use it where you live.

Here's how to think through the decision, plus what to actually buy for your specific situation.

## Start With Your Building's Rules, Not the Grill

Before you fall in love with a specific style of grill, find out what you're actually allowed to use. This is the single most important step, and skipping it is how people end up buying a grill they can't legally use on their own balcony.

Check three things:

* **Your lease or condo bylaws.** Many explicitly ban propane tanks and charcoal on balconies.
* **Your local fire code.** Cities regularly restrict open-flame cooking devices above a certain floor or within a certain distance of a building.
* **Your building's specific balcony construction.** Combustible balconies (wood) are treated more strictly than concrete ones in many fire codes.

If you're on a ground-floor patio with no shared walls above you, you likely have more freedom. If you're several floors up on a balcony attached to a multi-unit building, electric is often your only legal option. We cover this in much more depth in our full safety guide, but it's worth repeating here because it changes which grill type you should even be shopping for.

## The Three Grill Types for Small Outdoor Spaces

### 1. Electric Grills

Electric grills are the most balcony-friendly option that exists, because there's no open flame, no gas tank, and no charcoal to dispose of. You plug it in, it heats up, and you cook. This is why they're so often the only type explicitly allowed in apartment complexes and condo associations.

**Renter-friendly:** yes, no venting or gas line needed, and most plug into a standard outdoor-rated outlet.

**What to get:** a compact electric grill, usually $60-$150, with a nonstick or cast-textured cooking plate and a drip tray. Look for one rated at 1,500 watts or higher for real searing heat, and check that it's labeled for outdoor or covered-patio use.

### 2. Portable Gas Grills

Small propane grills, often using a 1-pound disposable canister instead of a full tank, give you that classic grilled taste and instant heat control. They're a great fit if your building allows gas, or if you have a patio or ground-floor space where a full tank is permitted.

**Renter-friendly:** usually yes as furniture, but check whether propane specifically is banned even if the grill itself would be fine.

**What to get:** a tabletop or small-cart propane grill, typically $80-$250, with a single or double burner. Look for one that uses the small screw-on propane canisters if you don't want to store a full tank, since those are easier to store in a tight space.

### 3. Compact Charcoal Grills

Nothing beats charcoal for smoky flavor, but it's also the type most likely to be restricted on balconies, since it involves open flame, embers, and ash disposal. If you're allowed to use one, a small kettle-style or portable charcoal grill can deliver serious flavor in a tiny footprint.

**Renter-friendly:** only if your building allows charcoal specifically. Many do not.

**What to get:** a compact kettle grill or portable box-style charcoal grill, generally $40-$120. Choose one with a lid that closes fully and vents you can adjust, since that gives you more control over heat in a small space.

## What to Look for Beyond the Fuel Type

Once you know which fuel type fits your situation, a few other features matter more in small spaces than they would in a backyard.

**Footprint and folding legs.** Look for a grill with a compact footprint, ideally under 2 feet in any direction, and legs or a stand that fold flat. This matters if you need to store the grill against a wall or in a closet between uses, which is common on balconies that double as the only outdoor space you have.

**Weight.** If you're carrying the grill up and down stairs, or moving it in and out of storage every time you use it, weight matters more than you'd think. A lightweight tabletop model is much easier to live with day to day than a heavier cart-style unit, even if the cart looks nicer.

**Heat-safe surfaces underneath.** Some compact grills run hotter on the bottom than you'd expect, which matters on a wood balcony or near vinyl siding. Look for models with raised feet or a heat shield on the base, and plan to use a grill mat underneath regardless (more on that in our accessories guide).

**Easy-to-empty drip trays or ash pans.** In a small space, you're not walking the grill out to a big trash can outside. You want a tray that pops out cleanly and doesn't spill on the way to the kitchen sink or trash.

**Storage cover availability.** Since balcony grills often live outside full-time, a fitted cover that keeps rain and pollen off matters more here than on a grill tucked into a garage.

## Matching Grill Size to Your Space

Sizing a grill for a small space isn't just about what fits physically, it's about what still leaves you room to actually stand, move, and set things down while you cook. A grill that technically fits against the railing but leaves no room to open its lid, or forces you to reach over a hot surface to grab a plate, isn't sized correctly even if the tape measure says otherwise. Walk through your actual cooking motions, not just the footprint, before you settle on a size.

**Under 15 square feet (a narrow balcony):** Stick with a small tabletop electric or single-burner propane grill. Anything with a full stand or cart will make the space unusable for anything else.

**15 to 40 square feet (a standard apartment balcony or small patio):** A tabletop grill on a stand, or a compact cart-style gas grill, works well. You have enough room to also fit a small side table or a couple of chairs.

**40+ square feet (a real patio):** You have more flexibility here. A small two-burner gas grill or a mid-size kettle charcoal grill (if allowed) gives you more cooking capacity without overwhelming the space.

## How Many People You're Actually Cooking For

It's easy to overbuy on grill size "just in case." In reality, most small-space grillers are cooking for one to four people on a regular weeknight, with bigger cookouts being the rare exception. Be honest with yourself about how often you'd actually use the extra capacity. A grill that's twice as big as you need doesn't just cost more upfront, it also takes longer to preheat, uses more fuel per cook, and eats into the exact storage space you were trying to protect by going small in the first place.

* **Cooking for 1-2:** A single-burner or small tabletop grill (roughly 150-250 square inches of cooking surface) is plenty.
* **Cooking for 3-4:** Look for 250-350 square inches of grilling surface, still very achievable in a compact, single-burner or double-burner unit.
* **Occasional larger gatherings:** Rather than buying a bigger grill you rarely need, consider cooking in batches, or borrowing/renting a larger grill for the rare big event. It's cheaper and takes up far less space the rest of the year.

## Ventilation and Smoke Considerations

Even where grilling is allowed, smoke can be its own problem in close quarters, especially if your balcony sits close to a neighbor's window or a shared air intake. Electric grills produce the least smoke by far, since there's no combustion happening. Gas produces some, mostly from food drippings hitting the burner. Charcoal produces the most, along with the smell that lingers longest on fabric and furniture.

If you've gotten noise complaints or comments from neighbors about smoke before, this alone might tip your decision toward electric, independent of what your lease technically allows.

## Cost Beyond the Purchase Price

The sticker price on a small grill is only part of what you'll spend on it over time, and small-space grills have their own cost patterns worth knowing before you buy.

Electric grills have the lowest ongoing cost. There's no fuel to buy, just a small bump in your electric bill that's barely noticeable for occasional use. The main future cost is eventually replacing the unit if the heating element wears out, which tends to happen after a few years of regular use.

Propane grills using small disposable canisters are convenient but not the cheapest way to buy fuel over time. Those 1-pound canisters cost more per ounce of propane than a full 20-pound tank would, so if you grill several times a week, the convenience comes at a real premium. If your building allows a standard tank and you have somewhere to store it, that's the more economical route for frequent grilling.

Charcoal has the most variable ongoing cost, since bag quality and burn efficiency differ a lot between brands. It also has hidden costs in time, since lighting and waiting for charcoal to reach cooking temperature takes meaningfully longer than turning a dial on gas or electric.

## Assembly and Ignition: What Actually Makes a Grill Easy to Live With

Two grills with nearly identical specs can feel completely different to use day to day, and most of that comes down to two things that rarely show up in a headline product description: how long assembly takes and how the grill starts up.

**Assembly.** Compact grills range from fully assembled out of the box to requiring 30-60 minutes with a screwdriver. If you're renting and might move within a year or two, factor this in twice, since you may be assembling it again after a move if it doesn't fold flat for transport. Read a few reviews specifically looking for complaints about assembly before you buy, since this detail is often buried past the star rating.

**Ignition.** For gas grills, a built-in push-button or twist igniter is worth prioritizing over a model that requires a separate lighter or matches, especially if you'll be lighting it one-handed while holding a plate or standing in wind on an upper floor. For charcoal, a simple built-in vent system that lets you control airflow without extra tools makes lighting and temperature control noticeably less fiddly.

Neither of these affects how the food turns out, but they affect whether you actually use the grill on a random Tuesday versus saving it for special occasions, which matters more for getting your money's worth out of a small-space purchase.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

* **Buying based on looks instead of your building's rules.** A beautiful charcoal grill that violates your lease is a grill you can't use, or one you'll be asked to remove.
* **Ignoring clearance requirements.** Even an allowed grill needs open space around it. Don't wedge it into a corner against a wall or under an overhang.
* **Choosing the biggest grill you can afford.** In a small space, a grill that's too big becomes clutter you have to work around every single day, not just when you're cooking.
* **Skipping a cover.** Grills left outside full-time without a cover degrade fast, especially electric models with exposed cords and connections.
* **Forgetting about storage between uses.** If you can't easily fold or move the grill, it'll end up permanently taking over your only outdoor seating space.

## **The Bottom Line**

The best small grill for your balcony or patio is the one that actually matches your building's rules, your available space, and how many people you typically cook for. For most apartment and condo dwellers, that means starting with an electric grill and only considering gas or charcoal once you've confirmed they're allowed. Get that part right first, and picking the specific grill becomes a much easier decision.
