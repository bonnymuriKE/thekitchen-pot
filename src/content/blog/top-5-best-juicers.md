A juicer rarely gets abandoned because the juice was bad. It gets abandoned because of the ten minutes of scrubbing afterwards.

So cleaning counts here as much as yield does. So do noise, and how much chopping the machine makes you do before anything goes in.

The first real choice is centrifugal or masticating. Everything else follows from it, which is why this guide to the best juicers starts there rather than with a list.

> **Quick answer:** If you want juice in two minutes and you mostly juice apples, carrots and oranges, buy a centrifugal machine like the [Breville Juice Fountain Cold](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B016J1ICDU/?tag=kitchenpot-20). If you juice kale, celery or wheatgrass, buy a masticating one like the [Omega NC900HDC](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00CIU92S6/?tag=kitchenpot-20). Greens are the line that separates the two types.

## How a Juicer Actually Gets the Juice Out

There are only two mechanisms in home juicing, and they behave nothing alike.

A **centrifugal juicer** spins a flat cutting disc at high speed. The disc shreds produce into wet pulp, then throws that pulp outward against a fine mesh basket. Spinning force pushes juice through the mesh and flings the pulp into a bin. It is quick, and it is loud, because a motor is doing all the work in a few seconds.

Speed has a cost. The shredding whips air into the juice, so you get a thick head of foam. Air plus friction heat starts oxidation, which is why centrifugal juice browns and separates within hours. Breville's answer on the Juice Fountain Cold is a slower cutting disc and a cooling design, and the company states the process transfers less than 1.8°F to the juice.

A **masticating juicer** does the opposite. A slow auger, turning at about 50 to 80 revolutions a minute, crushes produce against a screen and presses the pulp dry. There is almost no whipping and very little heat, so the juice comes out denser, with less foam and a longer life in the fridge. These are the machines sold as slow juicers or cold press juicers.

The trade-off is time at both ends. A narrow chute means more chopping before you start, and the slow auger means more waiting while it runs. Then there are more parts to rinse. A **citrus press** is a third, simpler thing: a spinning cone that only reams halved citrus, and nothing else.

| | Centrifugal | Masticating | Citrus press |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Speed of the part that cuts | Very fast disc | 50 to 80 RPM auger | Slow reaming cone |
| Foam in the glass | Lots | Very little | Some pulp, little foam |
| Leafy greens | Poor | Very good | Not applicable |
| Hard produce | Very good | Good | Not applicable |
| Juice keeps | Same day | Up to about 72 hours | Same day |
| Noise | Loud | Quiet | Quiet |
| Prep chopping | Little, wide chutes | More, narrow chutes | Halve the fruit |
| Cleaning | Fewer parts, fine mesh | More parts, coarser screen | Easiest |

If you are still deciding between juicing and blending, the split is simple. A juicer removes fiber and gives you liquid, while a blender keeps everything and gives you a thick drink. ## Yield, Foam and How Long the Juice Keeps

Three things happen to juice the moment it leaves the produce, and the machine decides how fast.

**Foam** comes from air whipped in by a fast disc. A centrifugal juicer makes a thick head that you either skim or drink. A masticating auger turns too slowly to whip much air, so the glass looks clearer and settles less.

**Oxidation** follows the air in. Oxygen and a little friction heat start breaking down color and flavor, which is why centrifugal juice separates into layers and dulls within hours. Slow juicing delays that. Sage, Breville's sister brand, puts properly stored masticating juice at up to 72 hours, and Breville rates its own sealed Cold Spin jug at up to three days.

**Yield** is where the two types separate hardest, and it depends entirely on what you feed them. Hard, watery produce such as apples, carrots, beets and cucumber juices well either way. Fibrous produce is a different story, and the table below shows where the gap opens.

| Produce | Centrifugal | Masticating | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Apples, pears | Very good | Very good | Little reason to pay more |
| Carrots, beets | Very good | Very good | Both leave fairly dry pulp |
| Cucumber, celery | Fair | Very good | Long fibers wrap a spinning disc |
| Kale, spinach, chard | Poor | Very good | Leaves slip past the mesh |
| Wheatgrass, herbs | Very poor | Good | Needs crushing, not shredding |
| Soft berries, citrus | Fair | Good | A citrus press beats both on oranges |

However you juice, treat the result as a fresh food. The FDA points out that [fresh-squeezed juice is untreated](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/what-you-need-know-about-juice-safety) and needs refrigeration, and it advises extra caution for young children, older adults and anyone with a weakened immune system. Fill the bottle to the brim so there is no air on top, and keep it cold. ## Leafy Greens Are the Real Dividing Line

If you only remember one thing from this guide, remember this.

A centrifugal disc works by shredding and throwing. Leaves are light and flat, so they get flung across the mesh instead of pressed into it, and much of the leaf leaves the machine still wet. That is why a centrifugal juicer can turn a whole bag of spinach into an inch of juice.

A masticating auger works by squeezing. The leaf gets pinned between the auger and the screen and crushed until the liquid has nowhere to go but through. Celery is the clearest example, because its long fibers wrap around a spinning blade but flatten neatly under an auger.

So the question is not which juicer is better. It is what is going in the chute. Apples and carrots every morning means a centrifugal machine is enough. Celery, kale or wheatgrass means a masticating one, and our roundup of the [best commercial masticating juicers](/blog/best-commercial-masticating-juicer/) covers heavier options if you juice greens in volume.

## Feed Chutes and How Much Chopping You Save

Chute size is the spec that quietly decides how often you use the machine.

A 3-inch chute swallows a whole apple. Prep becomes washing the fruit and pulling the stalk, which takes about a minute for a jug of juice. A 1.5-inch chute means every apple gets quartered and every carrot halved lengthways, and that adds five to ten minutes each time.

Hurom's approach changes the maths again. Its 2-liter hopper with a 5.4-inch opening takes a whole batch at once, so the machine feeds itself while you do something else. You still halve large produce, but you are not standing there with a pusher.

If your juicer has a narrow chute, good prep tools pay for themselves. A sharp knife matters most, and a dull one is the real slowdown, which is why a [knife sharpener](/blog/best-knife-sharpener/) belongs in the drawer. A [large cutting board that sits over the sink](/blog/best-over-the-sink-cutting-board/) gives you somewhere to work in a small kitchen, and a [vegetable chopper](/blog/best-vegetable-choppers/) speeds up the repetitive cuts. For apples in particular, an [electric apple peeler and corer](/blog/best-electric-apple-peeler-corer-slicer/) turns a slow job into a quick one.

## Cleaning Is Why Juicers End Up in the Cupboard

Every juicer is easy to clean if you do it straight away. Every juicer is miserable to clean an hour later.

The reason is the mesh. Pulp dries into the holes of a filter basket and sets like glue, and once it has, you are picking at it with a brush. Rinse the parts within a minute or two of finishing and most of the work disappears.

| Machine | Parts to wash | The hard part | Dishwasher |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Breville BJE430SIL | Lid, disc, mesh basket, jug, pulp bin | Fine mesh basket | Some parts, check the manual |
| Omega NC900HDC | Auger, screen, drum, end cap, tray | Screen needs the brush | Removable parts, top rack |
| Hamilton Beach 67601A | Pusher, cover, strainer, bowl, pulp bin | Fine strainer | Yes, all five |
| Hurom H320N | Hopper, auger, strainer, brush, bowl | Most parts to handle | Check the manual |
| Breville 800CPXL | Cone, filter, dome | Almost nothing | Removable parts |

Three habits keep it short. Rinse every part under a running tap before you sit down with the juice. Use the brush that came in the box, because a sponge cannot reach into mesh. Let the parts air dry on a rack instead of towel-drying them, so nothing sits damp in a cupboard.

If the parts go in the dishwasher, put them on the top rack and run a normal cycle rather than an intensive one. A machine that is not cleaning well makes this worse, and our walkthrough on [how to clean a dishwasher](/blog/how-to-clean-a-dishwasher/) fixes the usual causes.

## How Loud Is Too Loud?

Noise is the spec nobody checks and everybody notices at 6am.

A centrifugal juicer is loud by design, because a motor is spinning a disc very fast under load. It is a short burst, usually under a minute, but it is closer to a blender than a kettle. If you share a wall or have a sleeping household, that matters.

A masticating juicer is much quieter. The auger turns at 50 to 80 RPM, so there is no whine, just a low grinding sound as produce is crushed. Hurom describes its AC motor as near-silent, and Omega markets the NC900HDC on its quiet motor too.

Power draw follows the same pattern. The centrifugal machines here pull 800 and 850 watts, while both masticating juicers use 150. Neither figure will move your bill much for two minutes a day.
