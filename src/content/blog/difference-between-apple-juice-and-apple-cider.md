Some brands sell the exact same bottle under both names. Martinelli's, the California company famous for its sparkling apple drinks, says on its FAQ page that its apple juice and apple cider are both 100% juice from fresh US apples, and it keeps the cider label only because some shoppers prefer the traditional name.

So if the names can mean the same thing, why does the orchard stand cider taste so different from the juice box in your kid's lunch?

**The short version:** in the US, apple cider is usually fresh-pressed apple juice that hasn't been filtered, so it's cloudy, tart and full-bodied, and it may or may not be pasteurized. Apple juice is filtered until clear, then pasteurized (and sometimes sweetened or fortified) so it lasts for months on a shelf. That's the core difference between apple juice and apple cider. Everything else, from taste to shelf life to how you cook with them, follows from those two steps.

| | Apple cider (US) | Apple juice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Filtered? | No, pulp and sediment stay in | Yes, strained and clarified until clear |
| Looks like | Cloudy, opaque, amber to brown | Clear, bright gold |
| Tastes like | Tart, earthy, tastes like raw apples | Sweeter, lighter, more uniform |
| Pasteurized? | Often, but raw cider is still sold | Almost always |
| Where you find it | Refrigerated case, orchards, farmers' markets, mostly in fall | Shelf-stable aisle, all year |
| Shelf life | Days to a couple of weeks, always refrigerated | Months unopened, 7 to 10 days once opened |
| Alcohol | None, unless labeled hard cider | None |

## How Each One Is Made

Both drinks start the same way. Apples are washed, ground into a coarse mash (cider makers call it pomace), and pressed to squeeze out the liquid. At that moment, what's in the bucket is fresh cider.

What happens next decides the name.

**For cider**, that's more or less the end of the road. The liquid is bottled with its fine bits of pulp and apple solids still floating in it. That sediment is why cider looks murky and why you're told to shake the jug. Many producers now pasteurize or UV-treat it before bottling, but they skip the filtering.

**For juice**, the liquid goes through more steps. Processors typically treat it with enzymes that break down pectin (the natural gel that keeps apple solids suspended), then filter or spin it until it runs clear. It's pasteurized with heat, sealed, and often sold in shelf-stable bottles or boxes. Some juices are made from concentrate, meaning the water was removed for shipping and added back later. Some have added vitamin C, sugar, or both, so read the label if you want plain 100% juice.

If you've ever made juice at home with a [masticating juicer](/blog/top-5-best-juicers/), what came out of the spout was much closer to cider than to store juice. It's cloudy, foamy and tastes like biting into an apple. Commercial juice plants use the same idea at a much bigger scale, which you can read about in our look at [commercial masticating juicers](/blog/best-commercial-masticating-juicer/).

## Is There a Legal Definition of Apple Cider?

Not at the federal level. The FDA treats cider as juice. Its juice safety rules apply to "the aqueous liquid expressed or extracted from one or more fruits," and cider falls right into that. There's no separate federal standard that says how cloudy or unprocessed a drink must be to call itself cider.

A few states have their own wording. Massachusetts agriculture officials describe fresh cider as raw apple juice that hasn't been filtered to remove coarse particles, while apple juice is filtered and pasteurized so it keeps longer. That's the everyday American meaning most people carry around.

Because the label isn't tightly regulated, you'll see a lot of overlap in stores. A jug in the fall produce section marked "cider" might be pasteurized and fairly clear. A bottle marked "unfiltered apple juice" might look exactly like cider. The name on the front tells you less than the words in smaller print: *filtered or unfiltered*, *pasteurized or not*, and *from concentrate or not*.

## The Safety Question: Pasteurized vs Unpasteurized Cider

This is the part that actually matters for your family, so it's worth slowing down.

Raw apple juice can carry harmful germs. Apples picked up off the ground, a dirty press, or contaminated water can introduce bacteria and parasites, and pressing spreads them through the whole batch. The pathogens the FDA's juice guidance singles out for apple cider are *E. coli* O157:H7 and *Salmonella*, and *Cryptosporidium* has also been linked to raw cider.

These aren't hypothetical risks:

- In 1996, unpasteurized apple juice from Odwalla was contaminated with *E. coli* O157:H7. Seventy people got sick, 14 developed a serious kidney complication, and a 16-month-old girl died.
- In fall 2004, an outbreak linked to unpasteurized cider in upstate New York sickened more than 300 people. It pushed New York to pass the country's first mandatory cider pasteurization law, which took effect in 2006 and allows UV treatment as an alternative to heat.
- In 2015, an orchard near Placerville, California, was linked to 13 confirmed *E. coli* infections from unpasteurized apple juice, and seven people in Northern California became ill from raw cider in 2017, according to Food Safety News.

### What the rules require now

After those early outbreaks, the FDA made two big changes. First, packaged juice that hasn't been pasteurized or otherwise treated must carry this warning:

> WARNING: This product has not been pasteurized and therefore may contain harmful bacteria that can cause serious illness in children, the elderly, and persons with weakened immune systems.

Second, the juice HACCP rule, published in 2001, requires juice processors to use a process that achieves at least a 100,000-fold (5-log) reduction in the most likely pathogen. Pasteurization does that, and so do some approved non-heat methods like UV light.

There's one important gap. Businesses that sell juice only directly to customers, such as a small cider mill or a farm stand, are exempt from the HACCP rule. That's why raw cider still shows up at orchards and farmers' markets. The FDA says untreated juice is also sold at grocery stores, health food stores and juice bars, and is most likely to be in the refrigerated section.

### Who should skip raw cider

The FDA's list of people at higher risk includes children, older adults, pregnant women, and anyone with a weakened immune system, such as transplant patients and people with HIV/AIDS, cancer or diabetes. For those groups, choose pasteurized or treated cider every time.

### How to make raw cider safer at home

If you've already bought an unpasteurized jug, you can heat-treat it yourself. The University of Maine Cooperative Extension says commercial cider in Maine must be heated to 155°F for 10 seconds. At home, the simplest approach is to pour it into a pot and bring it just to a gentle simmer, which is well above that, then cool it and refrigerate it. A [candy thermometer](/blog/7-best-candy-thermometer/) clipped to the side takes the guesswork out if you want to hit a number. A heavy [stockpot with a lid](/blog/best-stockpot-with-a-lid/) is handy for a full gallon.

Heat does change the flavor a little, making it taste slightly cooked. For hot mulled cider that doesn't matter at all.

## Which Is Healthier: Apple Juice or Apple Cider?

Less different than you might hope. Both are basically apple sugar water with some nutrients along for the ride.

USDA data for unsweetened bottled apple juice (without added vitamin C) lists, per 1-cup serving:

| Nutrient (per cup) | Unsweetened apple juice |
| --- | --- |
| Calories | 114 |
| Carbohydrates | 28 g |
| Total sugars | 23.9 g |
| Fiber | 0.5 g |
| Potassium | 251 mg |
| Vitamin C | 2.2 mg (unless fortified) |

Fresh cider lands in roughly the same place for calories and sugar, because the sugar is in the liquid, not the pulp. You won't get a meaningful fiber boost either. The bits floating in cider are tiny, and most of an apple's fiber stays behind in the pressed pomace.

Where cider does pull ahead is in plant compounds called polyphenols. In a 2007 study published in the *Journal of the Science of Food and Agriculture*, researchers at the Agricultural University of Wroclaw in Poland found cloudy apple juice had about four times the polyphenol concentration of clear juice, because clarifying strips out the pulp where many of those compounds live.

A few practical takeaways:

- **Check for added sugar.** Some apple juice drinks and "apple juice cocktails" are only part juice. Plain 100% juice or cider has no added sugar.
- **Portion matters more than the type.** Nearly 24 grams of sugar per cup adds up fast if you refill. Smaller glasses help.
- **A whole apple is still better.** You get the fiber and chew, which fills you up in a way juice doesn't.

If you like fresh juice but want to keep the fiber, blending instead of juicing keeps the whole fruit. Our guide to [blenders for smoothies](/blog/best-blenders-for-smoothies/) covers machines that handle whole apples. If you're weighing appliances, this explainer on [food processors vs blenders](/blog/what-is-the-difference-between-a-food-processor-and-a-blender/) shows which one handles fruit best.

## Hard Cider, Sparkling Cider and Cider Vinegar: Clearing Up the Names

Three other drinks borrow the word and cause a lot of confusion.

**Hard cider** is apple juice that's been fermented so the sugar turns into alcohol. In the US it's always labeled "hard." In the UK, Ireland, Australia and much of Europe, plain "cider" means this alcoholic drink, and what Americans call cider would just be cloudy apple juice. If a British recipe calls for a cup of cider, it means hard cider. Keep a few cans cold in a small [beverage fridge](/blog/best-mini-fridge-for-beer/) if you cook with it often.

**Sparkling cider** is non-alcoholic carbonated apple juice, the kind that comes in a champagne-style bottle for holidays. It's pasteurized and shelf-stable.

**Apple cider vinegar** is cider that has been fermented twice, first into alcohol and then into acetic acid. It's sour and sharp, and it is not a substitute for cider or juice. Use it by the tablespoon, not the cup.

Fresh sweet cider sits on the same path. Left too long in the fridge, its natural yeasts start fermenting it, which is why old cider goes fizzy and then sour.

## Cooking and Baking: When You Can Swap Them

In most recipes, apple juice and apple cider are interchangeable one for one. The difference shows up as sweetness and depth. Cider brings more tang and a rounder apple flavor; juice is sweeter and more one-note.

| Recipe | Better choice | If you only have the other one |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hot mulled cider | Cider | Juice works; add a squeeze of lemon |
| Pork chop pan sauce or braise | Cider | Juice plus 1 tsp cider vinegar per cup |
| Brine for pork or turkey | Either | No change needed |
| Apple cider donuts | Reduced cider | Reduce juice the same way; flavor is milder |
| Glaze for ham or carrots | Either | Juice will be a touch sweeter |
| Smoothies and kids' drinks | Juice | Cider is fine, just tarter |
| Apple cider vinaigrette | Cider vinegar | Neither juice nor cider replaces the vinegar |

A few tips for cooking with them:

- **Reduce it for more flavor.** Simmering cider or juice until it's reduced by half or more concentrates the apple flavor and makes a syrupy base for glazes and donuts. A wide pan reduces faster than a tall pot.
- **Pair it with pork.** Apple and pork are a classic match. A splash of cider in the pan after [oven-cooked sausages](/blog/how-to-cook-sausages-in-the-oven/) makes a quick sauce.
- **Balance with acid.** If a recipe tastes flat after swapping juice for cider, a little lemon juice fixes it. Our guide on [how much juice is in one lemon](/blog/how-much-juice-is-in-one-lemon/) helps you measure without guessing, and a [handheld lemon squeezer](/blog/how-to-use-a-lemon-juicer/) keeps seeds out of your pot.
- **Measure carefully when scaling.** Cider jugs are usually sold by the half gallon or gallon. If a batch recipe uses quarts, here's [how many ounces are in a quart](/blog/how-many-ounces-in-a-quart/).

## How Long Each One Lasts

Shelf life is the biggest practical difference in day-to-day life.

| Product | Unopened | Opened, in the fridge |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shelf-stable apple juice | Months in the pantry (check the date) | About 7 to 10 days |
| Pasteurized refrigerated cider | Around 1 to 2 weeks | About 7 to 10 days |
| Raw, unpasteurized cider | Use by the date on the jug | About 5 days |
| Frozen cider or juice | Best within 8 to 12 months | Use within 3 to 5 days after thawing |

These are general guides from producers and food writers; the date on your bottle comes first. Martinelli's, for example, says its opened products should be refrigerated and used within 5 to 10 days.

Some storage habits that help:

- **Keep cider cold.** The University of Maine extension says cider should be stored at 40°F or below and not left at room temperature for more than two hours.
- **Freeze extra.** Cider freezes well. Leave room at the top of the container for expansion, since liquid grows as it freezes. Sturdy [airtight containers](/blog/best-airtight-food-storage-containers/) or freezer-safe jars work well.
- **Know the warning signs.** Darkening, foam, fizz or a sour, boozy smell means it has started fermenting. Toss it.

If fridge space is tight, a gallon jug takes up a lot of room. Splitting it into smaller bottles and freezing half is easier than shuffling everything else around, and these tips for [organizing a small kitchen with no pantry](/blog/how-to-organize-a-small-kitchen-with-no-pantry/) help with the rest.

## Making Your Own Fresh Cider

Pressing cider at home is a fun fall project, and you don't need an orchard press. A good juicer turns a bag of apples into a small batch of cloudy, cider-style juice.

1. **Pick firm, fresh apples.** Skip bruised or rotting fruit, and never use apples picked up off the ground. The University of Maine lists using dropped apples as a clear "don't," because they're more likely to carry harmful bacteria.
2. **Wash everything.** Scrub the apples under running water, and clean the juicer parts with hot soapy water before you start.
3. **Core and cut the apples** to fit your machine. An [electric apple peeler and corer](/blog/best-electric-apple-peeler-corer-slicer/) speeds this up if you're doing a lot.
4. **Juice them** and pour the result straight into a clean container. Don't strain it if you want cider; strain it through a fine cloth if you want something closer to juice.
5. **Heat it if anyone at higher risk will drink it,** following the simmer method above.
6. **Refrigerate right away** and drink it within a few days.

Mixing sweet and tart varieties gives the most balanced flavor. A single sweet variety alone can taste flat.

## So Which Should You Buy?

Use this rule: **drink cider when you want flavor, buy juice when you want convenience.**

Pick up a jug of pasteurized cider in the fall for mulling, braising and drinking cold. Keep a bottle of 100% apple juice in the pantry for recipes, lunchboxes and days when the cider's gone. And if a jug says "unpasteurized," think about who's drinking it before you pour.
