## German vs Japanese Chef's Knife: What's the Difference?

The short answer: German knives are heavier and tougher, and Japanese knives are thinner and sharper. Neither is better. They suit different cooks.

| | German style | Japanese style |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Steel hardness | Softer, about 55 to 58 HRC | Harder, often 58 to 61 HRC |
| Blade | Thicker and heavier | Thinner and lighter |
| Shape | Strong curve for rocking | Flatter, for push and pull cuts |
| Edge life | Needs honing often | Stays sharp longer |
| Toughness | Forgiving, rarely chips | Can chip on bones or frozen food |
| Examples here | Wüsthof, Zwilling, Mercer | Global, Tojiro, Misen steel |

### Why Hardness Matters

Hardness is a trade between sharpness and toughness. Hard steel holds a keen edge for a long time, but it is brittle and can chip on bone. Softer steel bends instead of chipping, so it needs honing more often.

### Which Style Suits You?

Choose German if you cut a lot of chicken, squash and root vegetables. Choose Japanese if you mostly cut vegetables, herbs and boneless meat and will treat the knife gently. The MAC sits in between.

## What Size Chef's Knife Should You Get?

Get an 8-inch blade unless you have a reason not to. It is long enough to slice a large onion or a head of cabbage in one stroke. It is short enough to control on a normal cutting board.

| Blade length | Good for | Watch out for |

| 6 inches | Small hands, small boards, tight counters | Too short for melons and cabbage |
| 7 to 8 inches | Almost every home cook | Nothing major |

## Forged vs Stamped: Does It Matter?

Less than most people think. A forged blade is hammered into shape from hot steel. A stamped blade is cut from a flat sheet, like a cookie cutter, then hardened.

Forged knives are usually heavier and have a bolster. Stamped knives are lighter and cheaper, and the Victorinox shows they can be excellent. Our guide to [forged vs stamped knives](/blog/forged-vs-stamped-knives/) goes deeper.

## Knife Steel in Plain Words

Nearly every chef's knife today is stainless steel with extra carbon. The carbon makes the steel harder so it holds an edge. The chromium is what stops rust, the same way it does in [stainless steel cookware](/blog/what-is-stainless-steel-cookware/).

You will see a few names on labels:

- **German steel (often X50CrMoV15):** Tough and easy to sharpen. Used in most German-style knives.
- **VG10:** A harder Japanese stainless steel that takes a very keen edge. Used in the Tojiro.
- **Cromova 18:** Global's own stainless steel recipe.
- **ACUTO 440:** The Japanese steel in the current Misen knife.

Do not chase steel names. A well-made knife in ordinary steel beats a badly made one in fancy steel, much like the point in [whether expensive cookware is worth it](/blog/is-expensive-cookware-actually-worth-it/).

## What Makes a Chef's Knife Handle Comfortable?

Comfort is personal, but three things matter.

**Grip material.** Plastic and rubbery handles like the Victorinox and Mercer stay grippy when wet. Wood-look and steel handles look smarter but can feel slick with oily hands.

**Thickness.** A slim handle like the Global suits smaller hands. Larger hands often prefer the fuller German handles.

**Balance.** Rest the knife on one finger at the bolster. If it tips hard toward the blade or the handle, it may tire your hand. Most picks here balance close to the bolster.

## How to Keep a Chef's Knife Sharp

A sharp knife is safer than a dull one, because a dull blade slips off a tomato skin and goes where you did not aim.

### Hone Often, Sharpen Rarely

Honing and sharpening are different jobs. Honing straightens the thin edge, which bends a little each time you cut. Sharpening grinds away a tiny amount of steel to make a new edge.

1. **Hone** with a honing steel every few uses. Five or six light strokes per side is plenty.
2. **Sharpen** when honing no longer brings the edge back. For most home cooks that is once or twice a year.
3. **Test** by slicing a sheet of paper or a ripe tomato. Clean cut, you are fine. Tearing or slipping, it is time to sharpen.

### Choosing a Sharpener

A pull-through sharpener is quick and easy. A whetstone gives the best edge but takes practice. An electric sharpener sits in between. Our [knife sharpener guide](/blog/best-knife-sharpener/) compares all three.

Match the sharpener to the edge angle. Most picks here are sharpened to about 14 to 15 degrees per side. If you also own ceramic blades, they need a diamond sharpener, as our guide on [how to sharpen ceramic knives](/blog/how-to-sharpen-ceramic-knives/) explains.

### Cut on the Right Board

The board does as much to protect your edge as the sharpener. Wood and soft plastic boards are kind to blades. Glass, stone, metal and bamboo boards dull edges fast.

Hacking into a coconut shell or frozen meat is a quick way to chip a chef's knife. Our guide on [how to crack open a coconut](/blog/how-to-crack-open-a-coconut/) shows a safer way. For paper-thin slices, a [mandoline slicer](/blog/best-mandoline-slicers/) saves your knife and your time.

Clearing a knife block off the counter is also one of the easiest [countertop organization ideas](/blog/countertop-organization-ideas-for-a-small-kitchen/) to act on.

## How Much Should You Spend on a Chef's Knife?

You do not need to spend a lot to get a very good knife.

| Price band | What you get | Who it suits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Budget, under about $60 | Sharp, light knives that need more honing | First kitchens and beginners |
| Mid-range, about $60 to $130 | Harder steel or a forged German build | Cooks ready to step up |
| Premium, about $130 and up | Best fit and finish, top steel or heft | Cooks who prep most days |

Above that, you mostly pay for looks and rare steels. A good sharpener and a wood board will do more for your cooking.

## Do You Need Other Knives Too?

Not many. A chef's knife, a paring knife and a serrated bread knife cover almost everything in a home kitchen. Add a boning or [fillet knife](/blog/best-electric-fillet-knife/) only if you break down a lot of fish or meat.

A chef's knife also cuts down on gadgets. Chopping one onion by hand is quicker than washing a [vegetable chopper](/blog/best-vegetable-choppers/). A [food processor](/blog/best-food-processor-under-100/) only pulls ahead for large batches, and a [meat slicer](/blog/best-meat-slicer-for-home-use/) only for deli-thin cuts.

Good knife skills make [meal prep for one person](/blog/easy-meal-prep-ideas-for-one-person-in-a-small-kitchen/) much faster. Splitting a large [chicken breast](/blog/how-much-does-a-chicken-breast-weigh/) into portions also helps you [cook for one without wasting food](/blog/cook-for-one-without-wasting-food/), and suits quick [one-pan meals for one](/blog/one-pan-meals-for-one/).
