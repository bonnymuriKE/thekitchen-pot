Pre-ground pepper is mostly dust. The aroma sits in volatile oils that start escaping the moment a peppercorn cracks, which is why a decent mill changes how your food tastes.

The catch is that pepper mills all look the same from the outside. What separates a $20 mill from a $200 one is the burr inside it, how finely you can control the gap between the two halves of that burr, and how much pepper comes out per turn.

## How a pepper mill actually grinds

Inside every manual mill is a pair of burrs: a cone-shaped inner burr fixed to a central shaft, and a matching outer ring. Turning the knob spins one against the other. Peppercorns fall into the gap, get cracked by the wide upper teeth, then get sheared into smaller pieces as they travel down into the narrower lower teeth before dropping out.

That two-stage design matters. A cheap mill with a single row of blunt teeth crushes peppercorns instead of cutting them, which is why the output looks like a mix of powder and half-crushed chunks. Cole & Mason describes its own mechanisms as stripping the corn rather than crushing it, and Peugeot uses a double row of grooves for the same reason: crack first, grind second.

### Why steel burrs suit pepper

Black peppercorns are hard, dry and full of oil. A steel burr has sharp, machined teeth that bite into that shell and shear it. Peugeot uses case-hardened steel in its pepper mills, and Cole & Mason uses carbon steel in its Gourmet Precision pepper mechanism. Both hold a cutting edge for years because the metal is heat-treated to be hard on the surface and tougher underneath.

The trade-off is rust. Peugeot's own guidance is blunt about it: the pepper mill warranty does not cover rust, and salt must never go in. One damp refill or one experiment with sea salt and a carbon steel burr starts corroding.

### Why ceramic suits salt

Salt is the opposite problem. It is softer than peppercorns but chemically aggressive, so it eats steel. Ceramic mechanisms, usually alumina, do not corrode at all. Cole & Mason uses white alumina ceramic for its salt mechanisms and says the material is around 60% stronger than the older steatite ceramic used in cheaper mills, which is what lets it cut through rock salt without chipping.

Ceramic is also why a lot of budget "salt and pepper" mills feel underwhelming on pepper. Ceramic is hard but its teeth are blunter than machined steel, so it grinds pepper acceptably rather than sharply. If pepper is what you care about, buy a steel pepper mill and keep salt in a separate ceramic one. It is the same logic behind choosing the right tool for the job elsewhere in the kitchen, from [forged versus stamped knives](/blog/forged-vs-stamped-knives/) to picking a [sauté pan](/blog/best-saute-pan/) with the right base.

### How grind adjustment changes particle size

Every adjustment system does the same physical thing: it moves the inner burr up or down relative to the outer ring, changing the gap between them.

- **Tighter gap** means pepper cannot leave until it has been cut small. That is your fine grind.
- **Wider gap** means bigger fragments escape after one or two cuts. That is coarse.

How that gap is controlled is where mills differ. A thumbscrew at the base, like the Unicorn's, is infinitely variable but you have to find your setting by feel and remember it. A click-stop ring, like Peugeot's u'Select or Cole & Mason's Gourmet Precision, locks the burr into fixed positions so the same number always gives the same grind. A few mills, including the Pepper Cannon, use a fine-threaded collar that sweeps a huge range.

| Grind | Rough particle size | What it suits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fine | Powder to 0.5mm | Eggs, sauces, vinaigrettes, baking |
| Medium-fine | About 0.5 to 1mm | Everyday table pepper, soups, pasta |
| Medium | About 1 to 1.5mm | Roasted vegetables, stews, rubs |
| Coarse | About 1.5 to 2.5mm | Steak crust, cheese boards, cured meats |
| Cracked | 2.5mm and up | Pickling brine, pepper-crusted roasts |

One habit worth building: adjust the setting before you fill the hopper, or at least give the knob a quarter-turn backwards first. Forcing the adjuster while peppercorns are jammed in the burr is how the cheap ones strip.

## What to look for in a pepper mill

### Burr material and size

Steel for pepper, ceramic for salt, and the bigger the burr diameter the more pepper you get per turn. That single rule explains most of the price differences on this page. If a listing does not say what the mechanism is made of, assume it is the cheap option.

### Grind range and how it is set

Ask two questions. How wide is the range, and can you return to a setting? Click-stop rings win on repeatability, thumbscrews and collars win on range. If you mostly want one grind and want it every time, buy click-stops.

### Capacity and refilling

| Hopper size | Roughly | Suits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small (under 1/4 cup) | A week or two of table use | Dining table, second mill |
| Medium (1/4 to 1/2 cup) | A month of normal cooking | Most home kitchens |
| Large (1 cup or more) | Months, or weeks of heavy use | Rub makers, big families |

A side-loading door or a wide screw-off cap is worth more than an extra inch of capacity. If you dread refilling, you will let the mill run empty.

### Height, weight and grip

Tall mills give more leverage, which means less effort per turn. Heavy mills stay put but are tiring one-handed. If grip strength is an issue, look at mills you hold by the body rather than pinch by a knob, or go electric. The same thinking applies to any tool you use repeatedly, which is why handle shape matters so much on [vegetable choppers](/blog/best-vegetable-choppers/) and [hand mixers](/blog/best-hand-mixers/) too.

### Where it will live

A 9-inch mill is lovely until you try to fit it in a cabinet. Measure the shelf. If your counters are already crowded, the fixes in our [small kitchen cabinet organization ideas](/blog/small-kitchen-cabinet-organization-ideas/) and [vertical storage ideas](/blog/vertical-storage-ideas-for-small-kitchens/) will tell you fast whether a tall mill is realistic.

## Peppercorns: what to put in the mill

The mill is half the equation. What you feed it matters just as much.

- **Black peppercorns** are picked unripe and dried. Tellicherry corns are simply larger black corns picked later, so they have more developed flavour. They are the default for any steel mill.
- **White peppercorns** are ripe corns with the outer skin removed. Milder heat, more earthy, good in pale sauces.
- **Green peppercorns**, dried, are softer and can gum up a fine setting.
- **Pink peppercorns** are not true pepper and are brittle. They work in a mixed blend but grind unevenly on their own.
- **Mixed rainbow blends** look good in a clear mill but grind unevenly because the corns differ in hardness.

Buy whole corns in small amounts and keep them somewhere cool and dark, the same way you would treat any spice or a bag of [potatoes](/blog/how-long-do-potatoes-last/) in a well-run pantry. A [lazy Susan](/blog/8-best-lazy-susan-organizers-for-your-kitchen/) in a corner cabinet keeps refill jars reachable instead of buried. Ground pepper loses its punch within weeks; whole corns hold it for a year or more. If you are reorganising your storage, our guide to [organising a small kitchen with no pantry](/blog/how-to-organize-a-small-kitchen-with-no-pantry/) has practical ideas for spice storage that does not eat counter space.

## Keeping a pepper mill grinding well

1. **Keep it dry.** Never rinse a steel mechanism and never leave a mill next to a steaming pot. Moisture plus pepper dust equals a caked burr. If your mill lives by the stove, keep it away from the splash zone, in the same way you would protect your [knives on a magnetic strip](/blog/best-magnetic-knife-strip/).
2. **Only pepper goes in a pepper mill.** No salt, no wet spice blends, no oily seeds.
3. **Check the corn size.** Anything over about 5mm can wedge in the burr. Sort out the giants before you fill.
4. **Back the adjuster off before you force it.** If the knob feels stiff, grind a couple of turns first to clear the burr.
5. **Empty and brush it yearly.** Tip out the old corns, tap the mill upside down, and brush the burr with a dry pastry brush. Old oily dust is the main cause of a mill that has "gone slow".
6. **Wipe wood, do not wash it.** A dry or barely damp cloth is enough. Wooden mills warp and crack in water, exactly like a wooden [cutting board](/blog/best-over-the-sink-cutting-board/).

Those habits are the difference between a mill that lasts two years and one that lasts twenty, in the same way that good habits keep [stainless steel pans](/blog/how-to-clean-stainless-steel-pans/) looking new.

## Manual or electric?

Electric mills solve one real problem: they let you season with one hand while the other is holding a pan. That is worth something if you have arthritis, a wrist injury, or you are plating while stirring.

They cost you output and consistency. Most battery mills use small ceramic burrs and a low-torque motor, so they grind more slowly than a good manual mill and struggle with big peppercorns. They also add batteries or a charging cable to your counter.

| | Manual mill | Electric mill |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Output per second | Higher with a steel burr | Usually lower |
| Grind range | Wider, especially coarse | Narrower |
| One-handed use | Difficult | Easy |
| Maintenance | Almost none | Batteries or charging |
| Typical price | $25 to $60 | $30 to $70 |

If the one-handed part matters most to you, look at rechargeable models and matched sets before you buy a manual mill.
