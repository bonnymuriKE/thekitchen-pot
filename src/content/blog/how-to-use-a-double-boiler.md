Water cannot get hotter than boiling. That single fact is the whole reason a double boiler exists, and it is why chocolate melted this way never scorches.

A burner has no ceiling. A dry pan left on medium heat can climb past 500°F in a few minutes. Sugar, milk solids and egg proteins all break down long before that.

Put a pot of water between the flame and the food and the ceiling appears. The food can now only reach 212°F, no matter how long you leave it.

> **Quick answer:** Put an inch or two of water in a saucepan and bring it to a bare simmer. Set a heatproof bowl or an insert on top so it sits above the water, not in it. Add your chocolate, eggs or sauce to the bowl and stir. The steam heats the bowl gently, the water holds the temperature at 212°F or below, and nothing burns.

## Why Water Caps the Temperature

Heat a pan of water and the temperature climbs steadily to 212°F at sea level. Then it stops. Keep the burner on high and the water still reads 212°F.

The extra energy is not wasted. It goes into turning liquid water into steam, and that change takes a lot of energy without raising the temperature at all. Physicists call it latent heat. Cooks can call it a free thermostat.

Your altitude shifts the number a little. Water boils about 1°F lower for every 500 feet you climb, so a kitchen in Denver tops out near 202°F. That is still far below the point where chocolate seizes or eggs scramble, so the method works everywhere.

Compare that with a [stainless steel saucepan](/blog/what-is-stainless-steel-cookware/) sitting directly on the burner. The metal in contact with the flame can run hundreds of degrees hotter than the food above it. Anything that settles on the bottom cooks fast and sticks, which is how you end up [scrubbing burnt sugar out of a pan](/blog/how-to-clean-stainless-steel-pans/).

## Steam Does the Heating, Not the Water

Here is the part most guides skip. In a working double boiler, the bowl is not sitting in hot water. It is sitting in steam.

Steam rises off the simmering water and hits the cold underside of the bowl. It condenses back into liquid, and condensation dumps a large amount of heat into the surface it lands on. That heat spreads across the whole bowl bottom at once, not in a hot ring where the burner is.

So the gap is not empty space. The gap is the heating element.

That also explains why you only need an inch or two of water. More water does not heat the bowl faster. It just gets closer to the bowl, and a rolling boil can throw drops over the rim.

### Why the Bowl Must Not Touch the Water

The usual rule is "the bowl must never touch the water." The rule is right, but the reason is often given wrong.

Boiling water is also 212°F, so the water itself is not the problem. The problem is what is under it. In a homemade rig, a bowl deep enough to reach the water is usually deep enough to touch the pot's metal base. That base sits directly over the burner and runs much hotter than 212°F.

Once the bowl touches metal, you have lost your ceiling. You are back to frying chocolate on a hot surface.

There is a second reason, and for chocolate it matters more. A bowl pressed down into the water displaces it and pushes the boil up around the edges. Water then finds its way into the bowl, and a few drops are enough to ruin the batch.

## How to Build a Double Boiler From a Bowl and a Pot

You do not need to buy anything. Most kitchens already hold the parts.

1. **Pick the pot.** A 2-quart saucepan is about right for a home batch. Anything wider than the bowl works, since the bowl needs a rim to rest on.
2. **Pick the bowl.** Stainless steel or tempered glass both work. Steel heats faster and cools faster, so it gives you more control. Glass holds heat longer, which is forgiving for custard and unforgiving for chocolate.
3. **Check the fit before you add heat.** The bowl should sit in the pot's mouth with its base at least an inch clear of the bottom. If it drops in too far, use a smaller bowl or a wider pot.
4. **Add water.** One to two inches is plenty. You want the surface well below the bowl.
5. **Bring it to a bare simmer, then turn it down.** You are looking for lazy bubbles and steady steam, not a rolling boil.
6. **Seat the bowl and start stirring.** Use a silicone spatula or a [flat metal turner](/blog/best-metal-spatula-set/) and keep the mixture moving.

A quick note on the seal. The bowl should cover the pot's mouth, but it must not lock in. Steam needs somewhere to escape, or pressure will lift the bowl and rattle it. A slight gap at one edge is fine and normal.

Wrap a dry towel around the outside if steam is curling up over the rim. That is the moisture that seizes chocolate, so keeping it away from the bowl is worth ten seconds of setup.

**One warning for glass-top ranges.** A heavy pot plus a heavy bowl puts real weight on the glass, and sliding it around scratches the surface. The same care that applies to [cast iron on a glass-top stove](/blog/how-to-protect-glass-top-stove-from-cast-iron/) applies here: lift, never drag.

## What to Cook in a Double Boiler, and at What Temperature

People buy a double boiler for three jobs. Everything else it does is a bonus.

| Job | Target temperature | What goes wrong above it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Melting dark chocolate | 115 to 120°F | Thick, dull, grainy texture |
| Melting milk or white chocolate | 100 to 105°F | Milk solids scorch and taste burnt |
| Working with tempered dark chocolate | 86 to 90°F | Temper breaks, sets soft and streaky |
| Working with tempered milk or white | 84 to 86°F | Same, but it happens sooner |
| Crème anglaise and stirred custard | 176 to 185°F | Curdles from 190°F |
| Lemon curd | Thickens in the custard range | Scrambles, then has to be strained |
| Hollandaise and béarnaise | Hold at 120 to 145°F | Yolks curdle from 160°F |

Two of those numbers need a safety note. The USDA puts the [safe minimum internal temperature for egg dishes](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/eggs/shell-eggs-farm-table) at 160°F, and says egg and milk mixtures are safe once they reach it. Custard passes that mark comfortably. Hollandaise held at 145°F does not, which is why many cooks use pasteurized eggs for it.

An [instant-read or candy thermometer](/blog/7-best-candy-thermometer/) turns all of this from guesswork into a number. It is the single most useful thing you can add to the setup. Clip-on candy models read the pot; a probe you dip reads the food, which is what you actually care about here.

## Melting Chocolate Without Seizing It

Chocolate is the job most people buy a double boiler for, and it is the least forgiving of the three.

Start by chopping the chocolate small and even. Big chunks melt from the outside in, so the edges overheat while the middle is still solid. Even pieces melt together.

Then work in this order:

1. Bring the water to a simmer and **turn the burner off**. Residual steam is enough for a small batch.
2. Seat the dry bowl and add about two thirds of the chopped chocolate.
3. Stir slowly and steadily. Stirring moves heat around instead of letting it pool at the bottom.
4. When most of it has melted, lift the bowl off and stir in the remaining third off the heat.
5. Check the temperature. Dark chocolate should stay under about 120°F; milk and white want closer to 105°F.

That last point is not fussiness. Milk and white chocolate contain milk solids and more sugar, so they brown and clump at temperatures dark chocolate shrugs off.

### Why One Drop of Water Ruins Melted Chocolate

Melted chocolate is dry particles of sugar and cocoa suspended in fat. There is no water in it at all.

Add a few drops and the sugar grabs them instantly. The damp sugar particles stick to each other and pull the cocoa along, and the smooth liquid turns into a stiff, grainy paste in seconds. King Arthur Baking describes water hitting melted chocolate as [creating rigid lumps immediately](https://www.kingarthurbaking.com/how-to-temper-chocolate). That is seizing.

Three things cause it, and all three are avoidable:

- **A wet bowl or spatula.** Dry everything completely before it touches chocolate.
- **Steam over the rim.** Keep the heat low and the bowl seated properly.
- **Condensation dripping off the bowl bottom.** Wipe the base when you lift the bowl off the pot.

Seized chocolate cannot be brought back for coating or dipping. It can still be rescued as a sauce. Stir in warm cream or milk, about a tablespoon per ounce of chocolate, and it loosens into ganache. If you are dairy free, the [whole milk substitutes](/blog/best-substitutes-for-whole-milk/) that work best here are the full-fat ones.

### Tempering Chocolate on a Double Boiler

Melting chocolate and tempering it are different goals. Melting just makes it liquid. Tempering sets up the right fat crystals so it hardens shiny, snaps cleanly and does not bloom grey.

The seeding method is the one that fits a double boiler:

1. Melt about two thirds of your chocolate over the steam. Take dark to roughly 120°F, milk and white to about 105°F.
2. Lift the bowl off and wipe the base dry.
3. Stir in the reserved third in small handfuls. Those unmelted pieces carry the crystal structure you want.
4. Keep stirring as it cools. Dark chocolate should land at 88 to 90°F, milk and white at 84 to 86°F.
5. Test it. Smear a little on a spoon and leave it somewhere cool. Tempered chocolate sets firm and glossy within about five minutes.

If it cools past the working range, set the bowl back over the warm water for a few seconds at a time. Short bursts and constant stirring are the whole trick. Overshoot the temperature and the crystals melt out, so you start again from step one.

## Custards, Curds and the 185°F Line

Eggs are the other reason to use gentle heat. Egg yolk proteins start to firm up between 144 and 158°F, and once they are mixed into milk and sugar the whole thing thickens between 176 and 185°F. Push past about 190°F and it curdles, according to the [coagulation temperatures published by BC Open Textbooks](https://opentextbc.ca/modernpastryandplateddesserts/chapter/coagulation/).

That is a narrow window. A direct burner can carry a sauce through it in seconds, and then you are straining scrambled egg out of your dessert.

For crème anglaise, pastry cream or a simple stirred custard:

- Warm the milk or cream separately, then pour it slowly into the yolks while whisking. This is tempering the eggs, and it stops them setting on contact.
- Return the mixture to the bowl over simmering water.
- Stir constantly with a spatula, scraping the bottom and sides.
- Stop when it coats the back of a spoon and holds a line when you draw a finger through it.

Lemon curd follows the same path with acid added. The lemon juice actually helps, because acid makes egg proteins set at a slightly lower temperature and gives you a little more room. Zest and juice the fruit first, since squeezing takes longer than you think and the curd waits for nobody. A decent [lemon juicer](/blog/how-to-use-a-lemon-juicer/) saves real time here.

If your custard still looks slightly lumpy, pour it through a fine sieve while hot. Most small accidents disappear. For a thickened pastry cream you may prefer cornstarch, and if you are out, these [cornstarch substitutes](/blog/what-to-use-instead-of-cornstarch/) behave differently under heat, so check before you swap.

## Hollandaise and Other Egg Emulsions

Hollandaise is the hardest of the three jobs, because you are doing two things at once. You are cooking yolks and building an emulsion, and both can fail.

The temperature rules are tight. Yolks begin to coagulate around 149°F and curdle between 160 and 170°F, and the finished sauce is normally held between 120 and 145°F. Culinary school Rouxbe sets out [those holding temperatures](https://rouxbe.com/tips-techniques/486-food-safety-of-hollandaise) along with a warning not to hold the sauce for more than about 90 minutes.

The method:

1. Whisk yolks with a splash of water or lemon juice in the bowl, off the heat, until pale and foamy.
2. Set the bowl over water that is steaming but barely bubbling.
3. Keep whisking. The yolks will thicken into a ribbon in a minute or two.
4. Lift the bowl off the heat and dribble in melted butter, whisking the whole time.
5. Season, then hold somewhere warm, not hot.

If the sauce breaks, whisk a fresh yolk in a clean bowl and add the broken sauce to it a spoonful at a time. If it gets grainy, it went too hot and there is no way back.

A hand whisk is fine for one batch. If you make hollandaise often, a [hand mixer](/blog/best-hand-mixers/) with a single beater does the arm work for you. Some cooks build the sauce with an [immersion blender](/blog/how-to-use-an-immersion-blender/) instead, which is faster but gives you less feel for when the yolks are setting.

## When the Double Boiler Goes Wrong

Most failures trace back to one of five things.

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chocolate turned thick and grainy | Water or steam got in | Turn it into ganache with warm cream |
| Chocolate dull and streaky when set | Went above the working range | Remelt and re-temper from the start |
| Custard has lumps | Went past 190°F | Strain it hot, lower the heat next time |
| Sauce split into butter and liquid | Heat too high, or butter added too fast | Rebuild on a fresh yolk |
| Nothing is melting | Water is not simmering, or the gap is too big | Raise the heat slightly, use a deeper bowl |
| Bowl rattles and hisses | Steam is trapped underneath | Break the seal at one edge |

Heat control is the common thread. Once the water is simmering, you can usually drop the burner to low and coast. A pan with a thick, layered base holds that low heat better than a thin one, which is one reason a [good cookware set](/blog/best-cookware-set-under-200/) makes gentle cooking easier.

## When You Should Skip the Double Boiler

It is a gentle tool, so it is slow. There are jobs where that is the wrong trade.

- **Big batches of chocolate.** Above about a pound, the water bath takes ages. Professionals use a chocolate melter instead.
- **Anything that needs to reduce.** Steam heat cannot boil off liquid at any useful rate.
- **Melting butter alone.** Butter is forgiving. A small pan on low is faster and there is nothing to ruin.
- **Cheese fondue.** A [fondue pot](/blog/how-to-use-a-fondue-pot/) is built for the job and keeps the cheese at serving temperature at the table.
- **Steaming vegetables or dumplings.** You want steam in contact with the food, not under a bowl, so use a [bamboo steamer](/blog/how-to-use-a-bamboo-steamer/) or a [steamer basket](/blog/best-steamer-basket-for-instant-pot/).

The microwave deserves a fair hearing for chocolate. In 20-second bursts at half power, stirring between each, it melts chocolate well and washes up faster. What it does not give you is the slow, even approach that tempering needs, so the double boiler still wins for anything you want to set with a snap.

## Buying One vs Improvising

A bowl over a pot does everything a purpose-built set does. A dedicated one just does it with less fiddling.

The [Farberware Classic 2-quart double boiler](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00004RGB7/?tag=kitchenpot-20) is the version most home cooks end up with. It is a stainless saucepan with a matching insert and a lid, so the bottom pan works as a normal saucepan when you are not melting anything. Farberware lists it as induction compatible and oven safe to 350°F, and it goes in the dishwasher. Owner reviews are [worth reading before you buy](https://www.amazon.com/product-reviews/B00004RGB7/?tag=kitchenpot-20), since fit and handle comfort are personal.

For small jobs there is a cheaper route. A [drop-in double boiler insert](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B007SPKRZI/?tag=kitchenpot-20) holds about two cups and sits inside a saucepan you already own. It suits a single batch of ganache or a couple of servings of custard, and it stores in a drawer rather than a cupboard.

Check induction compatibility before you order either one. Induction needs magnetic steel in the base, and some stainless pans do not have it. Our guide to [cookware for induction cooktops](/blog/best-cookware-for-induction-cooktop/) covers the magnet test, and if you are shopping for the hob itself, the [induction cooktop buying guide](/blog/best-induction-cooktop-for-the-money/) is the place to start.

Whatever you use, keep it clean. Sugar and milk residue bakes onto steel and gets harder to shift with every use. The same methods that clear [mineral scale out of an electric kettle](/blog/how-to-clean-an-electric-kettle/) work on the water pan, and ceramic inserts follow the [ceramic pan cleaning rules](/blog/how-to-clean-ceramic-pan/) instead.

## Double Boilers in a Small Kitchen

A double boiler is two pieces of cookware for one job, which is a hard sell when storage is tight.

- **Use a bowl you already own.** A mixing bowl that nests in your [stockpot](/blog/best-stockpot-with-a-lid/) or saucepan costs nothing and stores flat.
- **Pick the insert over the set.** A 2-cup insert takes a fraction of the room and handles most home batches. Sizing matters more than it looks, and [cookware sizes for cooking for one](/blog/best-cookware-sizes-for-cooking-for-one/) covers how small you can sensibly go.
- **Let the bottom pan earn its keep.** A double boiler pan that doubles as a saucepan is one pot, not two. The same logic drives most [small kitchen cabinet organization](/blog/small-kitchen-cabinet-organization-ideas/) wins.
- **Stack it properly.** Put a cloth between the insert and the pan so they do not scratch. These [ideas for storing pots and pans](/blog/store-pots-and-pans-in-a-small-kitchen/) go further.

One more space note. The pot you use should be stable and flat bottomed, so skip the [nonstick pans](/blog/what-is-nonstick-cookware/) with warped bases you were about to retire. A wobbly base under a bowl of hot custard is not worth the counter space it saves.

## Start With Chocolate

If you have never used one, melt four ounces of dark chocolate before you try anything else. It takes five minutes, it shows you exactly how much heat the steam delivers, and the worst case is a bowl of chocolate sauce.

Once you can feel how slowly the temperature moves, custard and hollandaise stop being scary. They are the same setup with a thermometer in your other hand.
