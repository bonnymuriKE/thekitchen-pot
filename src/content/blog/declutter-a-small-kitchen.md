Stop shopping for bins. Most small kitchens are not short on storage, they are holding too much, and the only way to declutter a kitchen like that is to take things out.

An organizer just stores the overflow more neatly. Below is our cull-first process, zone by zone, with plain keep and toss rules for each category. Organizers come last, and only the few that still earn a place once the extras are gone.

**Quick answer:** Clear the counters so you have somewhere to sort. Then take one zone per session, in this order: utensils, cabinets, food containers, pantry, fridge, appliances, under the sink. Anything you used in the past 12 months and have room for stays; the rest leaves the house. Give the emptier kitchen a week before you buy a single organizer, and then buy only for problems you can still see.

## Where Do I Start Decluttering My Kitchen?

Start before you touch a single drawer. Good setup makes the job fast and keeps you from quitting halfway.

Get four containers ready:

1. **Keep.** Things that go back into the kitchen.
2. **Donate or sell.** Clean, working items someone else can use.
3. **Toss or recycle.** Broken, stained, expired or missing parts.
4. **Elsewhere.** Things that belong in another room, like batteries, tape or the vase from last spring.

Then pick your order. We always start with the counters, because clearing them gives you a flat place to sort on. After that, go from the most used zones to the least used, ending under the sink.

Do one zone per session. A drawer takes about 15 minutes, and a full cabinet 30 to 45. You never end up with the whole kitchen on the floor at bedtime.

One rule for every zone: **empty it completely first.** You cannot judge a drawer by looking into it. Take everything out, wipe the inside, and only put back what passes the tests below.

## Keep or Toss: Rules That Make the Choices for You

Most people stall because every item feels like a big decision. Use rules instead.

| Question | If yes | If no |
|---|---|---|
| Have I used it in the last 12 months? | Keep for now | Donate it |
| Do I own another one that does the same job? | Keep the better one | Keep |
| Does it work, with all its parts and lids? | Keep | Toss or recycle |
| Would I buy it again today? | Keep | Donate it |
| Does it fit where I would use it? | Keep | Find it a home or let it go |
| Is it only here out of guilt (gift, cost, 'someday')? | Donate it | Keep if it passes the rest |

Two notes on these rules.

First, seasonal things get a pass on the 12-month rule only if you can name the day you used them. The turkey roaster you pulled out last Thanksgiving stays. The fondue pot from a party four years ago goes.

Second, rare things can live outside the kitchen. If you bake twice a year, the stand mixer can sit on a closet shelf.

## How to Declutter Kitchen Counters

Every item on the counter takes work space you need for chopping and plating.

Clear the whole counter, wipe it, and then let things back on only if you use them **every day**. For most people that is three to five things:

- The kettle or coffee maker
- One cutting board
- A crock with your 5 or 6 most-used tools
- Salt, pepper and oil on a small tray
- A fruit bowl, if you actually eat the fruit

Everything else goes into a cabinet or out of the house. Mail, keys and chargers go to the "elsewhere" box.

For the counter itself, our goal is at least 24 inches of clear space beside the sink and 15 inches beside the stove. Those are the strips you prep on. If something blocks them, it has to move. Appliances you keep but do not use daily need a home off the counter. Our guide to [storing small appliances in a small kitchen](/blog/store-small-appliances-in-a-small-kitchen/) covers where each one can go. Never store an air fryer or toaster oven where you also run it inside a closed cabinet. Pull it out onto an open counter with several inches of air around it before you turn it on.

## How to Declutter Kitchen Utensils

The utensil drawer is where duplicates breed. Most people own three spatulas, five wooden spoons and a peeler they hate.

Dump the drawer onto the cleared counter and group like with like. Seeing six spatulas side by side makes the choice obvious.

### How Many Utensils Do You Really Need?

Fewer than you own. Here is the set we recommend for a one- or two-person kitchen:

| Tool | How many | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Flexible spatula (turner) | 1 to 2 | One silicone for nonstick, one metal if you use cast iron |
| Wooden or silicone spoon | 2 | One for sweet, one for savory if you like |
| Silicone spatula (scraper) | 1 | For bowls and sauces |
| Tongs | 1 | 9 to 12 inches |
| Ladle | 1 | |
| Whisk | 1 | A medium balloon whisk does everything |
| Vegetable peeler | 1 | Keep the one you like using |
| Can opener | 1 | |
| Kitchen shears | 1 | Also opens packages and snips herbs |
| Measuring cups and spoons | 1 set each | Nested sets stack small |
| Chef's knife | 1 | 8 inches for most cooks |
| Paring knife | 1 | |
| Bread knife | 0 to 1 | Only if you buy whole loaves |

That is about 12 to 15 pieces. If you cook a lot or bake, add a few. Our list of [small kitchen gadgets worth buying when you cook for one](/blog/small-kitchen-gadgets-worth-buying-when-you-cook-for-one/) covers the extras that earn their space, and [space-saving baking tool essentials](/blog/space-saving-baking-tool-essentials/) covers the baking side.

What to let go:

- Duplicates of anything on the list above, keeping the best one
- Single-job gadgets you have not used in a year: avocado slicers, egg separators, garlic presses you never clean, strawberry hullers
- Anything melted, cracked, rusty or with a loose handle
- Freebie tools that came with an appliance and never left the box

## How to Declutter Kitchen Cabinets

### Dishes, Mugs and Glasses

Count the people in your home. Then use this rule: **keep enough dishes for two days of meals, plus a few for guests.**

For two people, that usually means 6 to 8 dinner plates, 6 to 8 bowls, 6 to 8 small plates, and 8 glasses. Mugs are the worst offender. Most homes have 15 or more. Keep two per person plus two spares, and let the novelty and freebie mugs go.

Chipped plates and cracked glasses go straight to the toss pile. They are a safety problem, not a style choice.

### Pots, Pans and Bakeware

Pull out every pot and pan. Most small households cook nearly everything with four pieces: a 10-inch skillet, a 2- to 3-quart saucepan, a 5- to 6-quart pot or Dutch oven, and one sheet pan. Let go of warped pans that rock on the burner, nonstick pans with flaking coating, and the second and third skillets of the same size. Keep lids only for pots you still own. Lids for pans that left years ago are pure clutter.

## How to Declutter Food Storage Containers

This is the fastest win in most kitchens. Pull every container and every lid out and lay them all on the counter.

Now match them. Every container that finds its lid goes in the keep pile. Every orphan, lid or base, goes to recycling. Also toss anything that is stained, warped from the dishwasher, cloudy or that smells after washing.

Then cap the count. For one or two people, 10 to 14 containers in two or three sizes is plenty. If you meal prep, keep a few more of the size you use for lunches.

If your keepers are a mix of brands and shapes that will not stack, this is the one time we say replace instead of organize. A matching set nests into a fraction of the space. ## Declutter the Pantry and Spices

Expired food is not saving you money. It is taking the shelf where fresh food should go.

Empty one pantry shelf at a time and check every date. Use this order:

1. **Toss** anything past its date that has changed smell, color or texture, anything with signs of bugs, and opened bags that were never sealed.
2. **Use soon** anything still good but close to its date. Put it at eye level in a "use first" spot.
3. **Donate** sealed, in-date food you will not eat. Food banks take most canned and boxed goods.
4. **Combine** duplicates. Three half-bags of rice become one.

For spices, ground spices lose most of their flavor after about a year, and whole spices after two to three. If you cannot smell it when you open the jar, it will not taste of much either. Toss it.

Flour, rice, oats and pasta stay fresh and bug-free longer in airtight containers. That also helps with [keeping pests out of a small kitchen](/blog/how-to-keep-pests-out-of-a-small-kitchen/). ## Declutter the Fridge and Freezer

Clear the fridge the night before grocery day, when it is emptiest.

Take everything out, one shelf at a time, and keep the door open as little as you can. Toss:

- Cooked leftovers older than 3 to 4 days
- Anything moldy, slimy or sour-smelling
- Condiments past their date, and the ones you bought for one recipe and never used again
- Sauce packets and takeout extras you never reach for

Wipe each shelf with warm, soapy water before things go back. Check that the fridge is at 40°F or below and the freezer at 0°F. Store raw meat on the bottom shelf in a tray or sealed container, so drips cannot reach anything else.

In the freezer, toss anything with thick ice crystals or gray, dry patches (freezer burn) and anything with no label you cannot identify. From now on, write the name and date on everything you freeze.

## Declutter Under the Sink and the Junk Drawer

Under the sink collects half-used bottles. Pull it all out and check for leaks while it is empty. A damp cabinet floor means a drip that needs fixing first.

Keep one of each cleaner you use: dish soap, an all-purpose spray, a scrub powder or cream, and sponges. Combine half-full bottles of the same product. Toss dried-out sponges and rags with holes. Keep cleaners in their original bottles, and never mix bleach with ammonia or acid cleaners. If you have small children, move strong cleaners up high or add a latch.

The junk drawer gets the same treatment as every other zone. Empty it. Toss dead pens, mystery keys, expired coupons and takeout menus. Move batteries, tape and tools to the "elsewhere" box. Keep a junk drawer if you like, but make it a small one with a tray inside.

## What Should I Get Rid of in My Kitchen?

Use this as a quick hit list. These items rarely earn their space in a small kitchen.

| Category | Let go of | Keep |
|---|---|---|
| Food storage | Lids with no base, stained or warped tubs | One matching set, 10 to 14 pieces |
| Mugs and cups | Freebies, chipped ones, extras | 2 per person plus 2 spares |
| Utensils | Duplicates, single-job gadgets | About 12 to 15 core tools |
| Cookware | Warped or flaking pans, same-size doubles | 4 to 6 core pieces |
| Pantry | Expired food, stale spices, mystery bags | What you eat in a normal month |
| Fridge | Old leftovers, dead condiments, sauce packets | This week's food |
| Appliances | Machines unused for 12 months | Daily and weekly machines |
| Linens | Torn towels, stained oven mitts | 6 to 8 dish towels, 2 oven mitts |
| Paper | Takeout menus, old manuals | Warranties, filed or scanned |
| Plastic bags | Most of them | One small bag of bags, no more |

## Where to Send What You Toss

A donate box that sits by the door for a month is still clutter. Thrift stores take working dishes, pots and appliances, and food banks take sealed, in-date food. Local buy-nothing groups move good cookware fast. Wrap chipped dishes and broken glass in newspaper before they go in the trash.

Our rule: everything leaves within 48 hours. Put the box in your car the day you finish a zone.

Here is the mistake most people make. They buy a cart full of bins, then declutter, then find half the bins are the wrong size or have nothing to hold.

## How Do You Organize a Small Cluttered Kitchen After the Purge?

Organize by zone, not by item type. Put each thing next to the place you use it.

- **Prep zone** (beside the sink or stove): knives, cutting boards, mixing bowls, measuring tools
- **Cooking zone** (by the stove): pots, pans, spatulas, oils and spices
- **Cleaning zone** (at the sink): soap, sponges, towels, trash and recycling
- **Dish zone** (near the sink or dishwasher): plates, bowls, glasses and flatware, so unloading takes one step
- **Food zone** (pantry and fridge): dry goods, snacks and the "use first" shelf

The things you use daily go between your hip and your shoulder. Heavy pots go low. Rare things go up high or out of the kitchen. ## How Do You Keep a Small Kitchen From Getting Cluttered?

Staying clear takes a few small habits.

- **One in, one out.** A new mug means an old mug leaves. A new pan means the one it replaces goes to the donate box that day.
- **The 10-minute nightly reset.** Wash or load the dishes, wipe the counters and put back anything that is not on your daily list.
- **A standing donate box.** Keep one in a closet. When you notice something you never use, drop it in. When it is full, it leaves.
- **Shop from a list.** Check the fridge and pantry once a week before you shop. Most food clutter is a second jar bought because you forgot the first.
- **Say no to freebies.** Branded mugs, sauce packets and extra chopsticks add up fast.
- **A full review twice a year.** Spring and fall work well. It takes an hour once the big purge is done.

Plan meals around what is in the fridge, too. Our [easy meal prep ideas for one person in a small kitchen](/blog/easy-meal-prep-ideas-for-one-person-in-a-small-kitchen/) help use up food before it turns into clutter.

## Eight Things We Would Never Do When Decluttering

- **Buying organizers first.** You end up storing clutter neatly instead of removing it.
- **Doing the whole kitchen at once.** You run out of energy with everything on the floor. Go zone by zone.
- **Sorting without emptying.** Looking into a drawer hides what is at the back. Take everything out.
- **Keeping things "just in case."** If you can borrow it or buy it again cheaply, let it go.
- **Keeping the donate box at home.** It slowly gets unpacked. Get it out within 48 hours.
- **Putting things back where they were.** Use the zone plan, even if the old spot was "always" there.
- **Keeping heat appliances in cabinets while in use.** Store them inside if you like, but run them only on an open counter with air around them.
- **Skipping the labels.** Without names, shelves turn into catch-alls again.
