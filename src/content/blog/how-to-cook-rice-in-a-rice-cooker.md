White rice wants about 1¼ cups of water per cup of rice in a cooker. Brown rice wants roughly 2¼. Nearly everything else is detail.

The detail that trips people up comes first, though. The plastic cup in the box is not a US cup.

It holds 180 ml, about three quarters of a standard cup. Measure with it and the pot's water lines line up. Measure with a kitchen cup and they do not.

## Rice to Water Ratios in a Rice Cooker

These ratios assume you measure the rice and the water with the **same cup**, whatever size that cup is. That is what makes a ratio a ratio.

| Rice type | Water per 1 cup rice | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Long-grain white (basmati, jasmine) | 1 to 1¼ | Rinse well; basmati likes the low end |
| Medium-grain white | 1¼ | The middle of the road setting |
| Short-grain white (sushi, calrose) | 1 to 1¼ | Rinse hard, rest the full 15 minutes |
| Brown rice, any length | 2 to 2¼ | Soak first on a basic cooker |
| Parboiled or converted | 1½ to 2 | Stays separate even if you overdo it |
| Wild rice | 1½ | Long cook; it splits open when done |
| Jasmine, no rinse | 1 | Skip the rinse and cut the water |

Zojirushi's own guidance puts long-grain white at 1¼ cups of water per rice cup and wild rice at 1½. Aroma calls for 2¼ for brown rice. Those are the numbers worth anchoring to, because the rest of the range is just personal preference about softness.

Start in the middle of a range and adjust by a quarter cup at a time. Rice is forgiving in one direction only, so err dry. You can always steam a dry pot back to life, but you cannot un-wet mushy rice.

### The Cup in the Box Is Not a US Cup

The cup that ships with almost every rice cooker holds 180 ml. It comes from the **gō**, a traditional Japanese unit, and the whole appliance is built around it. Aroma states the 180 ml size plainly, and Zojirushi describes its cup as roughly six ounces, about three quarters of a US cup.

So a "10-cup" rice cooker does not hold 10 US cups of raw rice. It holds 10 of those smaller cups, which is closer to 7½ US cups. That is why so many cookers seem smaller than advertised.

Losing the plastic cup is not a disaster. Just pick any cup, mug or glass and use it for both the rice and the water. The ratio is what matters, not the absolute size. If unit conversions in the kitchen tend to bite you, our guide to [ounces in a quart](/blog/how-many-ounces-in-a-quart/) is a useful bookmark.

### When to Trust the Water Lines Instead

The lines inside the pot are the easiest path, and on most cookers they are calibrated for short and medium-grain white rice. Zojirushi says this directly.

Use them like this: add the rinsed rice using the cooker's cup, level the surface, then add water up to the line matching the number of cups you added. No measuring jug needed.

Where the lines stop helping is with rice they were not designed for. Long-grain basmati and most brown rice do better with a measured ratio. Many cookers print a separate brown rice scale, so check the far side of the pot before you assume there isn't one.

## Rinse the Rice, and Here Is Why

Rinsing is the step people skip and then wonder why their rice clumps.

Milling white rice scrapes off the bran and polishes the grain. That polishing leaves fine starch dust clinging to the outside of every kernel. The dust is mostly **amylopectin**, the branched starch that turns sticky when it hydrates.

Drop unrinsed rice into water and that dust dissolves first. It thickens the cooking liquid into a light paste, and the paste dries into glue between the grains. America's Test Kitchen describes the same effect, and the fix is simply to wash the dust away before it ever gets wet in the pot.

How to do it:

1. Put the rice in the cooker pot or a bowl and cover it with cold water.
2. Swirl with your hand for 10 to 15 seconds. The water turns cloudy white.
3. Pour off the cloudy water, tilting the bowl and cupping the rice with your palm.
4. Repeat three or four times, until the water runs mostly clear rather than milky.
5. Drain well so the leftover rinse water does not add to your ratio.

A fine-mesh strainer makes the draining part painless, and it doubles for washing lentils and quinoa. You cannot over-rinse rice, so do not worry about going too far.

**One exception.** Brown rice keeps its bran layer, so there is very little loose starch to remove. Rinse it to clear field dust and broken hulls, not for texture. The same goes for wild and red rice.

Fortified white rice sold in the US carries added vitamins as a surface coating, and heavy rinsing washes some of that away. If that matters to you, one quick rinse instead of four is a fair compromise.

## Do You Need to Soak Rice?

Not for everyday white rice in a modern cooker. Most fuzzy logic models build a soak into the cycle automatically, which is part of why they take longer than the cheap switch models.

Soaking does earn its place in three cases:

- **Basmati.** A 20 to 30 minute soak lets the grains hydrate evenly, so they lengthen instead of cracking. Cut the cooking water by a couple of tablespoons per cup to account for what the rice already drank.
- **Brown rice on a basic cooker.** Soaking for 30 to 60 minutes softens the bran and shortens the cook. Without it, a switch-model cooker often clicks off before the center is done.
- **Old rice.** Rice that has sat in the cupboard for a year is drier and takes water more slowly.

Soak in the water you plan to cook in. That way the liquid the rice absorbs is already counted in your ratio and nothing gets thrown off.

Skip soaking for jasmine if you like it a little firm, and skip it entirely for parboiled rice, which has already been hydrated and dried at the mill.

## What Actually Happens Inside the Cooker

A basic rice cooker has no timer and no thermometer you can set. It is a hot plate, a pot and one temperature sensor, and the physics does the rest.

Here is the sequence. The heater warms the pot, the water comes to a boil, and the rice starts absorbing it. Starch inside each grain gelatinizes, meaning it swells and locks water into its structure. That is what turns hard, chalky rice into something soft enough to eat.

Through all of that, the pot cannot get hotter than boiling. Liquid water at sea level tops out at 212°F, and any extra energy goes into turning water into steam instead of raising the temperature. The same ceiling is why [electric kettles](/blog/how-do-electric-kettles-work/) shut off when they do. Hard water leaves the same chalky scale in a rice cooker pot that it leaves in a kettle, and [cleaning an electric kettle](/blog/how-to-clean-an-electric-kettle/) uses the same vinegar fix.

So the cooker does not need to know anything about rice. It only needs to know when the water is gone.

### Why the Cooker Clicks Off When It Does

The moment the last free water is absorbed or boiled away, the temperature of the pot stops being pinned at boiling and shoots up. That jump is the signal.

On a simple switch cooker, the sensor is a small magnet sitting against a piece of thermally sensitive ferrite. The ferrite is magnetic while it stays below about 103°C, roughly 217°F, so it holds the magnet and keeps the heating circuit closed. Push past that temperature and the ferrite loses its magnetism, the magnet drops away, and a spring flips the switch. That is the clunk you hear.

Fancier cookers replace the magnet with a thermistor, an electronic sensor that reports temperature continuously to a small computer. Zojirushi says its models use a thermostat that detects the rise above 212°F. Its micom cookers then adjust heat and timing to suit how much rice is in the pot. The trigger is the same either way.

This explains a few things that otherwise look like faults:

- **The cooker is not measuring doneness.** It is measuring dryness. Those are different, and the gap between them is why the ratio matters so much.
- **Too little water and it clicks early**, leaving crunchy centers, because the water ran out before the starch finished swelling.
- **Too much water and it clicks late**, after the grains have taken on more than they should and gone slack.
- **Lifting the lid mid-cycle** vents steam and loses heat, which is why the manual tells you not to.

## Let It Rest After the Click

The click is not the finish line. Rice needs 10 to 15 minutes in the closed pot afterwards, and skipping this is the second most common reason for disappointing results.

Two things happen during that rest. Moisture redistributes from the wetter grains at the bottom to the drier ones near the top, so the whole pot evens out. At the same time the outside of each grain firms up slightly as it cools off the boil, which is what keeps the grains separate when you serve.

Zojirushi treats this as a real stage of cooking and calls it steaming, alongside soaking and cooking. Most keep-warm settings hold the pot somewhere around 150°F to 165°F, which is warm enough to finish the job without drying the rice out.

Then fluff it. Use the flat paddle that came with the cooker, or a silicone spatula, and lift and fold from the bottom. Do not stir in circles. Stirring crushes the grains against each other and releases starch, which is exactly the gummy texture you were avoiding.

## How to Cook Rice in a Rice Cooker, Step by Step

Putting it all together, start to finish.

1. **Measure the rice** with the cooker's cup, or any cup you will also use for water. Level it off rather than heaping it.
2. **Rinse** in cold water three or four times, until the water runs mostly clear. Drain well.
3. **Add water** to the ratio in the table above, or up to the matching line in the pot.
4. **Soak if the rice calls for it.** Basmati and brown rice benefit; everyday white rice does not need it.
5. **Add salt or fat now, if you want it.** A quarter teaspoon of salt per cup, or a teaspoon of butter or oil, goes in before the lid.
6. **Level the surface** and make sure no grains sit stranded above the waterline on the pot wall.
7. **Close the lid and start the cooker.** Pick the right program if your model has them.
8. **Leave it alone.** No lifting, no stirring, no peeking.
9. **Rest 10 to 15 minutes** after the click, lid closed.
10. **Fluff with a paddle**, lifting and folding, then serve.

Nine of those ten steps are free. Only the cooker costs money, which is a strong argument for spending a little more on one you will use several times a week.

## Brown Rice Needs Different Rules

Brown rice is the same grain with the bran and germ still attached. That intact bran is a waxy, fibrous jacket, and water has to work through it before it can reach the starch inside.

Everything follows from that. Brown rice needs about twice the water, roughly twice the time, and it holds its chew no matter how long you cook it. A 1¼ ratio that produces lovely white rice will produce gravel with brown.

What to do:

- **Use 2 to 2¼ cups of water per cup of rice.** Aroma specifies 2¼ for its cookers.
- **Soak for 30 to 60 minutes** if your cooker has no brown rice program.
- **Expect 45 to 60 minutes** of cooking, against 20 to 30 for white.
- **Rest for the full 15 minutes.** Brown rice evens out more slowly.

A dedicated brown rice setting is worth having if you eat it often, because it runs a longer soak at low heat before the boil. ## Troubleshooting: Gummy, Crunchy, Scorched or Wet

Most rice problems trace back to three variables: water, rinsing and rest. This table maps the symptom to the cause.

| What went wrong | Why it happened | The fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gummy, grains stuck together | Too much water, or no rinse | Cut water by ¼ cup per cup; rinse until clear |
| Crunchy or chalky centers | Not enough water, or no soak on brown rice | Add ¼ cup water; soak brown rice first |
| Wet and soupy on top | Too much water, lid opened during cooking | Leave lid off on keep-warm 10 minutes |
| Scorched layer on the bottom | Too little water, or the pot sat on keep-warm for hours | Check the ratio; turn keep-warm off sooner |
| Boiled over through the vent | Pot overfilled, or unrinsed starchy rice | Stay under the MAX line; rinse; add a teaspoon of oil |
| Clicked off far too early | Water ran out before the rice cooked | More water next time; check the pot sits flat on the plate |
| Dry and hard after sitting | Left on keep-warm too long | Hold two hours at most, then refrigerate |

Two rescues worth knowing. For rice that came out dry and hard, sprinkle two to three tablespoons of hot water over the top, close the lid and run the cycle again briefly. For rice that came out too wet, leave the lid off with the cooker on keep-warm and let steam escape for 10 minutes before you fluff it.

If the pot boils over every single time no matter what you do, look at the pot itself. A warped base or a worn nonstick coating changes how heat transfers. Our explainer on [what nonstick cookware is](/blog/what-is-nonstick-cookware/) covers when a coating is past saving, and our [nonstick pan buying guide](/blog/best-nonstick-pans-with-buying-guide/) covers replacements.

No cooker at all? A heavy [lidded pot](/blog/best-stockpot-with-a-lid/) does the same job on the stove. Use the same ratios, bring it to a boil, then drop the heat to the lowest setting for 15 minutes.

## How Long Can Cooked Rice Sit in the Cooker?

This is the part worth taking seriously, because cooked rice carries a specific risk that most foods do not.

Raw rice commonly carries spores of *Bacillus cereus*, and those spores survive cooking. If cooked rice then sits in the temperature range where bacteria thrive, the spores germinate and produce a toxin that reheating will not destroy. That is the illness people call fried rice syndrome.

NC State Extension's [guidance on leftover rice](https://brunswick.ces.ncsu.edu/2024/02/safety-of-leftover-rice/) is clear about the rule: keep rice hot, above 135°F, or get it cold, below 41°F. The trouble lives in between.

In practice:

- **Keep-warm is a holding mode, not storage.** It is designed to sit above the danger range, but two hours is a sensible ceiling for quality as well as safety.
- **Never leave rice in a switched-off cooker overnight.** The pot cools slowly and spends hours in exactly the wrong range.
- **Cool leftovers quickly.** Spread the rice in a shallow [sealed container](/blog/best-airtight-food-storage-containers/) rather than leaving it in a deep mound.
- **Reheat only what you are about to eat.** Repeated heating and cooling gives spores more chances.

Refrigerate promptly and cooked rice keeps for several days. Freezing works too, though the texture softens, so use frozen rice in fried rice or soup rather than serving it plain. If leftovers are part of your routine, the same cooling logic applies to other proteins, which is what makes [chicken storage timing](/blog/how-long-does-chicken-last-in-the-fridge/) worth knowing as well.

## Choosing a Rice Cooker That Matches How You Cook

Size is the decision people get wrong most often.

Remember the 180 ml cup. A 3-cup cooker makes roughly 2¼ US cups of raw rice at capacity, which is plenty for one or two people. A 5.5-cup model suits a small family. Going bigger than you need is a real cost, because a mostly empty pot cooks small batches less evenly and eats counter space you do not have.

For a single-person kitchen, a small cooker earns its spot faster than almost any other appliance. A few features actually change results:

- **Fuzzy logic or micom control** adjusts heat and timing to the load instead of running one fixed cycle. It is the single biggest jump in quality.
- **A brown rice program** matters if you eat brown rice more than occasionally.
- **A removable, thick inner pot** heats more evenly and washes far more easily than a thin one.
- **A detachable lid or inner lid** saves you scrubbing starch out of a hinge every week.

## What Else Goes in the Pot

A rice cooker is a controlled steam box, so anything that cooks in water suits it.

- **Other grains.** Quinoa at 1:1½, steel-cut oats, farro and pearl barley all work. Watch for foaming and add a few drops of oil.
- **Steamed vegetables.** Many models ship with a tray that sits above the rice, so the vegetables cook in the same steam. A [steamer basket](/blog/best-steamer-basket-for-instant-pot/) often fits too, and a [bamboo steamer](/blog/how-to-use-a-bamboo-steamer/) does the same job on the stove.
- **Sushi rice.** Cook short-grain rice at 1:1¼, then fold in seasoned vinegar while it is still warm. Our [sushi kit guide](/blog/best-sushi-making-kit/) covers the rest of the setup.
- **Soups and beans.** Slower than a pressure cooker but completely unattended. Comparing routes, our [Instant Pot versus pressure cooker](/blog/instant-pot-vs-pressure-cooker/) breakdown shows where each one wins.

A multicooker can do all of this and more. If you are weighing one appliance against two, [what an Instant Pot is](/blog/what-is-an-instant-pot/) and the [Instant Pot versus Ninja Foodi](/blog/instant-pot-vs-ninja-foodi/) comparison are the places to start. Dedicated rice cookers still make better rice, which is the trade.

## The One Habit That Fixes Most Rice

Write down what you did.

Keep a note on your phone with the rice brand, the ratio and how it turned out. Three pots in, you will have a number that works for the rice you actually buy, and you will stop guessing forever. Brands vary more than rice types do, so the chart above is a starting point rather than a law.

Then change one thing at a time. A quarter cup less water, or a longer soak, or a proper rest. Change two and you learn nothing.
