You pull a tray of cookies out of a regular oven and the ones in the back corner are dark brown while the front row is still pale. So you bake the next batch, rotate halfway, and wait again. With three dozen to go, it takes all afternoon.

A convection oven fixes most of that. The fan moves hot air around the pans, so cookies bake more evenly and you can fit two trays in at once. The catch is that you can't just follow the recipe as written. This guide shows you how to bake cookies in a convection oven, including the temperature change, how long to bake, which pans work best, where to put the racks, and how it all works in a small countertop convection toaster oven.

> **Quick answer:** Lower the recipe's temperature by 25°F (so 375°F becomes 350°F), unless your oven converts it for you. Use light-colored, low-sided cookie sheets, and bake one tray on the middle rack or two trays spaced evenly apart. Start checking two to three minutes before the recipe's shortest bake time, and take the cookies out when the edges are golden and the centers still look slightly soft.

## What a Convection Oven Does Differently

A regular oven heats with elements at the top and bottom. The air inside stays fairly still, so the areas closest to the elements run hotter than the rest. That's why cookies near the back or on the bottom rack often brown faster.

A convection oven adds a fan, usually at the back, that pushes hot air over, under and around the food. GE Appliances describes it simply: the fan circulates hot air all around the food for more even heating and browning. Moving air transfers heat faster than still air, which is why convection feels hotter than the number on the dial. It's the same reason a windy 40°F day feels colder than a calm one.

For cookies, that has three effects:

- **Faster browning.** The surface dries and colors quicker, so edges get crisp.
- **More even baking.** Hot air reaches every part of the tray, so fewer burnt corners and pale middles.
- **Multiple trays at once.** America's Test Kitchen notes that convection lets you bake two sheets of cookies at the same time that would be better baked one sheet at a time in a standard oven.

Some ovens have a "true" or "European" convection system with a third heating element around the fan. Others just use a fan to move air heated by the regular elements. Both work for cookies. Check your manual to see which one you have and whether it has a dedicated convection bake setting, which usually runs the fan more gently than convection roast. King Arthur Baking recommends the lower-speed convection bake setting for baked goods because a strong fan can push cookies around on the pan.

## What You Need

You don't need special equipment, but a few choices make a big difference.

| Item | Why it helps | What to look for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rimless or low-rimmed cookie sheets | Air flows across the whole tray instead of being blocked by tall sides | Light-colored aluminum, like a [Nordic Ware Naturals half sheet](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0049C2S32/?tag=kitchenpot-20) |
| Quarter sheet pans | Fit countertop ovens and small full-size ovens | The [Nordic Ware quarter sheet](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B078NJRJ7T/?tag=kitchenpot-20) measures about 13 x 9.6 inches |
| Silicone baking mat or parchment | Stops sticking and holds cookies in place when the fan runs | Mats are reusable; clip or weight parchment in strong ovens |
| [Oven thermometer](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=oven+thermometer&tag=kitchenpot-20) | Shows whether your oven runs hot or cold | Hang it on the middle rack |
| Cookie scoop | Makes every cookie the same size so they bake at the same rate | A 1.5 tablespoon scoop suits most drop cookies |
| Wire cooling rack | Stops the bottoms from overcooking on the hot pan | One that fits over a sheet pan saves space |

The pan matters more than most people think. The Kitchn recommends low-sided or rimless baking sheets for convection because they let air circulate and brown food better. Dark pans absorb more heat and can burn cookie bottoms faster, especially with a fan running, so light aluminum is the safer pick.

## How to Convert Cookie Recipes for Convection

Almost every cookie recipe is written for a regular oven. Here's how to adjust it.

### Lower the temperature by 25°F

This is the main rule, and it comes up again and again from reliable sources. King Arthur Baking says to reduce the temperature by 25°F. GE Appliances says the same for its convection ranges. America's Test Kitchen mentions that manufacturers suggest 25°F to 50°F, and that a 25°F drop worked well for cookies in its kitchen.

| Recipe temperature (regular bake) | Convection temperature | Common cookies at this temperature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 400°F | 375°F | Thin, crisp cookies; some shortbread bars |
| 375°F | 350°F | Classic chocolate chip (the Nestlé Toll House recipe uses 375°F) |
| 350°F | 325°F | Sugar cookies, oatmeal cookies, thicker drop cookies |
| 325°F | 300°F | Shortbread, butter cookies, delicate cut-outs |
| 300°F | 275°F | Biscotti second bake, very pale butter cookies |

### Check whether your oven converts automatically

Many newer ovens have an auto conversion feature. GE Appliances notes that most of its convection ranges make the 25°F adjustment on their own. If your oven does this, set the recipe temperature and let the oven subtract. If you lower it yourself as well, you'll end up 50°F too low and your cookies will be pale and slow to set. Check the manual or the display. Some ovens show the converted temperature once you press start.

### Start checking earlier

Convection usually bakes cookies a little faster, but how much faster depends on your oven, your pan and the cookie. Instead of cutting the time by a fixed amount, keep the recipe's time as a guide and start checking two to three minutes before the shortest time listed. For a recipe that says 9 to 11 minutes, look at about 7 minutes.

King Arthur Baking's rule for convection is to make your first check 10 minutes earlier than the recipe says, but that's written for longer bakes like breads and roasts. For cookies that only bake 8 to 12 minutes, a couple of minutes early is plenty.

### Know what "done" looks like

Cookies are ready when:

- The edges are set and golden.
- The tops look dry, not shiny or wet.
- The centers still look a touch soft or underbaked.

Cookies keep baking on the hot sheet for a few minutes after you take them out. If they look fully done in the oven, they'll be overdone once they cool.

## How to Bake Cookies in a Convection Oven, Step by Step

Here's the full process, using any drop cookie recipe such as chocolate chip or oatmeal.

1. **Set up the racks first.** For one tray, put a rack in the middle. For two trays, space the racks evenly, leaving plenty of room between them. Do this while the oven is cold.
2. **Preheat on convection bake.** Set the oven to the recipe temperature minus 25°F, unless your oven auto-converts. Let it fully preheat. Convection ovens tend to preheat faster, but wait for the signal before putting cookies in.
3. **Line your cookie sheets.** Use a silicone mat or parchment. If your fan is strong, weigh down the corners of parchment with a little dough or use a mat instead, so the paper doesn't flap onto the cookies.
4. **Scoop evenly.** Use a cookie scoop so every cookie is the same size. Leave about 2 inches between them, since convection still lets cookies spread.
5. **Chill the dough if the recipe calls for it.** Cold dough spreads less, which helps with convection's faster heat.
6. **Load the trays.** Slide them in quickly so you don't lose heat. If you're using two racks, try to keep them centered so air can move around the edges of each tray.
7. **Rotate at the halfway point.** Turn each tray front to back, and if you're baking two, swap top and bottom. America's Test Kitchen found this step still mattered with a 25°F reduction.
8. **Check early.** Look two to three minutes before the recipe's shortest time. Pull them when the edges are golden and the centers look barely set.
9. **Cool on the pan briefly.** Leave the cookies on the sheet for 2 to 5 minutes so they firm up, then lift them onto a wire rack with a thin [metal spatula](/blog/best-metal-spatula-set/).
10. **Let the pan cool between batches.** Putting dough on a hot pan starts it melting before it reaches the oven, which leads to flat, greasy cookies. Rotate between two or three pans, or run the pan under cool water and dry it.

### Baking two trays at once

This is the biggest advantage of convection, so it's worth getting right.

- **Space the racks well.** Whirlpool suggests using slots 2 and 4 or 5 when cooking on multiple racks, so there's room for air between pans.
- **Stagger if you can.** Whirlpool also recommends placing pans so no dish sits completely above or below another. With cookie sheets, that's not always possible, so just keep the space between racks as open as you can.
- **Don't overcrowd.** The Kitchn warns that packing the oven blocks air circulation and cancels out the benefit of the fan. Two sheets is usually the sweet spot for home ovens.
- **Rotate halfway.** Swap top and bottom trays, and turn each front to back.

ATK also tried reducing the temperature by 50°F and extending the bake time. The cookies browned evenly without swapping racks, but ATK wouldn't recommend it as a general rule, so treat it as an experiment rather than the standard method.

## Which Cookies Do Best With Convection?

Convection doesn't suit every cookie equally. Here's how common types tend to behave.

| Cookie type | Convection result | Tips |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Crisp chocolate chip | Excellent: even golden edges | Lower 25°F, check 2 to 3 min early |
| Thin, crispy cookies (lace, tuiles) | Very good for crispness | Watch closely; they go from done to burnt quickly |
| Shortbread and butter cookies | Good, even color | Bake at the lower end of temperatures for a pale finish |
| Sugar cookie cut-outs | Good for large batches | Chill the cut shapes before baking to hold edges |
| Oatmeal cookies | Good | Keep an eye on raisins at the edges, which can scorch |
| Thick, soft bakery-style cookies | Mixed: tops set before centers | Some bakers prefer regular bake for these |
| Biscotti | Very good, especially the second bake | Convection helps dry them evenly |

If a recipe produces thick, soft, underbaked-center cookies, try one tray on convection and one on regular bake. Some bakers prefer the regular setting for these because the slower heat gives the dough time to spread and stay tender. For crisp cookies and big holiday batches, convection is hard to beat. Shortbread and other tender cookies often lean on cornstarch to soften the crumb, so keep our list of [what to use instead of cornstarch](/blog/what-to-use-instead-of-cornstarch/) nearby if the box is empty.

What about other baked goods? King Arthur Baking and The Kitchn both suggest skipping convection for cakes, quick breads, custards and wet batters, because the moving air can make them bake unevenly or rise less. Cookies, biscuits, scones, pizza and roasted vegetables are where convection shines. If you like baking pizza too, a [pizza stone](/blog/best-pizza-stone/) works well on convection, and so do crusty loaves shaped in a [bread proofing basket](/blog/best-bread-proofing-basket-reviews/).

## Troubleshooting Convection Cookies

When something goes wrong, the cause is usually temperature, time or the pan.

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Edges burnt, centers raw | Temperature too high | Lower by another 25°F, or check if your oven already auto-converts |
| Bottoms too dark | Dark pan, or rack too low | Switch to light aluminum; move the rack up one level |
| Cookies too flat | Dough too warm or pan still hot | Chill the dough 30 minutes; cool pans between batches |
| Cookies pale and dry | Double conversion (you and the oven both lowered it) | Set the recipe temperature and let the oven convert |
| Cookies slid or tilted | Fan too strong for parchment | Use a silicone mat, or weigh down parchment corners |
| One tray browner than the other | Uneven airflow between racks | Swap trays and rotate front to back halfway through |
| Some cookies done, others not | Uneven sizes | Use a cookie scoop for identical portions |

If you still get odd results, check the real temperature. Hang an oven thermometer on the middle rack and preheat. Plenty of ovens run a little hot or cold compared with the dial, and even a small difference shows up quickly with cookies. For precise sugar work alongside baking, a good [candy thermometer](/blog/7-best-candy-thermometer/) is worth having too.

## How to Bake Cookies in a Convection Toaster Oven

In a small kitchen, a countertop convection oven is often the easiest way to bake. You don't have to heat the oven on a [full-size electric range](/blog/best-slide-in-electric-range/) for a dozen cookies, and many apartment kitchens have tiny ovens or none at all.

Breville's Smart Oven Pro, for example, has a Cookies preset that defaults to 350°F (175°C) for 11 minutes, with convection turned on and the rack in the middle position. That's a useful starting point for most drop cookies. Here's how to get good results from any countertop model:

- **Measure the inside before buying pans.** A quarter sheet pan fits many larger toaster ovens, but not all. Measure the rack, and leave at least an inch of space around the pan so air can circulate.
- **Keep the rack in the middle.** The elements are much closer to the food than in a full-size oven. If the bottoms brown too fast, move the rack up a level rather than dropping the temperature.
- **Bake a test cookie.** Countertop ovens vary a lot. Bake one or two first, note the time, then do the rest.
- **Lower the temperature 25°F on convection, unless it's a preset.** Presets are already set for convection.
- **Check your manual about parchment.** Some countertop oven makers warn against using parchment because the paper can get too close to the heating elements. A silicone mat or a lightly greased pan is a safe alternative.
- **Bake in small batches.** Expect four to eight cookies per tray. Keep extra dough chilled between batches.

Freeze the rest of the dough in scoops, then bake a few at a time straight from the freezer, adding a minute or two. That's handy if you're cooking for one or two people.

## Does Convection Save Energy?

A little, in most cases. Because convection bakes faster and at a lower temperature, the oven runs for less time. The bigger energy win in a small household is baking in a countertop oven instead of heating a full-size one. ## Storing Your Cookies

Once your cookies have cooled completely, store them in an airtight container at room temperature. Keep crisp and soft cookies in separate containers, since the moisture from soft ones will make crisp ones go limp. Our guide to the [best airtight food storage containers](/blog/best-airtight-food-storage-containers/) has options that stack well in small cupboards.

Always use oven mitts or [heat resistant gloves](/blog/10-best-heat-resistant-gloves-for-cooking/) when moving trays, especially when you're rotating two pans at once and reaching into a hot oven.
