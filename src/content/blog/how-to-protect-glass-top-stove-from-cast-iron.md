Whirlpool says cast iron is fine on a glass cooktop. GE tells you to skip it. Both are right, and the difference comes down to the bottom of your pan.

Cast iron does not scratch glass because it is iron. It scratches because the casting is rough, or because a grain of grit is caught underneath it.

So the job is not really about cast iron at all. It is about five habits, and the first one takes two minutes with your fingernail.

- Check the pan base for burrs and rough spots, and smooth them.
- Wipe both the pan base and the glass before the pan goes down.
- Lift the pan on and off. Never slide it.
- Preheat on medium, not high, and give it time.
- Clear a sugary spill while the glass is still hot.

## What Cooktop Makers Actually Say

The advice online is confident and contradictory, so here is what the manufacturers themselves put in writing.

**GE discourages cast iron.** Its guidance on [cookware for radiant glass cooktops](https://products.geappliances.com/appliance/gea-support-search-content?contentId=16259) warns that a burr or rough spot on the cookware will scratch the glass. GE also notes that cast iron holds so much heat that it can cause the element to shut down. The brands it recommends instead are stainless steel, heavy-weight aluminum and copper-bottom pans.

**Whirlpool and Maytag allow it with rules.** Whirlpool's guide to [cast iron on glass cooktops](https://www.whirlpool.com/blog/kitchen/can-you-put-cast-iron-on-glass-cooktop.html) says the pans can be used without damage. It asks you to clean and dry the base, place the pan gently, and lift rather than slide when repositioning. Maytag adds that you should remove the pan promptly when you finish, so it does not scorch the cooling glass.

Both positions come from the same reasoning. A clean, smooth, carefully handled cast iron pan is fine. A rough one that gets dragged is not. GE assumes the worst case, and Whirlpool assumes you will follow instructions.

If you want to keep the warranty conversation simple, read your own manual. Some brands name cast iron directly, and that sentence is the one that matters if you ever make a claim.

## The Three Ways Cast Iron Damages a Glass Cooktop

Each type of damage has a different cause and a different fix. Knowing which is which saves a lot of worry.

| Damage | What causes it | What it looks like | Can it be fixed? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Scratching | Rough casting, burrs, trapped grit | Fine grey swirls or dull patches | No, but it stays cosmetic |
| Cracking | Impact, a dropped pan, dragging weight | A straight line or a spider pattern | No, the glass panel is replaced |
| Pitting | Sugar or plastic melted on hot glass | Small shallow craters | No, and warranties usually exclude it |
| Heat marks | Residue scorched onto the glass | Brown rings, white haze | Usually yes, with cooktop cleaner |

Scratches are the common one, and they are the least serious. Cracks are rare and expensive. Pitting is the one people never see coming, which is why it gets its own section further down.

## Check Your Pan Base in Two Minutes

Before you change any habits, find out what you are working with. Modern mass-produced cast iron comes out of sand molds, and the finish varies a lot between pans and even between production runs.

Turn the pan over and run through these four checks.

1. **The fingernail test.** Drag a fingernail slowly across the base and around the outer rim. A catch or a click means a burr. That single high point is what cuts the glass.
2. **The paper towel test.** Wipe the base with a folded white paper towel. Snags and torn fibers mark the rough spots. Grey or black streaks mean loose seasoning and carbon, which also acts like grit.
3. **The flatness test.** Set a ruler or a metal straightedge across the base. A gap in the middle means the pan is bowed and will rock. Rocking concentrates weight on the rim.
4. **The rim check.** Look at the outside edge where the casting was parted. This is where burrs live, and it is also the part that touches the glass first.

Pans with a machined base pass all four easily. Older collectible pans often do too, because they were ground smooth at the factory. If you are curious about the difference, our [Griswold cast iron review](/blog/griswold-cast-iron-skillet-review/) covers how vintage pans were finished, and the [Stargazer skillet](/blog/stargazer-cast-iron-skillet/) is a current maker that machines the cooking surface.

Enameled pans are not automatically safe, either. The unglazed ring around the base of many enameled pots is bare, rough iron. Check a [Le Creuset skillet](/blog/le-creuset-cast-iron-skillet/) or a [Dutch oven](/blog/milo-dutch-oven-review/) the same way you would check bare cast iron.

## How to Smooth a Rough Cast Iron Base

If a pan fails the fingernail test, you have two options. Retire it from the glass cooktop, or take the burrs off. Smoothing is simple and takes about 20 minutes.

1. Strip the seasoning off the outside base with hot soapy water and a stiff brush. You want bare metal to work on.
2. Wrap medium-grit sandpaper around a sanding block. Somewhere around 80 to 120 grit removes burrs without eating the casting.
3. Work the outer rim first, then the flat base, in small circles. Keep the pressure light and even.
4. Stop often and repeat the fingernail test. You are removing high points, not reshaping the pan.
5. Finish with a finer grit if you want, then wash and dry the pan completely.
6. Re-season the bare metal. Thin coat of oil, wipe it almost dry, bake it upside down.

Do not sandblast or grind aggressively. That removes far more iron than you need to, and it leaves a porous surface that takes many rounds of seasoning to recover.

A bowed base is a different problem. Sanding will not flatten it, and a pan that rocks should stay off the glass. Use it in the oven or on a gas ring instead.

> **Quick habit worth building:** wipe the pan base and the glass with a dry cloth every single time, right before the pan goes down. Cooktop glass has a Knoop hardness of about 600, and common quartz sand sits higher on the hardness scale. One grain is enough to cut a line.

## Lift, Never Slide

This is the habit that matters most, and it is the one people break without noticing.

Sliding a pan across the glass does two things. It drags any trapped grit in a straight line, which is where those long fine scratches come from. It also pushes the pan's rim sideways under full weight, which is exactly the load the glass handles worst.

So build these into how you cook:

- Lift the pan with two hands, straight up and straight down. A cast iron handle plus a helper handle makes this easier than a single long handle.
- Set it down flat. Do not tip it onto one edge and drop the rest.
- If a pan needs repositioning, lift it. Nudging it two inches is still sliding.
- Never rest the pan half on and half off a burner while you reach for something.
- Use oven mitts you trust. Our guide to [heat resistant gloves](/blog/10-best-heat-resistant-gloves-for-cooking/) is worth a look, because a good grip is what stops a drop.

Stirring counts too. A heavy [metal spatula](/blog/best-metal-spatula-set/) pushed hard against the side of a skillet can shift the whole pan. Hold the handle with your other hand.

## Weight Limits, and Why Impact Matters More

GE Appliances publishes a number for this. Its range and cooktop glass surfaces are tested to a maximum weight limit of 50 pounds, and that figure covers radiant, induction and gas-on-glass tops.

Almost no home cast iron comes close. A 10-inch skillet full of food is nowhere near 50 pounds. A large Dutch oven full of stock gets closer, and a [big stockpot](/blog/best-stockpot-with-a-lid/) of water for pasta can too, so that is the one worth thinking about.

The real danger is not static weight. It is impact.

Weight spread evenly across a flat base is what the glass is designed for. A pan dropped from even a few inches lands on a rim, an edge or a burr, and all that force arrives at one small point in a fraction of a second. That is how cooktops crack. Cast iron is unforgiving here because it does not flex or dent. It transfers everything straight into the glass.

Two practical rules follow from that. Fill heavy pots on the cooktop rather than carrying them full from the sink. And keep [pots and pans stored](/blog/how-to-organize-pots-and-pans/) where you can reach them without lifting one over another, since the drops happen during the fumble, not the cooking.

## Preheat Gently, and Give It Time

Cast iron and radiant elements are a slightly awkward match, and rushing makes it worse.

A radiant element under glass is a coil that glows in a ring. It heats the glass, and the glass heats the pan. Cast iron conducts heat poorly, so that heat spreads sideways through the iron slowly. Crank the dial to high and you get a scorching ring with cool edges. The pan is also under uneven stress while that happens.

Maytag's advice is to heat cast iron gradually on low so the metal expands slowly. That is the right instinct.

- Start on medium or medium-low and wait five minutes before you judge it.
- Match the pan to the element size. An oversized pan traps heat under itself with nowhere to go.
- Expect everything to take longer, including cooling down. Turning a boil down to a simmer lags well behind the dial.
- Move the pan off the heat when you are done, not when the food is served. A hot pan left on cooling glass bakes residue into a ring.

That last point covers GE's other warning. Cast iron holds so much heat that the sensor under the glass can read high and cycle the element off. Your pan stops getting power and the food stalls. It is not a fault, and dropping the setting one notch usually clears it.

If you want fast, responsive heat on glass, a lighter pan wins. This is exactly why our guide to the [best wok for an electric stove](/blog/best-wok-for-electric-stove/) leans toward flat-bottomed carbon steel over cast iron. Thin steel reacts to the dial. Iron ignores it.

## The Damage Nobody Sees Coming: Spills Under a Hot Pan

Here is the one that costs people a cooktop, and it has nothing to do with scratching.

GE's [glass cooktop cleaning instructions](https://products.geappliances.com/appliance/gea-support-search-content?contentId=16244) warn that sugary spillovers and melted plastic can cause pitting of the surface. The page also states that this damage is not covered by the warranty unless the spill is removed while it is still hot. Pitting is a permanent crater in the glass.

Sugar is the problem because molten sugar chemically attacks the glass at cooking temperatures. Jam, syrup, a sticky glaze, a sugary braise boiling over. Any of those under a heavy pan can sit unnoticed for half an hour.

So here is the emergency routine, in order:

1. Turn the burner off.
2. Lift the pan away and set it somewhere safe.
3. Put on an oven mitt. The glass is still hot and you are about to work on it.
4. Use a cooktop razor scraper at a shallow angle to push the spill to a cool part of the glass.
5. Wipe it away with paper towels.
6. Let the rest cool, then clean normally.
7. Do not use that burner again until every trace is gone.

Melted plastic follows the same rules. Hardened plastic or porcelain that has cooled onto the glass often cannot be removed at all, and GE says that case needs the glass replaced.

**Prevention beats all of this.** Use a lid when something sugary might boil over, and keep an eye on anything reducing in a shallow pan. Cast iron makes the risk higher only because it hides the spill from you.

## Cleaning Without Adding New Scratches

Bad cleaning creates more scratches than bad cooking does.

The routine is short. Wait for the glass to cool. Squeeze a little cream cooktop cleaner on, spread it with a paper towel or the soft pad made for it, then buff off the residue with a dry cloth. Never heat the cooktop while cream cleaner is still on it.

For baked-on rings, a cooktop razor scraper held at a shallow angle lifts residue the cleaner cannot. Keep the blade flat, keep it moving, and stop as soon as the residue is gone. A [Cerama Bryte cooktop cleaning kit](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000MIWRTM/?tag=kitchenpot-20) pairs the cream with a scraper and pads in one box, which is the combination cooktop manuals usually describe. Owner reviews for these kits consistently note that the scraper does more work than the cream on burnt rings, so do not skip it. You can browse [what buyers say](https://www.amazon.com/product-reviews/B000MIWRTM/?tag=kitchenpot-20) before choosing a size.

Keep these away from the glass:

- Steel wool and green scouring pads.
- Powdered abrasive cleansers that work fine on [stainless steel pans](/blog/how-to-clean-stainless-steel-pans/) but are too harsh here.
- Ammonia and glass sprays, which streak on a hot surface.
- A dishcloth that has been anywhere near sand, salt or crumbs.

One more thing that transfers marks: a dirty pan bottom. Grease baked onto the underside of a pan cooks onto the glass as a brown ring every time. Wash pan bottoms properly and the cooktop stays cleaner. The same principle applies inside the dishwasher, which is why our guide to [cleaning a dishwasher](/blog/how-to-clean-a-dishwasher/) starts with the filter rather than the tub.

## Are Heat Diffusers and Cooktop Mats Worth It?

Short answer: no, not as protection.

A heat diffuser is a metal disc that sits between the element and the pan. The idea sounds sensible, and plenty of pages push it as the fix for cast iron on glass. The logic does not hold up.

- No major cooktop manufacturer lists a diffuser or a mat as an approved accessory. GE's approved cookware list names stainless steel, heavy-weight aluminum, copper-bottom, porcelain-enamel, carbon steel and titanium, with nothing about liners.
- A diffuser is still a rigid object resting on the glass, with its own edge and its own underside to trap grit.
- It adds a thermal barrier, so the cooktop works harder and you wait longer. That matters if you care about [energy-efficient cooking in a small kitchen](/blog/how-to-choose-energy-efficient-cookware-for-a-small-kitchen/).
- Silicone mats sold for cooktops are for covering a cold surface, not for cooking on. Heat can degrade them onto the glass.

A diffuser does have a real use, which is gentle simmering and protecting delicate pots on a fierce burner. That is not the same job as protecting your cooktop from a skillet. Clean bases and careful lifting do that job better and for free.

## When a Different Pan Is Simply the Better Answer

Cast iron is not the only way to get a good sear, and glass cooktops are happier with other materials.

- **Tri-ply stainless steel.** Flat, smooth, light enough to lift, and it responds to the dial. - **A good sauté pan.** For anything with liquid, a [sauté pan with a lid](/blog/best-saute-pan/) beats a skillet on glass because the lid stops boil-overs.
- **Carbon steel or nonstick for eggs.** Light, quick and forgiving. Our [nonstick pan guide](/blog/best-nonstick-pans-with-buying-guide/) and [ceramic cookware picks](/blog/best-ceramic-cookware-set/) both work fine on radiant glass.
- **A flat griddle.** A [griddle pan for pancakes](/blog/best-griddle-pan-for-pancakes/) spanning two elements is a common choice, but check your manual first, since some makers set a maximum span.

Here is how the common materials behave on a radiant glass top.

| Material | Risk to the glass | Heat response | Worth using on glass? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bare cast iron | High if the base is rough | Slow to heat, slow to change | Yes, once you check the base |
| Enameled cast iron | Medium, the bare foot ring is rough | Slow, same as bare iron | Yes, with the same checks |
| Tri-ply stainless | Low | Quick and even | Yes, the default choice |
| Heavy aluminum | Low | Fastest of the group | Yes |
| Carbon steel | Low if the base is flat | Quick, similar to aluminum | Yes |

## If the Glass Is Already Scratched or Cracked

Take a breath and work out which one you have.

**Fine scratches** are cosmetic. They do not spread, they do not affect heating, and no polish removes them. Cooktop cream fills them slightly and makes them less obvious. Live with them and protect the rest.

**A crack** is different. Stop using that burner immediately. A crack over a live element lets spills reach the wiring, and heat will open the crack further. Ceramic glass cannot be patched, so the panel gets replaced. On many ranges that part costs enough that a whole new appliance becomes the sensible comparison, which is where our guide to [slide-in electric ranges](/blog/best-slide-in-electric-range/) comes in.

**Pitting** sits in between. It is permanent, it usually is not covered, and it does not stop the cooktop working. Keep it clean and keep sugar off the hot glass from now on.

## The Habit That Actually Saves Cooktops

Everything here comes down to one small move. Before the pan goes down, wipe the base and wipe the glass.

Do that every time and the burrs you missed have nothing to grind against. Lift instead of sliding and the force never lands where it does damage. Cast iron and glass can live together for years on those two habits alone.

If you want a place to start, run the fingernail test on every cast iron pan you own tonight. You will know in five minutes which ones are already safe, and which one has been doing the damage. Then sort out where those pans live, because [storing pots and pans in a small kitchen](/blog/store-pots-and-pans-in-a-small-kitchen/) without stacking them badly is half the battle. A [pot rack](/blog/pots-and-pans-rack-reviews/) or a set of [stackable pots and pans](/blog/7-best-stackable-pots-and-pans/) keeps heavy iron off the top of a fragile pile.
