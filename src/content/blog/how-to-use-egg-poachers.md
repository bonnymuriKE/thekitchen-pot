A good poached egg is soft, tender and sitting on buttered toast in under ten minutes. Making one the traditional way, though, involves a pot of simmering water, a lot of nerves, and wispy egg whites floating everywhere. That's why egg poachers exist.

The trouble is that egg poachers come in four quite different styles: stovetop pans with little cups, silicone pods, microwave poachers and electric egg cookers. Each works a bit differently, and the most common complaints, like eggs that stick, overcook or come out watery, usually come from using one like another.

This guide shows you how to use an egg poacher of every type, with times for runny, jammy and firm yolks, fixes for the usual problems, and tips for storing one when your kitchen is short on space.

> **Quick answer:** Grease each cup lightly with butter or oil, bring about half an inch of water to a boil in the poacher pan, then lower it to a gentle simmer. Crack an egg into each cup, cover the pan, and cook for about 3 1/2 to 6 minutes, depending on how set you like the yolk. Lift the cups out and slide each egg onto toast with a small spatula or spoon.

## What Is an Egg Poacher and How Does It Work?

An egg poacher holds each egg in its own small cup while heat cooks it gently. That keeps the white in a neat round shape instead of spreading out in the water.

Strictly speaking, most poachers steam eggs rather than poach them. A traditional poached egg is cooked directly in simmering water. In a poacher, the egg sits in a cup above or on the water, and steam trapped under the lid does most of the work. The texture is a little firmer and more even, like a very soft-boiled egg without the shell, and the shape is always tidy.

Here are the four main types:

- **Stovetop poacher pans.** A shallow pan with a removable tray that holds two to six cups. Water goes in the base, the tray sits on top, and a lid traps the steam.
- **Silicone poaching cups.** Flexible cups that float on simmering water in any pot or skillet you already own.
- **Microwave egg poachers.** Plastic trays with a vented lid that cook one or two eggs in about a minute.
- **Electric egg cookers.** Countertop appliances that steam eggs with a measured amount of water and turn off on their own. Many include a poaching tray.

## How to Use a Stovetop Egg Poacher Pan

This is the classic design and the easiest way to make several poached eggs at once. These steps follow the instructions from Eggssentials, which makes poacher pans.

1. **Add about half an inch of water** to the base of the pan. The water should sit below the cups, not touch them. Steam, not water, cooks the eggs.
2. **Grease the cups.** Coat each one with a little butter or nonstick spray. A thin layer is enough.
3. **Bring the water to a gentle boil**, then set the tray with the cups in place and lower the heat to a low simmer. A rolling boil can make water spill over into the cups.
4. **Crack an egg into each cup.** For fewer broken yolks, crack each egg into a small bowl first, then tip it into the cup.
5. **Put the lid on.** Eggssentials says the eggs cook unevenly and take much longer without the cover.
6. **Cook for 3 1/2 to 6 minutes.** Start checking at 3 1/2 minutes. The white should be fully set and opaque, and the yolk should look filmy on top.
7. **Lift out the tray.** Use an oven mitt and the knob in the center of the tray. Run a small spatula or spoon around each egg and slide it onto your plate.

Time your first batch carefully and note what you like. Eggssentials points out that even 15 seconds changes how firm the yolk is, and every stove runs a little differently.

**Tip for a smoother finish:** some cooks add a small pat of butter to each cup before the egg. It gives a richer taste and helps the egg release. If you only use spray, the egg will taste cleaner and closer to a true poached egg.

## How to Use Silicone Egg Poaching Cups

Silicone cups are cheap, small and work in any pot or skillet that has a lid, which makes them popular in small kitchens. The PoachPod, one of the original floating designs, is heat resistant to 675°F and safe in the microwave and dishwasher.

1. **Fill a pan with water** deep enough for the cups to float freely, with room at the top so the lid still closes. A deep skillet or wide saucepan works well.
2. **Bring the water to a boil, then lower it to a gentle simmer.**
3. **Brush each cup with oil or melted butter.** Silicone is nonstick, but a thin coat helps the egg slide out.
4. **Crack an egg into each cup** and gently set the cups on the water.
5. **Cover the pan** and keep the heat low.
6. **Cook to your liking.** The recipe site Olga's Flavor Factory suggests 4 to 5 minutes for runny yolks, 6 to 8 minutes for soft, jammy yolks, and up to 10 minutes for yolks cooked all the way through.
7. **Lift the cups out** with tongs or a slotted spoon, holding the rim. Turn each cup upside down over your plate and gently push the bottom so the egg pops out. If it sticks, slide a thin spatula between the egg and the silicone.

Silicone cups take a little longer than a metal poacher pan because silicone doesn't conduct heat as well as steel. Keep the lid on so the top of the egg cooks through.

## How to Use a Microwave Egg Poacher

A microwave poacher is the fastest option and a good one if you don't have a stove, or you're making one egg at work or in a dorm. These steps come from Nordic Ware's care and use instructions for its two-cup microwave poacher.

1. **Crack an egg into each cup** and add 1/2 teaspoon of water to each cup with the egg. If you're only cooking one egg, add water to the empty cup too.
2. **Prick each yolk gently with a toothpick.** This lets steam escape so the yolk doesn't burst and spatter.
3. **Put the cover on.**
4. **Cook in 15 to 20 second bursts** until the egg is done the way you like it.
5. **Let it stand, covered, for 30 seconds.** The eggs keep cooking during this time.
6. **Remove the cover with a hot pad**, tilting it away from you to avoid the steam.

Nordic Ware gives these approximate total cooking times:

| Number of eggs | 1,000-watt microwave | 600-watt microwave |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 egg | About 40 seconds | About 70 seconds |
| 2 eggs | About 60 seconds | About 100 seconds |

Microwaves vary a lot, so use those numbers as a starting point. Nordic Ware also says not to preheat the poacher and never use it on the stovetop. If your microwave doesn't have a turntable, rotate the poacher halfway through.

Microwave eggs can overcook in seconds. Stop early, check, and add another short burst if needed.

## How to Poach Eggs in an Electric Egg Cooker

Electric egg cookers are best known for hard-boiled eggs, but most come with a poaching tray. The Dash Rapid Egg Cooker is one of the best-known examples. The steps below follow Dash's manual for its Rapid Egg Cooker.

1. **Fill the measuring cup to the "Poached" line** with cold water and pour it onto the heating plate.
2. **Set the poaching tray** on top of the base.
3. **Lightly grease the poaching tray** before adding the eggs.
4. **Crack the eggs into the tray**, put the lid on, and switch it on.
5. **Let it run.** The cooker shuts off automatically when it's done.
6. **Lift the lid carefully**, since the steam is hot, and slide the eggs out.

The measured water is what sets the cooking time, so don't guess. If you want softer or firmer yolks than the default, adjust the water in small amounts and note what works. ## Egg Poacher Problems and How to Fix Them

Most poacher troubles have a quick fix once you know the cause.

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Eggs stick to the cups | Cups not greased | Coat each cup with butter, oil or spray every time |
| Top of the egg still runny and clear | Lid off, or heat too low | Keep the lid on, raise the heat slightly |
| Yolks hard, whites rubbery | Cooked too long or water boiling hard | Lower to a gentle simmer, check at 3 1/2 minutes |
| Water bubbling into the cups | Too much water, or heat too high | Use about 1/2 inch of water, reduce to a simmer |
| Yolk bursts in the microwave | Yolk not pricked or bursts too long | Prick the yolk, cook in 15 to 20 second bursts |
| Watery pool around the egg | Older egg with thin whites | Use the freshest eggs you have |
| Silicone cups tip over | Water too shallow or boiling hard | Add more water, keep it at a gentle simmer |

## Are Poached Eggs From a Poacher Safe to Eat?

Poached eggs are often served with a runny yolk, and that's worth thinking about for a moment. The FDA advises cooking eggs until both the yolk and the white are firm, because undercooked eggs can carry Salmonella. It notes that young children, older adults, pregnant women and people with weakened immune systems are at greater risk of serious illness.

If you or someone you're cooking for is in one of those groups, cook the yolks until set. For everyone who loves a soft yolk, the FDA suggests using shell eggs that have been pasteurized to destroy Salmonella. Look for the word "pasteurized" on the carton.

The FDA also recommends keeping raw eggs in the fridge at 40°F or below. Leftover cooked egg dishes should be refrigerated and eaten within 3 to 4 days, though poached eggs are best eaten right away.

## How to Clean an Egg Poacher

Cooked egg white is sticky, so clean your poacher soon after you use it.

- **Stovetop poacher pans.** Soak the cups in warm soapy water for a few minutes, then wash with a soft sponge. Eggssentials says its pans are dishwasher safe but recommends hand washing and drying to keep the steel shiny. For white spots from hard water, it suggests a mix of water and vinegar. Our guide on [how to clean stainless steel pans](/blog/how-to-clean-stainless-steel-pans/) covers the same fix in more detail.
- **Nonstick cups.** Use a soft sponge only. Scouring pads will wear through the coating. If you're not sure how to care for coated cookware, here's what you need to know about [nonstick cookware](/blog/what-is-nonstick-cookware/).
- **Silicone cups.** PoachPods are dishwasher safe. If a greasy film builds up, soak them in hot water with a little dish soap.
- **Microwave poachers.** Nordic Ware recommends hand washing, soaking first for stuck-on egg, and avoiding steel wool, harsh abrasives and scouring powders.
- **Electric egg cookers.** Wash the tray and lid, and wipe the heating plate. Dash recommends a 10:1 water-to-vinegar solution for mineral buildup on the plate. It's the same kind of scale you'll see in a kettle, and our guide on [how to clean an electric kettle](/blog/how-to-clean-an-electric-kettle/) covers descaling in more depth.

## Poaching Eggs When You Cook for One

A four-cup poacher pan is overkill when you only want one or two eggs. You can still use it: just cook in two cups and leave the others empty. But smaller tools make more sense day to day.

Two silicone cups in a small saucepan with a lid is about the most compact setup there is. It also uses less water and less energy than heating a big pan. ## Can You Poach Eggs Without a Poacher?

Yes. The traditional method just needs a saucepan and a slotted spoon. The Incredible Egg recommends 2 to 3 inches of water in a large saucepan or deep skillet, brought to a boil and then turned down to a gentle simmer. Break each egg into a small cup or saucer, slip it into the water, and cook for 3 to 5 minutes until the white is set and the yolk starts to thicken. Lift it out with a slotted spoon and drain.

It also says adding vinegar or salt to the water isn't necessary and can flavor the eggs. Serious Eats' Food Lab recommends straining each egg through a fine-mesh strainer first to remove the thin, watery part of the white, which gives you neater eggs with fewer wisps.

## The Bottom Line on Using an Egg Poacher

Learning how to use an egg poacher mostly comes down to three habits: grease the cups every time, keep the water at a gentle simmer instead of a hard boil, and keep the lid on. Start timing at 3 1/2 minutes on the stovetop, 40 seconds in the microwave, or follow the water line on an electric cooker, then adjust by small amounts until your yolks come out exactly how you like them.

Pick the type that suits your kitchen: silicone cups for tiny spaces, a poacher pan for feeding a few people, a microwave poacher if you don't have a stove, and an electric cooker if you want to walk away while breakfast cooks.
