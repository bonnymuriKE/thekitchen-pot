An Instant Pot uses 700 to 1500 watts. The exact figure depends on the size of the pot and the model, and Instant Pot prints it on the base of every unit.

Most 6-quart pots, the size in the majority of kitchens, are rated 1000 watts. The 3-quart Mini drops to 700. Air fryer models climb to 1500.

That number is a ceiling, though, not a reading. The pot only pulls its full rating while it is heating, so what it costs you to run is a different question. Both answers are below.

## Instant Pot Wattage by Model and Size

Every figure here comes from the power specification Instant Pot publishes on its own product pages, listed as watts at 120 volts and 60 hertz.

| Model | Size | Rated watts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Duo Mini | 3 quart | 700 W |
| Duo | 6 quart | 1,000 W |
| Duo Plus | 6 quart | 1,000 W |
| Rio | 6 quart | 1,000 W |
| Pro | 6 quart | 1,200 W |
| Pro Plus | 6 quart | 1,200 W |
| Duo | 8 quart | 1,200 W |
| Duo Crisp + Air Fryer | 6 quart | 1,500 W |
| Duo Crisp + Air Fryer | 8 quart | 1,500 W |

Three patterns fall out of that table.

**Bigger pot, more watts.** A larger inner pot holds more food and more liquid, so it needs a stronger element to get there in a sensible time. The jump from 6 quarts to 8 quarts adds 200 watts on the Duo line.

**Faster pot, more watts.** The Pro and Pro Plus stay at 6 quarts but move up to 1,200 watts. That extra power shortens the wait before the pot reaches pressure.

**Air frying is the big jump.** The [Duo Crisp models](/blog/instant-pot-vs-ninja-foodi/) are rated 1,500 watts because crisping needs hot moving air rather than hot water. That is a different job, and it costs more power. The same gap shows up between a pressure cooker and a countertop fryer in our list of the [best air fryers under $100](/blog/best-air-fryers-under-100/).

If you want the spec for your own unit, flip it over. The rating label on the base carries the watts, the volts and the frequency. Instant Pot's [6-quart Duo product page](https://instantpot.com/products/instant-pot-duo-6qt-multi-use-pressure-cooker) shows the same figure in the same format, so the two should match.

Not sure which model you have, or what all the buttons do? Our explainer on [what an Instant Pot is](/blog/what-is-an-instant-pot/) walks through the lineup, and [Instant Pot vs pressure cooker](/blog/instant-pot-vs-pressure-cooker/) covers how these differ from the stovetop kind.

## Why the Label Wattage Is a Ceiling, Not a Reading

Here is the part most wattage articles skip, and it is the part that decides your bill.

An Instant Pot has one heating element in the base. That element has two states: on and off. There is no dial that feeds it 40 percent power.

So the pot draws its full rated wattage whenever the element is on, and close to nothing when it is off. What changes over a cook cycle is the share of time it spends on.

At the start, the element runs almost constantly. It is heating the metal pot, the food and the liquid, and turning some of that liquid to steam. Once the pot seals and pressure builds, the job changes. Now it only needs to replace the small amount of heat escaping through the walls and lid.

That is why owners who put a meter on a pot see the same pattern every time. A hard pull during heat-up, then short bursts that get further apart. Anyone running one off a battery bank, in an RV for example, ends up reporting the same thing.

The practical result: a 1,000-watt pot almost never averages 1,000 watts across a cook. The longer the pressure-cook time, the lower the average goes. Same idea behind the advice in our guide to [reducing kitchen energy use in a small apartment](/blog/how-to-reduce-kitchen-energy-use-in-a-small-apartment/): what matters is how long the heat stays on, not the number on the sticker.

## Watts to Kilowatt-Hours: The Only Math You Need

Your power company bills you in kilowatt-hours, not watts. Converting takes one line.

**Kilowatt-hours = watts ÷ 1,000 × hours run**

A kilowatt-hour is 1,000 watts held for one hour. So a 1,000-watt Instant Pot running flat out for an hour uses exactly 1 kWh. A 700-watt Mini doing the same uses 0.7 kWh.

Then multiply by your rate:

**Cost = kilowatt-hours × your price per kWh**

Find your price on your latest bill. Look for a line that reads cents per kWh or a total divided by usage. The US residential average sits near 18 cents per kWh, going by the [Energy Information Administration's short-term outlook](https://www.eia.gov/outlooks/steo/tables/pdf/7ctab.pdf), which puts the 2026 forecast at 18.2 cents.

Every number below uses 18 cents. Swap in your own rate and the pattern holds.

## What One Cook Cycle Actually Costs

Take a 6-quart Duo at 1,000 watts. Say you brown meat on Sauté for 10 minutes, then pressure cook for 45 minutes, and the pot takes about 15 minutes to reach pressure in between. The whole thing runs about 70 minutes.

**The ceiling:** 1,000 W ÷ 1,000 × 70/60 hours = 1.17 kWh. At 18 cents, that is **21 cents**.

That figure assumes the element never switches off, which it certainly does. Your real cost lands under it, and probably well under, because the element is only working hard for the first 25 minutes or so.

Scale that up. Cook like this five nights a week and the ceiling is about $4.20 a month. The real figure is lower. Either way, it is not the line item pushing your bill up.

Here is the same ceiling math across the range of ratings.

| Rated watts | 30 minutes | 1 hour | 2 hours | 1 hour a day, 30 days |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 700 W | 6.3¢ | 12.6¢ | 25.2¢ | $3.78 |
| 1,000 W | 9.0¢ | 18.0¢ | 36.0¢ | $5.40 |
| 1,200 W | 10.8¢ | 21.6¢ | 43.2¢ | $6.48 |
| 1,500 W | 13.5¢ | 27.0¢ | 54.0¢ | $8.10 |

*All figures at 18 cents per kWh, assuming the element runs constantly. Real use costs less.*

Read those as the worst case. A slow-cook session is the closest thing to it, since the pot spends hours nudging the temperature along, and even then the element rests between nudges.

## Instant Pot vs Oven vs Stovetop

This is the comparison that makes the wattage meaningful, because the pot rarely replaces nothing. It replaces something bigger.

A full-size electric range runs on a 240-volt circuit, not a regular outlet. A common GE replacement bake element is [rated 3,410 watts at 240 volts](https://www.therm-coil.com/products/model-tc-44t10011-ge-wb44t10011-range-oven-bake-replacement-element-3-410w-240v). That is more than three times a 1,000-watt Instant Pot, before you count the broil element.

The gap gets wider once you think about where the heat goes. An oven heats a large air-filled box, the racks, the walls and the door, and it does that for 10 to 15 minutes before the food even goes in. Much of that heat then leaves through the vent and into your kitchen.

A pressure cooker heats a few quarts of food inside a sealed pot. The steam it makes stays in and does the cooking instead of escaping. Nothing is warming your ceiling. Try the same stew in an open [stockpot](/blog/best-stockpot-with-a-lid/) and you will watch that steam leave the room.

| Appliance | Typical supply | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Instant Pot | 120 V wall outlet, 700 to 1,500 W | Heats a small sealed volume, steam stays in |
| Electric oven | 240 V circuit, bake element about 3,400 W | Heats a large cavity, plus preheat, plus vent losses |
| Electric cooktop burner | 120 V or 240 V, varies by burner | Open pan, steam and heat escape all around |
| Microwave | 120 V outlet | Very quick, but poor at braises and beans |

For dishes that would otherwise need a long oven braise, the pot wins outright. For a jacket potato it does not, and a [microwave](/blog/7-best-over-the-range-microwaves/) beats both. Our roundup of [small ovens and toaster ovens for baking in small kitchens](/blog/best-small-ovens-and-toaster-ovens-for-baking-in-small-kitchens/) covers the middle ground, and [energy-efficient cookware choices](/blog/how-to-choose-energy-efficient-cookware-for-a-small-kitchen/) matter on the stovetop too.

If you cook on gas, the comparison changes again. Electricity is billed per kWh and gas per therm, so the two do not line up directly. The pot still wins on wasted heat, since a gas flame licks around the outside of a pan. An [induction cooktop](/blog/best-induction-cooktop-for-the-money/) closes that gap on the stovetop, because it heats the pan itself rather than the air around it.

## Does the Function Change How Much It Uses?

Peak draw does not change, because there is one element and one rating. Run time does, and that is what shows up on the bill.

- **Sauté** holds the element on almost constantly. The lid is off, so heat escapes as fast as it arrives. This is the hungriest setting per minute.
- **Pressure cook** is hardest at the start, then settles. Longer cooks average lower than short ones.
- **Slow cook** uses a low, long duty cycle. Less per minute than sauté, but over six hours it can total more.
- **Keep Warm** barely registers. It holds food below a simmer using short pulses of the element.
- **Air fry**, on Duo Crisp models, runs the 1,500-watt element plus a fan, and it does not seal. This is the most demanding mode.
- **Yogurt** is a long, very gentle hold, so the total stays small despite the hours.
- **Rice** finishes fast, and a pressure cook beats a dedicated machine on time. Our walkthrough on [cooking rice in a rice cooker](/blog/how-to-cook-rice-in-a-rice-cooker/) covers the slower route.

One more thing worth knowing: the display and standby electronics draw a tiny trickle whenever the pot is plugged in. It is small enough that it will not show on a bill, but unplugging between uses is free to do. The same logic applies to the other counter appliances in our guide to [eco-friendly alternatives to common kitchen appliances](/blog/eco-friendly-alternatives-to-common-kitchen-appliances/).

## Can You Plug an Instant Pot Into an Extension Cord?

Instant Pot's own answer is no, and it is not subtle about it. The Pro Plus [user manual](https://www.williams-sonoma.com/netstorage/pdf/Instant_Pot_Pro_Plus_6Qt_White_Fluted_112_5678_01_User_Manual.pdf) states "DO NOT use extension cord," and adds that you should never drape the cord over a counter edge or use an outlet below the counter.

Two reasons sit behind that.

The first is heat. A 1,000-watt pot pulls a little over 8 amps at 120 volts, and a 1,500-watt model pulls about 12.5. Thin, cheap cords are often rated for less than that, and an overloaded cord warms up along its whole length.

The second is the trip hazard. The cord is short on purpose so a pot full of boiling liquid cannot be dragged off a counter.

If the outlet is out of reach, the fix is an outlet, not a cord. Rearranging the counter is usually easier than it sounds, and our [countertop organization ideas for a small kitchen](/blog/countertop-organization-ideas-for-a-small-kitchen/) cover how to free up a spot near power. Where you park the pot between uses matters too, which is why we included multi-cookers in our [vertical storage ideas for small kitchens](/blog/vertical-storage-ideas-for-small-kitchens/).

## What Circuit Load Means in a Small Kitchen

Watts matter for a second reason that has nothing to do with money. They decide what else you can run at the same time.

A standard US kitchen outlet sits on a 15-amp or 20-amp, 120-volt circuit. Multiply volts by amps and you get the limit:

- 15 amps × 120 volts = **1,800 watts**
- 20 amps × 120 volts = **2,400 watts**

Electricians also leave headroom on a circuit rather than loading it to the last watt, so treat about 80 percent of those numbers as the working ceiling. That is roughly 1,440 watts on a 15-amp circuit.

Now put a 1,500-watt Duo Crisp on that circuit. It has taken the whole working ceiling by itself. Add a toaster or a [kettle](/blog/how-do-electric-kettles-work/) on the same circuit and the breaker trips.

A few habits keep that from happening:

1. Learn which outlets share a breaker. Flip one breaker off and walk the kitchen to see what dies.
2. Put the heaviest two appliances on different circuits. Pot and kettle, or pot and air fryer.
3. Watch the counter in a studio or apartment, where several outlets often share one run.
4. Never daisy-chain power strips for cooking appliances.

Renters get stuck with whatever wiring exists, so planning around it beats fighting it. ## How to Measure Your Own Pot

Every number above is arithmetic on a rated figure. If you want the real one for your kitchen, measure it. It takes one cheap gadget and one meal.

A plug-in energy meter sits between the outlet and the appliance and totals kilowatt-hours as they are used. The [P3 Kill A Watt](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00009MDBU/?tag=kitchenpot-20) is the one most people buy, and [owner reviews](https://www.amazon.com/product-reviews/B00009MDBU/?tag=kitchenpot-20) show it is popular for precisely this kind of question.

How to use it:

1. Plug the meter into the wall, then the Instant Pot into the meter.
2. Reset the kWh counter to zero.
3. Cook a meal you make often, start to finish.
4. Read the kWh total at the end.
5. Multiply by your rate per kWh from your bill.

Do it once and you will stop guessing. Most people are surprised how low the number is, and the meter then goes on to answer the same question for the fridge, the [rice cooker](/blog/best-rice-cooker-for-brown-rice/) and anything else on the counter.

## Ways to Bring the Number Down

None of these will transform your bill, but they all work and none costs anything.

- **Start with hot tap water.** Cold water is the single biggest thing the element has to heat. Hot water shortens the climb to pressure.
- **Use the smallest pot that fits the meal.** A 3-quart Mini heating a one-person dinner beats an 8-quart doing the same job. Our guide to the [best cookware sizes for cooking for one](/blog/best-cookware-sizes-for-cooking-for-one/) applies the same reasoning.
- **Do not overfill.** More liquid means more to heat, and pressure cookers have a max fill line for safety anyway.
- **Choose natural release when the recipe allows.** The pot finishes cooking on stored heat with the element off.
- **Cook in batches.** One larger cook beats three small ones, since you only pay the heat-up cost once. Our [meal prep ideas for one person](/blog/easy-meal-prep-ideas-for-one-person-in-a-small-kitchen/) are built around that.
- **Skip Keep Warm when you are eating right away.** It is cheap, but it is not free.
- **Keep the sealing ring in good shape.** A worn ring lets steam escape, so the element works harder to hold pressure.

A [steamer basket](/blog/best-steamer-basket-for-instant-pot/) helps here as well, because stacking two foods in one cycle means one heat-up instead of two.

## Which Size Should You Buy If Power Matters?

If you are choosing a pot and the wattage figure is part of the decision, size is the lever you actually control.

| You cook for | Size to buy | Rated watts | The trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One person | 3 quart | 700 W | Too small for a whole chicken |
| Two to four | 6 quart | 1,000 to 1,200 W | The size most recipes assume |
| Five or more, or batch cooking | 8 quart | 1,200 W | Heavy, and takes real cupboard space |
| Anyone who wants crisping too | 6 or 8 quart Duo Crisp | 1,500 W | Two lids to store, highest draw |

The 6-quart is the default for good reason. Nearly every recipe online is written for it, and 1,000 watts is modest for what it does. The [Duo 6-quart](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00FLYWNYQ/?tag=kitchenpot-20) remains the plain version of that, and the newer [Rio 6-quart](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0B4PQDFCL/?tag=kitchenpot-20) carries the same 1,000-watt rating with an updated panel.

Solo cooks are the real exception. If most meals are for one, the 3-quart at 700 watts heats faster and stores easier. It earns a spot in our guide to the [best small kitchen appliances for cooking for one](/blog/best-small-kitchen-appliances-for-cooking-for-one/) for both reasons. Just check that your recipes scale down, since minimum liquid rules still apply. A small pot also frees a shelf, which helps if you are working through [a kitchen with no pantry](/blog/how-to-organize-a-small-kitchen-with-no-pantry/).

## What This Means for Your Bill

Wattage tells you how fast an appliance can pull power. It does not tell you what it costs, because time does that.

An Instant Pot is rated high and runs briefly, and it only runs flat out for part of that. So it sits among the cheaper things in your kitchen to operate, despite a number on the base that looks large next to a [blender](/blog/what-is-the-difference-between-a-food-processor-and-a-blender/) or a toaster.

Put the meter on your fridge instead. That one runs every hour of every day, and it is where the savings hide.
