Good small kitchen lighting ideas start with one fact: a single ceiling light is never enough. It lights the floor and leaves your counters in your own shadow.

The fix is not more fixtures everywhere. It is the right light in three places, with the right bulbs. Below is our full plan, with the numbers you need and the plug-in options that work in a rental.

**The short version:** Light a small kitchen in three layers: a flush ceiling fixture for general light, LED strips under the upper cabinets for the counters, and one small accent light. Aim for about 30 to 40 lumens per square foot overall and more on the counters. Use the same color temperature everywhere, 2700K to 3000K, with a CRI of 90 or higher. Renters can do almost all of this with plug-in and rechargeable lights.

## What Is the Best Lighting for a Small Kitchen?

The best lighting for a small kitchen is layered LED light. Layering means you use different lights for different jobs instead of asking one fixture to do everything.

There are three layers:

- **Ambient light** fills the whole room. This is your ceiling fixture or recessed lights. It lets you see the floor, open the fridge and find a pan.
- **Task light** shines on the spots where you work: the counter, the sink and the stove. In a small kitchen, this is mostly under-cabinet lighting.
- **Accent light** adds depth. A puck light in a glass cabinet, a strip above the upper cabinets or a small lamp on an open shelf. It is optional, but it makes a tiny room feel finished.

Layering matters because your ceiling light sits behind you when you face the counter. Your body blocks it, so you chop in your own shadow. Task light comes from in front of you and fixes that.

Layered light also makes the room look bigger, because lit corners stop reading as walls closing in. We cover the other tricks in [how to make a small kitchen look bigger](/blog/make-a-small-kitchen-look-bigger/).

## How Many Lights Do I Need in a Small Kitchen?

Count lumens, not fixtures. A lumen is a measure of how much light a bulb gives off. Watts only tell you how much power it uses. The box on every LED bulb lists both.

Our rule of thumb for kitchens:

- **General light:** about 30 to 40 lumens per square foot of floor.
- **Counter and sink:** about 70 to 80 lumens per square foot of work surface, roughly double the room.

To find your square footage, multiply the length of the room by the width in feet. An 8-by-10-foot kitchen is 80 square feet. At 30 to 40 lumens each, it needs about 2,400 to 3,200 lumens from the ceiling.

For a sense of scale, an old 60-watt bulb gives about 800 lumens. A 100-watt bulb gives about 1,600. LED versions of each use only around 8 to 15 watts.

| Kitchen size | Floor area | Ceiling light (lumens) | What that looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 x 7 ft (tiny galley or studio nook) | 35 sq ft | 1,000 to 1,400 | One flush fixture with a 1,200-lumen LED |
| 6 x 8 ft | 48 sq ft | 1,500 to 1,900 | One flush fixture, or two small ones |
| 8 x 10 ft | 80 sq ft | 2,400 to 3,200 | One large flush fixture or four 4-inch recessed lights |
| 10 x 10 ft | 100 sq ft | 3,000 to 4,000 | Four to six 4-inch recessed lights, or two fixtures |
| 10 x 12 ft | 120 sq ft | 3,600 to 4,800 | Six recessed lights, or a fixture plus a track |

Then add under-cabinet lights on every counter run. Those are on top of the ceiling numbers above.

Go a little higher if your cabinets or walls are dark, since dark surfaces soak up light. Go a little lower if everything is white. Put the ceiling lights on a dimmer, and you can always turn bright light down at night.

## Pick the Right Color Temperature

Color temperature is how warm or cool the light looks. It is measured in kelvins, written as K. Lower numbers look warm and yellow, like a candle. Higher numbers look cool and blue, like an overcast sky.

| Color temperature | How it looks | Where we use it |
|---|---|---|
| 2700K | Warm, soft, like a classic bulb | Kitchens open to a living room, warm wood or cream cabinets |
| 3000K | Warm white, clean but still cozy | Our default for most small kitchens |
| 3500K | Neutral white | White or gray kitchens that need a crisper look |
| 4000K | Cool white, bright and sharp | Garages and workrooms; feels clinical in most homes |
| 5000K and up | Bluish daylight | Skip it at home; skin and food look flat |

We recommend 3000K for most small kitchens. It is bright enough for prep work but still feels like home. If your kitchen opens straight into your living room, 2700K blends better with the lamps there.

The bigger rule is to match. Use the same color temperature in every fixture, including the under-cabinet strips and the bulb in the range hood. A warm ceiling light with cool blue strips under the cabinets makes the kitchen look patchy. The eye sees two rooms instead of one.

Check the number before you buy, because many LED strips default to 4000K or daylight.

## CRI Explained Simply

CRI stands for color rendering index. It is a score from 0 to 100 that tells you how true colors look under a light. Sunlight scores 100.

A cheap LED can read 80 on that scale. Under it, red tomatoes look dull, raw chicken looks gray and your paint color looks muddy. At 90 or higher, food looks like food.

This matters most in a kitchen, because you judge doneness by color. Is the steak still pink? Are the onions golden or burnt? Good CRI makes those calls easier.

On the box, look for **CRI 90 or higher** on every kitchen bulb and strip. If it lists **R9**, a score for deep reds like meat and tomatoes, look for 50 or more. Buy matching fixtures from one brand, since two bulbs both labeled 3000K can still look slightly different.

## Small Kitchen Lighting Ceiling Ideas

The ceiling layer does the general lighting. In a small kitchen, it should be bright, low-profile and out of the way.

### Flush and Semi-Flush Fixtures

A flush fixture sits tight against the ceiling. A semi-flush fixture hangs a few inches below it on a short stem. With a standard 8-foot ceiling, we pick flush every time. It keeps the ceiling looking higher and clears tall heads and cabinet doors.

To size a ceiling fixture, add the length and width of the room in feet. The answer is roughly the right diameter in inches. An 8-by-10-foot kitchen adds up to 18, so look for a fixture about 16 to 18 inches across. A 6-by-8 kitchen needs about 14 inches.

Choose a fixture with a frosted or opal glass cover. It spreads light evenly and hides the bright LED dots. Clear glass with an exposed bulb creates glare in a small room.

### Recessed Lights

Recessed lights, also called can lights, sit inside the ceiling. They are the cleanest look for a small kitchen, but they need wiring, so they are an owner project.

In a small kitchen, use 4-inch lights rather than 6-inch ones. They look less busy on a small ceiling. Space them about 4 feet apart on an 8-foot ceiling. A simple rule is to space them about half the ceiling height.

Place them over the front edge of the counter, about 24 to 30 inches out from the wall. That way the light lands on your work surface, not behind your head. A row down the center of the room puts you back in your own shadow.

Many owners now use slim LED wafer lights instead of old cans. They fit in a thin ceiling and need no housing above it.

### Track and Rail Lights

A track light is a single fixture with several heads you can aim. It is a good choice when you have one ceiling box in the middle of the room but the counters are along the walls. One track can send light to the sink, the stove and the prep counter.

### Old Fluorescent Boxes

Many rentals still have a long fluorescent box on the ceiling. Swap the tubes for LED tubes labeled plug-and-play, meaning they work with the existing ballast. Tubes that need the ballast bypassed are a job for an electrician.

## Under-Cabinet Lighting in a Small Kitchen

If you add only one light to a small kitchen, make it this one. Under-cabinet lights put bright, even light right on the counter, where you chop, measure and read recipes.

### Where to Mount It

Mount the strip toward the front edge of the cabinet bottom, about 1 to 2 inches back from the face. Do not put it against the wall.

Front mounting spreads light across the full depth of a 24-inch counter. Back mounting blasts the backsplash and leaves the front half dim. It also creates hot spots and glare on glossy tile.

Most upper cabinets have a lip, a small strip of wood that hangs below the cabinet bottom. Tuck the light just behind it so you never see the bulbs when you stand at the counter.

### How Long and How Bright

Run the lights the full length of each counter run. A strip that covers only half the counter leaves the other half looking dim by comparison.

Use these numbers when you shop:

| Detail | Our target |
|---|---|
| Strip length | Within a few inches of the full cabinet width |
| Brightness | About 200 to 400 lumens per foot of strip |
| Puck light spacing | One puck every 16 to 20 inches |
| Mount position | 1 to 2 inches behind the cabinet front |
| Color temperature | Match the ceiling, usually 3000K |
| CRI | 90 or higher |
| Strip type | Diffused, with a frosted cover |

A diffused strip has a frosted cover over the LEDs. It gives one smooth line of light. Bare LED tape shows a row of bright dots, and those dots reflect in shiny counters like a string of beads.

Puck lights leave scallops of shadow between them, so we save them for inside cabinets.

### Does Under-Cabinet Lighting Make a Kitchen Look Bigger?

Yes, it does. The backsplash is often the darkest wall in a small kitchen, because the upper cabinets block the ceiling light. Light that wall, and it seems to move back. The counter reads as deeper and the room feels wider.

Under-cabinet light works best on a clear counter. If the counter is crowded, you are just lighting clutter. ## How Do I Light a Kitchen With No Island?

Most small kitchens have no island. That means your light belongs over the counters around the edge of the room. The center of the floor needs the least light, since you only walk through it.

Here is what we do in a kitchen with no island:

1. **Light every counter run from below the cabinets.** This is your main work light.
2. **Aim the ceiling light at the counters.** Use recessed lights over the counter edges, or a track light with heads pointed at each work zone.
3. **Put one small light over the sink.** If there is no upper cabinet there, a small pendant or a flush light does the job.
4. **Light the stove with the range hood.** Swap its bulbs for bright LEDs.
5. **Add a lamp or pendant over the table, if you have one.** A peninsula or a small dining table is the one place a pendant belongs.

If you have a peninsula or a bar counter, it can take one or two small pendants. Keep them small, about 8 to 12 inches across. Hang the bottom 30 to 36 inches above the counter. Space two pendants about 24 to 30 inches apart.

## Small Kitchen Lighting Layout by Kitchen Shape

The shape of your kitchen decides where light should go. If you are still planning the room itself, start with our [small kitchen layout ideas](/blog/small-kitchen-layout-ideas/).

### Galley Kitchens

A galley has two parallel counter runs with an aisle between. Light both runs with under-cabinet strips. On the ceiling, use two small flush fixtures down the aisle or a row of recessed lights over each counter.

Light the far end, too. A lamp, a sconce or a bright window at the end of a galley draws the eye down the room and makes it feel longer.

### L-Shaped Kitchens

An L-shape has counters on two walls and an open corner of floor. The inside corner of the L is the darkest spot. Make sure the under-cabinet light runs all the way into that corner. Place one ceiling light near the corner and one near the open end.

### U-Shaped Kitchens

A U-shape wraps counters around three walls. It has the most counter, so it needs the most task light. Run strips under every upper cabinet. One central flush fixture is usually enough for general light, because the walls are close together.

### One-Wall and Studio Kitchens

A one-wall kitchen sits along a single wall, often in a studio. You see it from the living area, so the light should match the rest of the room. Use 2700K, put the under-cabinet strip on a dimmer and add one accent light on a shelf.

For a studio, we like a plug-in sconce or a small pendant at one end of the counter. It marks the kitchen as its own zone without a wall. Our [small kitchen ideas on a budget](/blog/small-kitchen-ideas-on-a-budget/) cover more ways to set off a studio kitchen cheaply.

## Light the Sink, Stove and Inside the Cabinets

These three spots get forgotten, and each one fixes a daily problem.

### The Sink

The sink is often under a window, so there is no upper cabinet to hang a light from. That makes it dark after sunset, right where you wash knives and check if a pan is clean. Add a small pendant, a flush light or a short plug-in wall light above it. If you are replacing the sink, a light-colored one like a [white undermount kitchen sink](/blog/best-white-undermount-kitchen-sink/) bounces more light back up.

An [over-the-sink cutting board](/blog/best-over-the-sink-cutting-board/) turns the sink into extra prep space. That makes good light there even more useful.

### The Stove

Your range hood light is the task light for the stove. Most hoods ship with dim bulbs. Swap them for LED bulbs made for appliances, matched to your 3000K. Check the bulb size listed in the hood manual first.

Grease on the hood cover cuts the light, too. Clean it when you clean the filters, as we explain in [how often to clean a range hood filter](/blog/how-often-to-clean-a-range-hood-filter/). ### Inside Cabinets and Drawers

A rechargeable motion-sensor light at the top front of a deep cabinet lets you see what is in the back. ## What Lighting Can Renters Add to a Kitchen?

Renters can add almost every layer without touching the wiring. Everything below plugs in or runs on a rechargeable battery, and it all comes down when you move.

| Plug-in or battery option | What it replaces | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Linkable plug-in LED light bars | Hardwired under-cabinet lights | Main counter light |
| Rechargeable motion-sensor strips | Hardwired strips where there is no outlet | Short counters, inside cabinets |
| Rechargeable puck lights | Recessed cabinet lights | Glass cabinets, deep shelves, pantries |
| Plug-in swag pendant on a ceiling hook | A hardwired pendant | Over a sink, peninsula or small table |
| Plug-in wall sconce | A hardwired sconce | End of a galley, beside a window |
| Small table lamp or clip light | An accent fixture | Open shelves, studio kitchens |
| Smart bulbs | A dimmer switch | Dimming and color control in existing fixtures |

### Plug-In Under-Cabinet Lights

This is the best renter upgrade there is. Linkable bars connect end to end with short cables, so one outlet can power a whole counter run. Hide the cord under the cabinet with adhesive clips, then drop it down the backsplash in a paintable cord channel.

### Rechargeable Lights

Rechargeable strips and pucks attach with magnets or adhesive pads and charge by USB. Pick ones with a motion sensor so the charge lasts longer. They are dimmer than plug-in bars, so use them where you have no outlet.

### Plug-In Pendants and Sconces

A swag pendant has a long cord and a plug. Hang it from a ceiling hook, loop the cord to the wall with a second hook and run it down to an outlet. Plug-in wall sconces mount with two screws and a cord cover. Two small screw holes are easy to patch at move-out.

### Cord Safety

Keep every cord away from the sink and the stove. Never run a cord across a burner or behind the range. Do not run cords under rugs, where they wear out unseen. Kitchen outlets near the sink are usually GFCI outlets, the kind with test and reset buttons. Plug into those when you can.

## Simple Wiring-Free Fixes

Before you buy new fixtures, try these. Most cost little or nothing.

1. **Swap every bulb for a brighter LED.** Check the fixture label for the maximum wattage. An LED bulb that gives 1,100 lumens uses around 10 to 12 watts, well under most limits.
2. **Match all the bulbs.** Same color temperature, same brand, CRI 90 or higher.
3. **Clean the ceiling fixture cover.** Dust, grease and dead bugs inside a glass cover can block a surprising amount of light. Wash it in warm, soapy water and dry it fully.
4. **Clear the window.** Take down heavy curtains and clear the sill. Daylight is free light.
5. **Use lighter, shinier surfaces.** A white or glossy backsplash, peel-and-stick if you rent, bounces light back into the room.
6. **Add a mirror at the end of a galley.** Keep it away from the stove, where grease and steam will cloud it.
7. **Take the clutter off the counter.** Every object casts a shadow. A clear counter looks brighter under the same light.

## Energy Saving Kitchen Lights

LED bulbs use at least 75 percent less energy than old incandescent bulbs and last for years. If any fixture still holds an old bulb, swap it first.

- **Use task light instead of brighter ceiling light.** A 10-watt strip over the counter does more for prep than a 30-watt ceiling bulb.
- **Add dimmers.** Use bulbs labeled dimmable and a dimmer labeled for LED. A mismatch causes flicker or buzzing.
- **Use motion sensors** on cabinet lights so nothing stays on by accident.
- **Look for the Energy Star label** on bulbs and fixtures.

## A One-Afternoon Small Kitchen Lighting Plan

Here is the order we would follow in any small kitchen. Most renters can finish it in one afternoon.

1. **Measure the room.** Multiply length by width for square footage. Multiply that by 30 to 40 for your ceiling lumen target.
2. **Measure every counter run.** Write down the length of each stretch under the upper cabinets. That is your under-cabinet shopping list.
3. **Pick one color temperature.** We suggest 3000K, or 2700K for a kitchen open to the living room.
4. **Clean the existing fixture and swap its bulbs.** Hit your lumen target with LED bulbs, CRI 90 or higher.
5. **Install under-cabinet lights.** Mount them 1 to 2 inches behind the cabinet front, full length, with cords clipped out of sight.
6. **Fix the dark spots.** Add a light over the sink, swap the range hood bulbs and put motion lights in deep cabinets.
7. **Add one accent light.** A lamp on a shelf, a sconce at the end of a galley or a puck in a glass cabinet.
8. **Check at night.** Stand at each work spot after dark. If you cast a shadow on what you are doing, that spot needs more task light.

## Common Small Kitchen Lighting Mistakes

These are the mistakes we see most often in small kitchens.

- **Relying on one ceiling light.** It always leaves you working in your own shadow. Add under-cabinet light.
- **Mixing color temperatures.** Warm ceiling light with cool strips looks patchy. Match every bulb.
- **Buying by watts.** Watts measure power, not brightness. Shop by lumens and CRI 90 or higher.
- **Mounting strips against the wall.** You get glare on the backsplash and a dim front edge. Mount them toward the front.
- **Using bare LED tape on glossy counters.** It reflects as a row of dots. Use diffused strips.
- **Hanging a big fixture from a low ceiling.** A chandelier or deep dome in a small kitchen eats headroom and makes the room feel shorter. Use a flush fixture.
- **Centering recessed lights in the room.** They should sit over the counters, not over the empty floor.
- **Running cords near water or heat.** Keep every cord clear of the sink and the stove.

Avoid these, and even a tiny, windowless kitchen can feel bright, open and easy to cook in.
