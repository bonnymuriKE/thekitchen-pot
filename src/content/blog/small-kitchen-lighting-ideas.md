An 8-by-10-foot kitchen needs roughly 2,400 to 3,200 lumens. The lone ceiling bulb most rentals come with gives about 800, which is why small kitchen lighting so often feels dim.

Piling on fixtures is not the fix. The right light in three places, with matching bulbs, is. Below is the full plan, with the numbers and the plug-in options that work in a rental.

**The quick fix:** Put a flush ceiling fixture overhead, LED strips under the upper cabinets and one small accent light. Target 30 to 40 lumens per square foot, with extra on the counters. Keep every bulb at the same color temperature, 2700K to 3000K, and CRI 90 or higher. Renters can do nearly all of it with plug-in and rechargeable lights.

## What Is the Best Lighting for a Small Kitchen?

The best lighting for a small kitchen is layered LED light. Layering means you use different lights for different jobs instead of asking one fixture to do everything.

There are three layers:

- **Ambient light** fills the whole room. This is your ceiling fixture or recessed lights.
- **Task light** shines on the spots where you work: the counter, the sink and the stove. In a small kitchen, this is mostly under-cabinet lighting.
- **Accent light** adds depth, like a puck light in a glass cabinet or a sconce by an open shelf. It is optional, but it makes a tiny room feel finished.

Layering matters because your ceiling light sits behind you when you face the counter, so you chop in your own shadow. Task light comes from in front and fixes that. Lit corners also make the room look bigger. We cover the other tricks in [how to make a small kitchen look bigger](/blog/make-a-small-kitchen-look-bigger/).

## How Many Lights Do I Need in a Small Kitchen?

Count lumens, not fixtures. A lumen measures how much light a bulb gives off. Watts only tell you how much power it uses.

Our rule of thumb for kitchens:

- **General light:** about 30 to 40 lumens per square foot of floor.
- **Counter and sink:** about 70 to 80 lumens per square foot of work surface, roughly double the room.

To find your square footage, multiply the length of the room by the width in feet. An 8-by-10-foot kitchen is 80 square feet. At 30 to 40 lumens each, it needs about 2,400 to 3,200 lumens from the ceiling.

For scale, an old 60-watt bulb gives about 800 lumens. An LED does the same on about 8 watts.

| Kitchen size | Floor area | Ceiling light (lumens) | What that looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 x 7 ft (tiny galley or studio nook) | 35 sq ft | 1,000 to 1,400 | One flush fixture with a 1,200-lumen LED |
| 6 x 8 ft | 48 sq ft | 1,500 to 1,900 | One flush fixture, or two small ones |
| 8 x 10 ft | 80 sq ft | 2,400 to 3,200 | One large flush fixture or four 4-inch recessed lights |
| 10 x 10 ft | 100 sq ft | 3,000 to 4,000 | Four to six 4-inch recessed lights, or two fixtures |
| 10 x 12 ft | 120 sq ft | 3,600 to 4,800 | Six recessed lights, or a fixture plus a track |

Under-cabinet lights come on top of these numbers. Go a little higher if your cabinets or walls are dark, since dark surfaces soak up light.

## Pick the Right Color Temperature

Color temperature is how warm or cool the light looks. It is measured in kelvins, written as K. Lower numbers look warm and yellow, like a candle. Higher numbers look cool and blue, like an overcast sky.

| Color temperature | How it looks | Where we use it |
|---|---|---|
| 2700K | Warm, soft, like a classic bulb | Kitchens open to a living room, warm wood or cream cabinets |
| 3000K | Warm white, clean but still cozy | Our default for most small kitchens |
| 3500K | Neutral white | White or gray kitchens that need a crisper look |
| 4000K | Cool white, bright and sharp | Garages and workrooms; feels clinical in most homes |
| 5000K and up | Bluish daylight | Skip it at home; skin and food look flat |

We recommend 3000K for most small kitchens. It is bright enough for prep work but still feels like home. If your kitchen opens straight into your living room, 2700K blends better with the lamps there.

The bigger rule is to match. Use the same color temperature in every fixture, including the under-cabinet strips and the bulb in the range hood. A warm ceiling light with cool blue strips makes the kitchen look patchy. Check the number before you buy, because many LED strips default to 4000K or daylight.

## CRI Explained Simply

CRI stands for color rendering index. It is a score from 0 to 100 that tells you how true colors look under a light. Sunlight scores 100.

A cheap LED can read 80. Under it, tomatoes look dull and raw chicken looks gray. At 90 or higher, food looks like food, and that matters when you judge doneness by color. Is the steak still pink? Are the onions golden or burnt?

On the box, look for **CRI 90 or higher** on every kitchen bulb and strip. If it lists **R9**, a score for deep reds like meat and tomatoes, look for 50 or more. Buy matching bulbs from one brand, since two bulbs both labeled 3000K can still look slightly different.

For ceiling fixtures, pendants and sconces with a standard screw-in socket, our pick is the [Philips Ultra Definition LED A19 Bulbs, 2700K 8-Pack](https://www.amazon.com/Philips-Ultra-Definition-LED-A19-Bulbs-2700K-8-Pack/dp/B0B927WMTY/?tag=kitchenpot-20). Each gives 800 lumens on 8 watts with a CRI above 90, and they are dimmable and flicker-free. One 8-pack covers a small kitchen with spares, so every bulb matches.

## Small Kitchen Lighting Ceiling Ideas

The ceiling layer does the general lighting. In a small kitchen, it should be bright, low-profile and out of the way.

### Flush and Semi-Flush Fixtures

A flush fixture sits tight against the ceiling. A semi-flush fixture hangs a few inches below it on a short stem. With a standard 8-foot ceiling, we pick flush every time. It keeps the ceiling looking higher.

To size a ceiling fixture, add the length and width of the room in feet. The answer is roughly the right diameter in inches. An 8-by-10-foot kitchen adds up to 18, so look for a fixture about 16 to 18 inches across.

Choose a fixture with a frosted or opal glass cover. It spreads light evenly and hides the bright LED dots.

### Recessed Lights

Recessed lights, also called can lights, sit inside the ceiling. They are the cleanest look for a small kitchen, but they need wiring, so they are an owner project.

In a small kitchen, use 4-inch lights rather than 6-inch ones. Space them about half the ceiling height apart, so about 4 feet on an 8-foot ceiling.

Place them over the front edge of the counter, about 24 to 30 inches out from the wall. That way the light lands on your work surface, not behind your head.

If you already have 5- or 6-inch cans, you do not need new fixtures. Fill them with [Feit Electric BR30 LED bulbs, 2700K 90 CRI](https://www.amazon.com/Feit-Electric-BR30-LED-2700K-90-CRI-4-Pack/dp/B081BSDSYS/?tag=kitchenpot-20). A BR30 is a flood bulb shaped to throw light down, and these give 650 lumens each, dim smoothly and make food look right.

### Track and Rail Lights

A track light is a single fixture with several heads you can aim. It is a good choice when you have one ceiling box in the middle of the room but the counters are along the walls. One track can send light to the sink, the stove and the prep counter.

### Old Fluorescent Boxes

Many rentals still have a long fluorescent box on the ceiling. Swap the tubes for LED tubes labeled plug-and-play, meaning they work with the existing ballast. Tubes that need the ballast bypassed are a job for an electrician.

## Under-Cabinet Lighting in a Small Kitchen

If you add only one light to a small kitchen, make it this one. Under-cabinet lights put bright, even light right on the counter, where you chop, measure and read recipes.

### Where to Mount It

Mount the strip toward the front edge of the cabinet bottom, about 1 to 2 inches back from the face. Do not put it against the wall.

Front mounting spreads light across the full depth of a 24-inch counter. Back mounting blasts the backsplash with glare and leaves the front half dim.

Most upper cabinets have a lip, a small strip of wood that hangs below the cabinet bottom. Tuck the light just behind it so you never see the bulbs when you stand at the counter. A slim hardwired bar hides best. The [Kichler 4U Series 30-Inch LED Under Cabinet Light](https://www.amazon.com/Kichler-4U-Series-30-Inch-LED-Under-Cabinet-Light/dp/B01ETARS2O/?tag=kitchenpot-20) is only 1 inch tall, so it disappears behind even a shallow lip.

### How Long and How Bright

Run the lights the full length of each counter run, or the unlit half looks dim by comparison.

Use these numbers when you shop:

| Detail | Our target |
|---|---|
| Strip length | Within a few inches of the full cabinet width |
| Brightness | About 200 to 400 lumens per foot of strip |
| Puck light spacing | One puck every 16 to 20 inches |
| Mount position | 1 to 2 inches behind the cabinet front |
| Color temperature | Match the ceiling, usually 3000K |
| CRI | 90 or higher |
| Strip type | Diffused, with a frosted cover |

A diffused strip has a frosted cover that gives one smooth line of light. Bare LED tape shows a row of dots that reflect in shiny counters. Puck lights leave scallops of shadow between them, so we save them for inside cabinets.

### Does Under-Cabinet Lighting Make a Kitchen Look Bigger?

Yes. The backsplash is often the darkest wall in a small kitchen. Light it, and it seems to move back, so the room feels wider. It works best on a clear counter, though. ## How Do I Light a Kitchen With No Island?

Most small kitchens have no island, so your light belongs over the counters around the edge of the room. The center of the floor needs the least light, since you only walk through it.

1. **Light every counter run from below the cabinets.** This is your main work light.
2. **Aim the ceiling light at the counters.** Use recessed lights over the counter edges, or a track with heads pointed at each work zone.
3. **Put one small light over the sink** and swap the range hood bulbs for bright LEDs.
4. **Save pendants for a peninsula or small table.** That is the one place a pendant belongs.

A peninsula or bar counter can take one or two small pendants. Keep them small, about 8 to 12 inches across. Hang the bottom 30 to 36 inches above the counter. Space two pendants about 24 to 30 inches apart. With no ceiling box there, use the [KUAUGST Plug-In Pendant Light with 15 ft Cord](https://www.amazon.com/KUAUGST-Plug-In-Pendant-Light-15-FT-Cord/dp/B09LHDBVMF/?tag=kitchenpot-20). Its fabric shade softens glare, it has an inline switch, and it takes one of the 2700K high-CRI bulbs above.

## Small Kitchen Lighting Layout by Kitchen Shape

The shape of your kitchen decides where light should go. If you are still planning the room itself, start with our [small kitchen layout ideas](/blog/small-kitchen-layout-ideas/).

### Galley Kitchens

A galley has two parallel counter runs. Light both with under-cabinet strips, and use two small flush fixtures down the aisle.

Light the far end, too. A sconce at the end of a galley draws the eye down the room and makes it feel longer. The [TISLYCO Plug-In Wall Sconces, Set of 2](https://www.amazon.com/TISLYCO-Plug-In-Wall-Sconces-Set-of-2/dp/B095P2ZHCX/?tag=kitchenpot-20) are a budget way to do it. The heads swivel, so you can aim one at the end wall and one at a shelf.

### L-Shaped Kitchens

The inside corner of an L is the darkest spot. Run the under-cabinet light all the way into it, and place one ceiling light near the corner.

### U-Shaped Kitchens

A U-shape has the most counter, so it needs the most task light. Run strips under every upper cabinet. One central flush fixture is usually enough, because the walls are close together.

### One-Wall and Studio Kitchens

You see a one-wall kitchen from the living area, so its light should match the room. Use 2700K, put the under-cabinet strip on a dimmer and add one accent light on a shelf.

For a studio, we like a plug-in sconce at one end of the counter. It marks the kitchen as its own zone without a wall. ### Plug-In Under-Cabinet Lights

The best renter upgrade there is. Linkable bars like the Enbrighten 22-inch connect end to end with short cables, so one outlet can power a whole counter run. Hide the cord under the cabinet with adhesive clips, then drop it down the backsplash in a paintable cord channel.

### Rechargeable Lights

Rechargeable bars charge by USB and are dimmer than plug-in bars, so use them where you have no outlet. Pick ones with a motion sensor so the charge lasts. The [MCGOR 10-Inch Rechargeable Motion Sensor Under Cabinet Light](https://www.amazon.com/MCGOR-10-Inch-Rechargeable-Motion-Sensor-Under-Cabinet-Light/dp/B0BDF8CVBN/?tag=kitchenpot-20) comes as a 2-pack with five brightness levels. Its magnet plate sticks on, so the bar pops off for charging and nothing is drilled. Pick the warm white version so it matches your ceiling.

### Plug-In Pendants and Sconces

A swag pendant has a long cord and a plug. Hang it from a ceiling hook, loop the cord to the wall with a second hook and run it down to an outlet. Plug-in wall sconces mount with two screws and a cord cover. Two small screw holes are easy to patch at move-out.

### Cord Safety

Keep every cord away from the sink and the stove, and never run one behind the range or under a rug. Kitchen outlets near the sink are usually GFCI outlets, the kind with test and reset buttons. Plug into those when you can.

## Simple Wiring-Free Fixes

Before you buy new fixtures, try these. Most cost little or nothing.

1. **Swap every bulb for a brighter LED.** Check the fixture label for the maximum wattage. An LED bulb that gives 1,100 lumens uses around 10 to 12 watts, well under most limits.
2. **Match all the bulbs.** Same color temperature, same brand, CRI 90 or higher.
3. **Clean the ceiling fixture cover.** Dust, grease and dead bugs inside it block a surprising amount of light.
4. **Clear the window.** Daylight is free light.
5. **Use lighter, shinier surfaces.** A white or glossy backsplash bounces light back into the room. Renters can cover a dark one with [Smart Tiles Peel and Stick Backsplash in Subway Soft White](https://www.amazon.com/Smart-Tiles-Peel-Stick-Backsplash-Subway/dp/B07XCHLLGP/?tag=kitchenpot-20), which has a slight sheen and peels off at move-out.
6. **Take the clutter off the counter.** Every object casts a shadow.

## A One-Afternoon Small Kitchen Lighting Plan

Here is the order we would follow. Most renters can finish it in one afternoon.

1. **Measure the room.** Multiply length by width for square footage. Multiply that by 30 to 40 for your ceiling lumen target.
2. **Measure every counter run.** That is your under-cabinet shopping list.
3. **Pick one color temperature.** We suggest 3000K, or 2700K for a kitchen open to the living room.
4. **Clean the fixture and swap its bulbs** for high-CRI LEDs that hit your lumen target.
5. **Install under-cabinet lights** 1 to 2 inches behind the cabinet front, full length.
6. **Fix the dark spots.** Add a light over the sink, swap the range hood bulbs and put motion lights in deep cabinets.
7. **Add one accent light.** A lamp on a shelf, a sconce at the end of a galley or a puck in a glass cabinet.
8. **Check at night.** If you cast a shadow at any work spot, it needs more task light.

## Lighting Mistakes We See All the Time

- **Relying on one ceiling light.** You work in your own shadow. Add under-cabinet light.
- **Mixing color temperatures.** Warm ceiling light with cool strips looks patchy. Match every bulb.
- **Buying by watts.** Watts measure power, not brightness. Shop by lumens and CRI 90 or higher.
- **Mounting strips against the wall.** You get glare on the backsplash and a dim front edge. Mount them toward the front.
- **Using bare LED tape on glossy counters.** It reflects as a row of dots. Use diffused strips.
- **Hanging a big fixture from a low ceiling.** It eats headroom. Use a flush fixture.
- **Centering recessed lights in the room.** They belong over the counters.
- **Running cords near water or heat.** Keep every cord clear of the sink and the stove.

Avoid these, and even a tiny, windowless kitchen can feel bright and easy to cook in.
