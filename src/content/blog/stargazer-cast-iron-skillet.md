It costs far more than a Lodge. This review looks at what the extra money buys.

## What Makes the Stargazer Different

**A smooth, machined cooking surface.** Most modern cast iron is left with the pebbly texture of the sand mold. Stargazer machines its cooking surface smooth. That makes eggs and fish release more easily once the pan is seasoned.

**Lighter weight.** At 5.2 pounds for the 10.5-inch, it is easier to lift and toss food in than heavier pans.

**A flared, drip-free rim.** Stargazer says the rim is flared so you can pour from any side without dribbling down the outside.

**A stay-cool handle.** The handle is long and split near the pan. Stargazer says it stays cooler on the stovetop and doubles as a utensil rest. It still gets hot in the oven.

**Made in the USA.** Each skillet is made in Allentown, Pennsylvania. For many buyers, supporting American manufacturing is part of the appeal, and it is one reason the price is higher than imported pans.

## How It Cooks

Cast iron is prized for holding heat and searing well. The Stargazer does both, with a few differences from heavier pans.

[Prudent Reviews compared Stargazer and Lodge](https://prudentreviews.com/stargazer-vs-lodge/) side by side. In its tests, the Stargazer boiled water in 3 minutes 24 seconds versus 4 minutes 45 seconds for the Lodge. The thicker Lodge held its heat a little longer once off the burner.

In practice, that means the Stargazer heats faster and responds more quickly when you adjust the burner. The Lodge stays hotter when you drop in cold food.

The smooth surface shines with eggs, fish and pancakes, once seasoning builds up. For steaks and cornbread, both pans perform very well.

## Seasoning and Care

1. **Season a bare pan.** Wipe a very thin layer of high-smoke-point oil over the whole pan. Buff it off until it looks almost dry. Bake upside down at 450°F to 500°F for an hour. Repeat three to five times.
2. **Cook fatty foods first.** Bacon, sausage and shallow frying build seasoning quickly.
3. **Clean while warm.** Rinse with hot water and scrub with a brush or chain mail. A little soap is fine.
4. **Dry on the stove.** Heat the pan for a minute to drive off all water.
5. **Oil lightly.** Wipe on a few drops of oil while warm.
6. **Avoid long soaks.** Standing water causes rust.

If rust appears, scrub it off, dry and re-season. Cast iron is very forgiving.

## Using Cast Iron on Different Stoves

**Gas.** Heat slowly on medium, then raise the heat. Cast iron can crack if shocked from cold to very hot.

**Electric coil.** Works well. Match the pan to the burner size.

**Glass-top.** Lift, do not slide, to avoid scratches. See [how to protect a glass-top stove from cast iron](/blog/how-to-protect-glass-top-stove-from-cast-iron/).

**Grill and campfire.** Cast iron goes anywhere. Use it on a gas grill for cornbread or on a campfire for breakfast.

## Smooth vs Textured Cast Iron

Modern mass-produced cast iron, like Lodge, keeps the slightly pebbly texture left by the sand mold. Seasoning fills in that texture over time, and the pan becomes very slick over months of regular cooking.

Machined pans, like Stargazer, start smooth. Seasoning sits in a thinner, more even layer, so the pan feels slick sooner.

Some cooks argue that textured pans hold seasoning better, because the oil has more surface to grip. In daily cooking, both work well once seasoned. The smooth pan simply gets there faster and is easier to wipe clean.

## Why Old Cast Iron Was Smoother

Before the mid-1900s, American foundries like Griswold and Wagner ground and polished their cooking surfaces. Pans were also cast thinner, so they weighed less.

As production sped up, most makers stopped the extra grinding to cut costs. Pans became thicker and rougher.

Stargazer and other new American brands set out to bring back that older style. The result is a pan that feels closer to a vintage piece but comes new, with a warranty.

## Common Cast Iron Mistakes

- **Putting it in the dishwasher.** It strips seasoning and causes rust.
- **Soaking overnight.** Standing water rusts cast iron fast.
- **Storing it wet.** Always dry on the stove and oil lightly.
- **Cooking acidic sauces for hours.** Tomato or wine sauces can strip seasoning and add a metallic taste.
- **Using too much oil when seasoning.** Thick layers turn sticky. Wipe on oil, then wipe nearly all of it off.
- **Heating an empty pan on high from cold.** Sudden heat can warp or crack cast iron. Preheat gradually.
- **Grabbing the handle bare-handed.** Even stay-cool handles get hot in the oven. Keep [heat-resistant gloves](/blog/10-best-heat-resistant-gloves-for-cooking/) nearby.

## Five First Recipes for a New Skillet

1. **Bacon.** The rendered fat seasons the pan as it cooks.
2. **Smash burgers.** Press beef thin on a very hot pan for crispy edges.
3. **Skillet cornbread.** Preheat the pan with butter in the oven, then pour in the batter.
4. **Pan-roasted potatoes.** Toss cubed potatoes in oil and roast in the skillet at 425°F.
5. **Seared salmon.** Skin side down in a hot, well-oiled pan until crisp.

These recipes use plenty of fat and high heat, which builds seasoning quickly.

## How to Restore a Rusty or Sticky Skillet

**Light rust.** Scrub the rust off with steel wool or a stiff brush and a little water. Rinse, dry on the stove, then oil and bake as in the seasoning steps.

**Sticky, gummy surface.** This means too much oil was left on during seasoning. Bake the pan upside down at 450°F for an hour to finish curing the oil, then wipe it down.

**Flaking seasoning.** Scrub away loose flakes, then re-season with thin coats. Flakes are harmless if eaten but make the pan look patchy.

**Burnt-on food.** Simmer a little water in the pan for a few minutes, then scrape with a wooden spatula. Coarse salt and a paper towel make a gentle scrub.

A smooth Stargazer is easier to restore than a rough pan, because there are fewer pits for rust and food to hide in.

## Cast Iron for Baking

A cast iron skillet doubles as bakeware. It holds heat evenly, which gives cornbread and biscuits crisp edges.

Try a skillet loaf of no-knead bread, shaped in a [bread proofing basket](/blog/best-bread-proofing-basket-reviews/) and baked in the preheated pan. Skillet cookies, upside-down cakes and Dutch baby pancakes also work well.

Cast iron is also one of the best ways to bring leftovers back to life. A hot skillet with a lid crisps pizza crust better than a microwave; see [how to reheat pizza](/blog/how-to-reheat-pizza/).

## Does Cast Iron Add Iron to Food?

Cooking in cast iron can add small amounts of iron to food, especially acidic or wet foods cooked for a long time. Well-seasoned pans add less, because the seasoning separates food from bare metal.

For most people this is harmless. If you have a condition that requires limiting iron, ask your doctor.

## Seasoned or Bare: Which Should You Buy?

**Pre-seasoned** is ready to cook on the day it arrives. Stargazer applies a base coat of canola, grapeseed and sunflower oils. Most people should choose this option.

**Bare** suits cast iron fans who like to build their own seasoning with a favorite oil. Some cooks prefer flaxseed or a commercial seasoning blend. A bare pan must be seasoned before its first use, or it will rust.

Either way, the seasoning will darken and improve with cooking. After a few months, a seasoned and a bare pan look and cook the same.
