An air fryer and a full-size oven can cook the exact same chicken thighs, crisp the same fries, and reheat the same leftovers. They just don't cost the same amount of electricity to do it.

This guide breaks down the actual math, using real wattage figures and current U.S. electricity rates, so you can see where the savings are real and where they stop mattering.

## The Short Answer

An air fryer typically draws 1,400 to 1,800 watts and cooks in less time than an oven, which draws 2,000 to 5,000 watts and needs several minutes to preheat. For small to medium meals, the air fryer usually costs less per use, often by half or more.

The gap narrows or disappears for large batches, since an oven's single preheat cycle can cook far more food at once than an air fryer's basket can hold.

## The Wattage Difference, Explained

Wattage tells you how much power an appliance draws while running, and it's the starting point for any real cost comparison.

A typical countertop air fryer draws somewhere between 1,400 and 1,800 watts. A full-size electric oven draws considerably more, generally 2,000 to 5,000 watts depending on the model and whether the broiler or multiple elements are active.

That gap alone suggests the air fryer should cost less to run, and it usually does. But wattage is only half the equation. Cooking time matters just as much.

## Doing the Real Math

To calculate actual cost, you need three numbers: wattage, time, and your electricity rate.

The formula: (watts ÷ 1,000) × hours used × your rate per kilowatt-hour (kWh) = cost.

**Air fryer example:** A 1,500-watt air fryer running for 20 minutes (one-third of an hour) uses 0.5 kWh. At the current U.S. average residential rate of about 17 cents per kWh, that's roughly 8 to 9 cents per use.

**Oven example:** A 2,400-watt electric oven, including a 10-minute preheat plus 35 minutes of cooking (45 minutes, or 0.75 hours total), uses about 1.8 kWh. At the same rate, that's roughly 30 to 31 cents per use.

In this example, the air fryer costs less than a third of what the oven does for a comparable small meal. This lines up closely with independent testing from consumer organizations, which have found air fryers using roughly half to a third of the energy of a full oven for similar portions.

## Why the Gap Exists

Two factors drive almost the entire difference: preheat time and interior volume.

An oven has to heat a large interior space, usually several cubic feet, before it's ready to cook anything. An air fryer heats a small basket, often just a few quarts, which takes a fraction of the time and energy to bring up to temperature.

Ovens also lose more heat to their surroundings simply because they're bigger and often less insulated per cubic foot than a compact countertop unit. Every minute spent preheating and holding temperature in a large, leaky space is electricity spent on something other than actually cooking your food.

## When the Oven Still Wins

The math flips once you're cooking enough food to fill an oven efficiently.

If you're roasting a full tray of vegetables, baking multiple items at once, or cooking a dish too large for an air fryer basket, the oven's single preheat cycle is doing the work of what would otherwise be several separate air fryer batches.

Compare one oven preheat cooking four portions against four separate air fryer runs cooking one portion each, and the oven often comes out ahead or close to even, since you're not paying the preheat "tax" multiple times over.

## A Few Caveats Worth Knowing

A few factors can shift these numbers in either direction for your specific situation.

Your local electricity rate matters more than almost anything else in this comparison, since rates vary significantly by state and utility provider. Check a recent bill for your actual rate per kWh rather than assuming the national average applies exactly to you.

Appliance age and condition also matter. An older oven with worn door seals loses heat faster and runs its elements longer to compensate, which widens the gap in the air fryer's favor.

Cooking style affects the comparison too. If you tend to cook large batches to meal-prep for the week, the oven's per-portion cost often improves significantly compared to running an air fryer multiple times.

## Air Fryers vs. Other Small Countertop Appliances

The air fryer isn't the only small appliance competing for the "cheaper than the oven" title, and it's worth knowing where it fits among the others.

A slow cooker uses even less wattage than an air fryer, often under 300 watts, but runs for hours rather than minutes, which can end up using a similar or greater total amount of energy depending on cook time. A microwave remains the cheapest option of all for small portions and reheating specifically, since it cooks almost entirely through direct energy transfer with very little wasted as ambient heat.

Think of these appliances as different tools for different jobs rather than competing for the same single task, since matching the right appliance to the right meal matters more than always reaching for the lowest-wattage option.

## How Often You Cook Changes the Math Too

A single meal's cost difference feels small, but it compounds over regular use.

If you air fry dinner four or five nights a week instead of using the oven, the savings calculated in the table above add up to real money over a year, not just pocket change per meal. This is the same logic behind choosing [energy-efficient cookware for a small kitchen](/blog/how-to-choose-energy-efficient-cookware-for-a-small-kitchen/): small, consistent choices compound more than people expect.

For occasional use, like baking once a week, the cost difference matters far less, and convenience or capacity should probably drive the decision instead.

## Calculate Your Own Exact Cost

The figures in this guide use national averages, but your own numbers take just a minute to work out.

1. Find your air fryer's wattage, usually printed on a label on the bottom or back of the unit.
2. Find your actual electricity rate on a recent utility bill, listed per kilowatt-hour.
3. Divide the wattage by 1,000 to convert to kilowatts.
4. Multiply that number by how many hours you typically run it per use.
5. Multiply the result by your rate per kWh.

Repeat the same steps for your oven's wattage, including your typical preheat time, to get an apples-to-apples comparison specific to your own kitchen and your own utility rates rather than a national average.

## Standby Power: The Cost Nobody Talks About

Both appliances can draw a small amount of power even when they're not actively cooking, and it's worth knowing about even though it's a minor factor.

A digital air fryer or oven with a clock, display, or Wi-Fi connectivity draws a small trickle of electricity continuously just to keep those features running. This standby draw is tiny compared to active cooking wattage, typically just a few watts, but it runs 24 hours a day, every day, which adds up to a small but real amount over a year.

Unplugging appliances you rarely use, or choosing simpler models without always-on displays, shaves a small amount off your total kitchen electricity use, though it's a minor factor compared to the cooking choices covered throughout this guide.

## Multi-Cookers and Pressure Cookers: Another Comparison Point

A multi-cooker or electric pressure cooker is worth a brief mention, since it occupies a similar efficiency niche to the air fryer for a different style of cooking.

These appliances typically draw 700 to 1,200 watts, often less than an air fryer, and excel at long, low-and-slow cooking that would otherwise mean hours of oven or stovetop time. For soups, stews, and braises specifically, a multi-cooker often beats both the air fryer and the oven on cost, simply because it's built around efficient, sealed, low-wattage cooking over a longer period.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

* **Comparing wattage alone without factoring in time.** A lower-wattage appliance that runs much longer can still cost more.
* **Ignoring preheat time in the oven calculation.** It's a real cost, not a free warm-up period.
* **Assuming the air fryer always wins.** Large batches often favor the oven once you account for multiple air fryer runs.
* **Using a national average rate without checking your own bill.** Local rates vary enough to meaningfully change the math.
* **Buying an oversized air fryer for small portions.** A bigger basket draws more power and takes longer to heat than you need for one or two servings.
