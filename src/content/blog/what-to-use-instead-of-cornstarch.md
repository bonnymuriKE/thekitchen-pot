All-purpose flour is the cornstarch substitute most kitchens already have. Use **2 tablespoons of flour for every 1 tablespoon of cornstarch**, whisk it into cold liquid before it goes near the heat, and simmer for a few minutes so it loses its raw taste. That works for gravy, soup, stew and most sauces.

If you have them, potato starch, tapioca starch and arrowroot are closer matches, because like cornstarch they're pure starch. They make glossier, clearer sauces than flour and they're gluten-free. The catch is that each one behaves a little differently with heat, acid and dairy, so the best swap depends on what you're making.

One more thing before the chart: if you compare sources, you'll notice the ratios don't always agree. America's Test Kitchen, The Kitchn and Healthline each give slightly different amounts for tapioca and arrowroot. Starches vary by brand and grind, and "thick enough" is partly taste. The ranges below show where the trusted sources land. Start at the low end and add more if you need it.

## Cornstarch Substitute Ratios

Amounts are for replacing **1 tablespoon of cornstarch**.

| Substitute | How much to use | Gluten-free? | Best for | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| All-purpose flour | 2 tablespoons | No | Gravy, stew, soup, pan sauce | Cloudy finish; needs simmering; clumps if added dry |
| Wondra (instant flour) | 1½ teaspoons | No | Last-minute gravy, pan sauce | Contains wheat |
| Potato starch | 1 to 1½ tablespoons | Yes | Soups, gravy, frying | Breaks down with long cooking |
| Tapioca starch | 1 to 2 tablespoons | Yes | Fruit pie, sauces, gluten-free baking | Loses power if boiled too long |
| Arrowroot powder | 2 teaspoons to 2 tablespoons | Yes | Fruit sauces, glazes, pies | Slimy with dairy; dislikes reheating |
| Rice flour | 2 tablespoons | Yes | Clear soups, puddings, shortbread, breading | Can taste slightly chalky if coarse |
| Xanthan gum | A pinch, built up slowly | Yes | Low-carb sauces, gluten-free baking | Slimy if overused |

Sources for these ratios: America's Test Kitchen (flour, Wondra, potato starch, tapioca, arrowroot, xanthan), The Kitchn (flour, rice flour, potato starch, tapioca, Wondra, arrowroot) and Healthline (flour, arrowroot, potato starch, tapioca, rice flour).

## What Can You Use to Thicken Gravy or a Stir-Fry Sauce?

This is the most common reason people look for a substitute, and it's also the easiest job to cover.

### Flour: the pantry default

Flour has about half the thickening power of cornstarch, which is why you use twice as much. Fine Cooking's Nicole Rees, quoted in The Washington Post, calls flour "a go-to for a reason in gravies." It gives a rich, opaque sauce and reheats well, which is handy for leftovers.

Two rules keep it smooth:

1. **Never sprinkle dry flour into hot liquid.** America's Test Kitchen warns it will clump. Whisk it into a few tablespoons of cold water or stock first, or cook it in butter or oil for a minute before adding liquid (a roux).
2. **Simmer it.** Flour needs a few minutes at a gentle bubble to cook out its raw, pasty taste. ATK also notes that flour darkens and burns faster than pure starch, so keep the heat moderate.

### Wondra: flour that acts like starch

Wondra is a finely milled, precooked "instant" flour sold in a shaker can. America's Test Kitchen says you need only half as much Wondra as cornstarch by volume, and it dissolves more easily and won't burn as fast as regular flour. If you make pan sauces often, it's worth keeping a can around.

### Potato starch: the closest match for a glossy sauce

Potato starch is what you want for a stir-fry sauce that looks like it came from a restaurant. ATK and The Kitchn both suggest 1 to 1½ tablespoons per tablespoon of cornstarch. It gives what The Kitchn calls a "silky, creamy mouthfeel."

It also thickens at a lower temperature than cornstarch. The Washington Post puts its gelling point around 136 to 150°F, while cornstarch needs to get close to boiling. That's why ATK recommends adding potato starch late in cooking: it thickens fast, and long simmering breaks it down.

If you're making something like [lo mein or chow mein](/blog/lo-mein-vs-chow-mein/), where the sauce goes in during the last minute of cooking, potato starch is an easy one-for-one-ish swap.

## What Works in Fruit Pie Filling?

Pies are a different challenge. The filling has to thicken in the oven, hold a clean slice once it cools, and cope with acidic fruit juice.

| Thickener | Result in pie | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Instant ClearJel | Clear, juicy, not gummy | King Arthur's co-winner, alongside its Pie Filling Enhancer |
| Cornstarch (for comparison) | Effective, slightly cloudy and a bit sticky | The baseline you're replacing |
| All-purpose flour | Faintly starchy, cloudier | Works, and you already have it |
| Quick-cooking tapioca | Slightly cloudy | Let filling sit 15 to 30 minutes before baking |
| Tapioca starch | Glossy, clear | ATK notes it stays supple longer than cornstarch |
| Arrowroot | Clear gel that doesn't weep with acid | Best in pies served at room temperature, not reheated |

King Arthur Baking compared thickeners in fruit pies and named **Instant ClearJel** a co-winner (alongside its own Pie Filling Enhancer), calling the result juicy without being gummy or starchy, and clear enough to let the fruit color show. Instant ClearJel is a modified cornstarch, so it's not a fix if you're avoiding corn, but it is a solid upgrade if you just prefer a clearer filling. It's sold by King Arthur and on [Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0BJYRF9S5/?tag=kitchenpot-20).

If you're avoiding corn entirely, **tapioca starch** and **arrowroot** are the best bets. The Kitchn says arrowroot "sets a beautiful gel in pie fillings" and "doesn't weep when mixed with acid," which matters with berries and rhubarb. The Washington Post describes tapioca as giving fruit juices "a glossy shine."

Flour works in a pinch, and King Arthur found every thickener it compared, flour included, produced a filling that held together when sliced. It's just a little cloudier and more starchy-tasting.

## Pudding, Custard and Anything With Dairy

Cornstarch is the classic thickener for stovetop puddings and pastry cream. Your substitute choices narrow here.

- **Skip arrowroot.** America's Test Kitchen tasters found an arrowroot-thickened dairy pudding "slimy." ATK's advice is to avoid arrowroot in anything that calls for dairy.
- **Skip flour.** ATK doesn't recommend flour for puddings either.
- **Try rice flour or tapioca.** The Kitchn says rice flour performs well in puddings at 2 tablespoons per tablespoon of cornstarch, and it lists tapioca starch as good for puddings too.
- **Lean on egg yolks.** Many custards thicken mostly from egg yolks, gently cooked. If a recipe uses both cornstarch and yolks, the yolks carry a lot of the load. A [double boiler](/blog/how-to-use-a-double-boiler/) gives you the gentle heat that keeps yolks from scrambling.

## For Frying and Crispy Coatings

Cornstarch is also used in batters and dredges because it fries up crisp and light. Two substitutes do the same job well.

- **Potato starch** is a pure starch like cornstarch, so it can take cornstarch's place in a dredge for fried chicken or tofu.
- **Rice flour** makes a thin, crackly crust. America's Test Kitchen says rice flour can stand in for cornstarch in equal amounts for breading.

All-purpose flour works too, but the crust will be heavier and browner. For air-fried food, a light, even coating and a thin film of oil matter more than which starch you pick. Our guide to the [best oil for an air fryer](/blog/best-oil-for-air-fryer/) covers which oils handle the heat, and the same crisping ideas apply when you [reheat chicken wings](/blog/how-to-reheat-chicken-wings/).

## Replacing Cornstarch in Baking

In cakes and cookies, cornstarch usually isn't there to thicken. It's there to make things tender by lowering the protein in the flour.

### Homemade cake flour

America's Test Kitchen's cake flour substitute is **7/8 cup all-purpose flour plus 2 tablespoons cornstarch** for each cup of cake flour. The cornstarch dilutes the protein so the cake bakes up softer and finer.

No cornstarch? Potato starch and arrowroot are also pure starches, so they should play the same role, though ATK's formula was written for cornstarch. If you have neither, you can use all-purpose flour alone and accept a slightly sturdier crumb, or buy a box of cake flour if you bake cakes often.

### Shortbread and tender cookies

ATK says rice flour can replace cornstarch one for one in shortbread. You may notice a faint chalkiness right out of the oven, which fades as the cookies cool. For more on baking evenly, see [how to bake cookies in a convection oven](/blog/how-to-bake-cookies-in-a-convection-oven/).

### Gluten-free baking

Tapioca starch is a common ingredient in gluten-free flour blends. ATK notes it's pure starch from cassava root and doesn't retrograde (firm up and stale) as quickly as cornstarch, which helps baked goods stay soft. Small amounts of xanthan gum add structure that gluten would normally provide.

## Gluten-Free and Low-Carb Swaps

If you're avoiding gluten, flour and Wondra are out. Potato starch, tapioca, arrowroot and rice flour all work, as covered above.

If you're cutting carbs, starches aren't much help, since they're carbohydrates by definition. Healthline lists these lower-carb thickeners:

- **Xanthan gum:** very strong. America's Test Kitchen suggests starting with "barely a pinch" and blending it in with an [immersion blender](/blog/how-to-use-an-immersion-blender/) to prevent clumps. It's flavorless and freezes well, but too much turns slimy.
- **Guar gum:** start around ¼ teaspoon and build up slowly.
- **Glucomannan:** about ¼ teaspoon for every 2 teaspoons of cornstarch, mixed with cold water first.
- **Ground flaxseed:** 1 tablespoon mixed with 4 tablespoons of water replaces about 2 tablespoons of cornstarch. The texture is a little gritty, so it suits stews more than smooth sauces.
- **Psyllium husk:** start with ½ teaspoon and add more as needed.

Gums thicken almost instantly and don't need heat, which makes them useful for cold sauces and dressings. If you don't have an immersion blender yet, our roundup of the [best immersion blenders](/blog/8-best-immersion-blenders/) is a good starting point.

## Thickening Without Any Starch

Sometimes you don't need a substitute at all.

- **Reduce it.** Simmer a sauce uncovered and let the water evaporate. It concentrates flavor as it thickens. A wide [sauté pan](/blog/best-saute-pan/) reduces much faster than a deep pot because there's more surface area.
- **Blend some of it.** For soups with beans, potatoes or vegetables, purée a cup or two and stir it back in.
- **Use what's already there.** A spoonful of mashed potato, a handful of cooked rice blended into soup, or a little grated cheese in a cream sauce will all add body.
- **Add butter at the end.** Whisking cold butter into a pan sauce off the heat gives it a glossy, slightly thicker finish.

## How to Add Any Thickener Without Lumps

Most thickening problems come from how the thickener goes in, not which one you pick.

1. **Make a slurry.** Stir the starch or flour into an equal amount of cold water, broth or wine until smooth. Cold liquid lets the granules separate before they swell.
2. **Add it gradually.** Pour the slurry into the simmering liquid while stirring or whisking. Add half, wait a minute, then decide whether you need the rest.
3. **Heat it enough.** Cornstarch and flour need to get close to a boil to thicken fully. Tapioca and potato starch thicken at lower temperatures, so they don't need a hard boil.
4. **Stop stirring so hard once it's thick.** The Washington Post notes that too much stirring can break down thickened mixtures like custards.

For a big batch of soup or stew, a sturdy [stockpot with a lid](/blog/best-stockpot-with-a-lid/) and a flat whisk make all of this easier. If you need to convert a recipe's liquid first, here's [how many ounces are in a quart](/blog/how-many-ounces-in-a-quart/).

## Why Did My Sauce Go Thin?

| What happened | Likely cause | What to do next time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tapioca sauce thinned out | Boiled too long | Add it near the end; The Kitchn warns long boiling weakens it |
| Potato starch sauce broke down | Cooked too long | Add it at the very end and serve soon |
| Arrowroot sauce went thin or stringy after reheating | Arrowroot doesn't like to be reheated | Use it in dishes you serve right away or cold |
| Flour gravy tastes pasty | Not cooked long enough | Simmer a few more minutes |
| Gummy, slimy texture | Too much gum or arrowroot, or arrowroot with dairy | Use less; switch to potato starch or rice flour for dairy |
| Lumps | Dry thickener hit hot liquid | Always make a slurry or roux first |

## Storing Substitutes When You Have No Pantry

Starches keep for a long time as long as they stay dry and sealed. Decant them into [airtight food storage containers](/blog/best-airtight-food-storage-containers/) so they don't pick up moisture or pantry pests. You don't need all of them: a bag of flour plus one pure starch, like potato starch, covers almost every recipe that calls for cornstarch.
