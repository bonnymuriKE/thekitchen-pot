A pressure cooker does not cook food faster by getting hotter on the outside. It cooks faster because it stops water from boiling away at 212°F.

Everything else in this comparison follows from that one fact.

An Instant Pot is a pressure cooker with a heating element, sensors and a computer built into it. A stovetop pressure cooker is the same physics with a burner and your attention doing the work. Which suits you depends on your kitchen more than your cooking.

## The short answer

| | Instant Pot (electric) | Stovetop pressure cooker |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High pressure | 10.2 to 11.6 psi | Up to 15 psi |
| Water boils at about | 240°F | 250°F |
| Time to reach pressure | 10 to 15 minutes | 3 to 6 minutes |
| Needs a burner | No | Yes |
| Other jobs it does | Slow cook, rice, yogurt, sauté, steam, keep warm | Pressure cooking only |
| Safe for pressure canning | No | Yes, if it is a rated canner |
| Typical lifespan | 3 to 8 years (electronics) | Decades (replace the gasket) |
| Attention needed | Set it and leave | Watch and adjust the burner |

**Buy an Instant Pot** if you want to walk away from the kitchen, you cook a lot of beans, grains, stock and braises, or your stove is unreliable or shared. **Buy a stovetop pressure cooker** if you want speed, if you already have good burners, or if you want one pot that will still work in twenty years.

## How a Pressure Cooker Actually Works

Water boils when the pressure of its escaping vapour matches the pressure of the air pushing down on it. At sea level, that happens at 212°F. Put more food and more heat into an open pot and the water still stops at 212°F, because every extra joule of energy goes into turning liquid into steam rather than raising the temperature. That is the ceiling every ordinary pot runs into.

Seal the lid and the steam has nowhere to go. It builds up above the food and raises the pressure inside, and because the boiling point of water climbs with pressure, the water in your pot can now get hotter than 212°F without boiling off.

The numbers are the whole story:

| Pressure inside the pot | Water boils at about | Where you see it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 psi (open pot) | 212°F | Any saucepan at sea level |
| 5.8 to 7.2 psi | 230°F | Instant Pot low pressure |
| 10.2 to 11.6 psi | 240°F | Instant Pot high pressure |
| 15 psi | 250°F | Most stovetop cookers, Instant Pot Max |

Those figures come from [Instant Pot's own specification page](https://instantpot.com/pages/frequently-asked-questions) and from the ratings stovetop manufacturers publish.

Thirty-eight degrees does not sound dramatic, but chemical reaction rates climb steeply with temperature. Tough collagen in a chuck roast converts to gelatin much faster at 250°F than at 212°F, dried beans hydrate and soften in a fraction of the time, and stock pulls flavour out of bones in an hour instead of six. That is where the "70% faster" style claims come from, and for long-cooked foods they are broadly fair.

What pressure cooking cannot do is brown. Browning needs a dry surface above about 300°F, and the inside of a pressure cooker is a wet 240°F environment. This is why every good pressure recipe tells you to sear first, and why a pot with a sauté function saves you a pan.

### What the lid is doing

The lid is the engineering. On a modern stovetop cooker there are three layers of protection working at once:

1. **A locking mechanism** that physically prevents the lid turning while there is pressure inside.
2. **A pressure regulator** on top that lifts and vents when the internal pressure passes the set point, holding the pot at a steady figure instead of letting it climb.
3. **An over-pressure plug**, a soft rubber disc that blows out and dumps the steam if the main valve ever blocks.

Electric models keep all of that and add a pressure sensor and a thermal cut-off, so instead of venting to control pressure they simply switch the element off. That is why an Instant Pot is quiet and a stovetop cooker hisses.

### One thing altitude changes

Higher up, the air pushes down less, so water boils lower. At 5,000 feet it boils around 202°F in an open pot, and a sealed cooker reaches a lower absolute temperature at the same gauge pressure. Manufacturers generally advise adding roughly 5% to the cooking time for every 1,000 feet above 2,000 feet. If you live in the mountains, that adjustment is not optional.

## What the Instant Pot Adds

Strip the branding away and an Instant Pot is a stainless inner pot, a heating element under it, a sealed lid and a small computer reading a pressure sensor and a temperature sensor. Our explainer on [what an Instant Pot is](/blog/what-is-an-instant-pot/) goes through the parts in detail.

The computer is the product. Because it can measure and adjust, it can do jobs a dumb pot cannot:

- **It holds pressure without you.** No burner babysitting, no adjusting the flame when the regulator starts rocking too hard. Set the time, walk away.
- **It sears in the same pot.** The sauté function runs the element without the lid, so you brown meat, deglaze, then lock the lid and pressure cook. One pot, one wash.
- **It slow cooks.** Same pot, low element, no pressure. That is the function stovetop cookers cannot copy.
- **It cooks rice and grains on a preset.** Handy, though a dedicated machine still does it better, as our guide to [cooking rice in a rice cooker](/blog/how-to-cook-rice-in-a-rice-cooker/) explains and our roundup of the [best rice cookers for brown rice](/blog/best-rice-cooker-for-brown-rice/) covers.
- **It makes yogurt.** It can hold 110°F for eight hours, which is very hard to do any other way at home.
- **It keeps food warm** for hours after the timer ends, so dinner survives a late arrival.

The cost of all that is speed and lifespan. Building pressure takes ten to fifteen minutes because the element is limited to what a household outlet can supply. A stovetop cooker on a strong gas burner gets there in three or four. And every electronic component is a component that can fail; the [wattage figures for an Instant Pot](/blog/how-many-watts-does-an-instant-pot-use/) tell you how hard those electronics are working.

If you are weighing an Instant Pot against other multi-function machines rather than against a stovetop pot, our [Instant Pot vs Ninja Foodi comparison](/blog/instant-pot-vs-ninja-foodi/) is the more useful read.

## Where Each One Wins

### Speed

Stovetop, and it is not close. It reaches pressure faster, runs at a higher pressure, and cools faster when you want a quick release. On a 20-minute recipe the stovetop pot can finish a third quicker door to door.

### Walking away

Electric. A stovetop cooker needs someone to turn the burner down once the regulator starts moving, and then to keep an ear on it. An Instant Pot needs nothing. If you cook while doing other things, that is worth more than ten minutes.

### Small kitchens

This one splits. A stovetop pressure cooker lives in a cupboard and doubles as a stockpot, so it takes zero counter space, which is a real argument in a studio. An Instant Pot occupies a permanent footprint but replaces a rice cooker, a slow cooker and sometimes a steamer, so the net gain can still be positive. Our [countertop organization ideas for a small kitchen](/blog/countertop-organization-ideas-for-a-small-kitchen/) and [vertical storage ideas for small kitchens](/blog/vertical-storage-ideas-for-small-kitchens/) both cover appliances that have outstayed their welcome.

If you cook for yourself, the calculation shifts again. Our guide to the [best small kitchen appliances for cooking for one](/blog/best-small-kitchen-appliances-for-cooking-for-one/) makes the case for a 3-quart electric model over a 6-quart anything, and [how to stock a small kitchen for solo cooking](/blog/how-to-stock-a-small-kitchen-for-solo-cooking/) covers what it should replace.

### Durability

Stovetop, easily. It is a stainless pot with a gasket. Replace the sealing ring every year or two and it outlasts the kitchen. Presto backs its stainless cookers with a 12-year limited warranty. Electric models carry a warranty measured in single years and die when a board or sensor goes, which is why a broken one is usually a replacement rather than a repair.

### Browning

Electric, by a nose, because the sauté function means no extra pan. But the sauté element is weaker than a gas burner, so searing is slower and often needs batches. If browning matters to you, sear in a separate [stainless steel pan](/blog/what-is-stainless-steel-cookware/) and transfer, and use our guide to [cleaning stainless steel pans](/blog/how-to-clean-stainless-steel-pans/) to deal with the fond afterwards.

### Energy use

Electric, in most cases. A sealed insulated pot pulling about 1,000 watts for a short burst then cycling uses far less energy than an oven at 325°F for three hours, and it does not heat your kitchen. That is why electric pressure cookers turn up repeatedly in our guides to [reducing kitchen energy use in a small apartment](/blog/how-to-reduce-kitchen-energy-use-in-a-small-apartment/) and the [most energy-efficient small kitchen appliances](/blog/most-energy-efficient-small-kitchen-appliances/).

### Canning

Stovetop, and only a model rated as a pressure canner. This is the one place the answer is a safety rule rather than a preference, and it is covered below.

## The Canning Rule You Have to Follow

Do not pressure can in an Instant Pot or any other electric multi-cooker.

The National Center for Home Food Preservation at the University of Georgia [does not support USDA pressure canning processes in electric multi-cookers](https://nchfp.uga.edu/newsflash/canning-in-electric-multi-cookers). Its reasoning is specific: no USDA thermal process work has been done tracking the actual temperature inside jars in these appliances, reaching a pressure reading does not prove the food inside the jar got hot enough, there are no established elevation adjustments, and the cooling phase after processing can run differently from a tested canner, which risks under-processing.

The exception is a dedicated electric canner whose manufacturer has validated its own thermal processing. A general-purpose multi-cooker is not that.

Low-acid foods processed incorrectly are the classic botulism risk, so this is not a rule to test. If home canning is the reason you are shopping, buy a rated stovetop pressure canner and follow a tested recipe.

## Pressure Cooking Safely

Both types are safe appliances when used as designed. Almost every accident traces back to the same handful of mistakes.

1. **Never fill past the max line.** Two-thirds full is the general limit, and half full for foods that foam or expand: beans, pasta, rice, oats, apple sauce. Foam blocks the vent, and a blocked vent is the one failure mode that matters.
2. **Always include enough liquid.** Pressure comes from steam, and steam comes from water. Most 6-quart pots need at least a cup. A dry pot scorches and never seals.
3. **Check the vent and the float valve before every cook.** Hold the lid up to the light. If you cannot see through the vent, clean it.
4. **Replace the sealing ring.** Rubber hardens and shrinks. Once a year for heavy use is reasonable, and a pot that struggles to reach pressure usually needs a new ring rather than a repair.
5. **Never force the lid.** If it will not turn, there is still pressure inside. Wait, or run cold water over a stovetop pot's rim.
6. **Keep your face away from the release valve.** Steam at 240°F burns far worse than boiling water, because condensing steam dumps a large amount of latent heat into your skin all at once. Turn the quick release with a long spoon, not a finger.
7. **Use natural release for foamy or large items.** Beans, stock, pulled pork and anything with a lot of starch should come down on their own for at least ten minutes.

That last point matters for food safety as much as for mess. Meat that comes out of a pressure cooker is fully cooked, but leftovers still follow the usual rules, and our guide to [how long chicken lasts in the fridge](/blog/how-long-does-chicken-last-in-the-fridge/) covers the timings.

## Can You Own Both?

Plenty of people do, and the split is logical: the electric pot handles beans, grains, stock and anything that benefits from being ignored, while the stovetop pot handles the weeknight jobs where ten minutes matters and the canning a multi-cooker cannot do.

If you only have room for one and you are short of counter space, the stovetop pot wins on footprint. If you are short of burners or working in a kitchen with an unreliable stove, the electric one wins.
