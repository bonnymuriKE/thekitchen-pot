A recirculating range hood cannot remove heat or steam. It traps grease and smell in a filter, then pushes the same hot, damp air back into the room.

Sort that out before anything else, because it decides which hoods are even worth a look. Then work out your CFM.

Get the CFM number from your cooktop rather than from the box, and the rest of this decision takes about ten minutes.

## How Much CFM Do You Need?

Two rules cover almost every kitchen. Run both, then buy the bigger number.

**Rule one, by width:** allow 100 CFM for every linear foot of cooktop. This is the long-standing industry guideline and it works fine for electric and induction.

**Rule two, by BTU:** for a gas range, divide the total burner output by 100. Broan puts it simply in its own guidance: aim for [1 CFM of ventilation per 100 BTU](https://broan-nutone.com/en-us/home/customer-service/range-hood-faqs).

| Cooktop width | Width rule (100 CFM per foot) | Gas example | Buy in this range |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 24 inches | 200 CFM | 40,000 BTU needs 400 CFM | 250 to 400 CFM |
| 30 inches | 250 CFM | 50,000 BTU needs 500 CFM | 300 to 500 CFM |
| 36 inches | 300 CFM | 60,000 BTU needs 600 CFM | 400 to 600 CFM |

Gas nearly always wins, because burning gas produces water vapor and combustion gases as well as cooking smoke. An [induction cooktop](/blog/best-induction-cooktop-for-the-money/) produces none of that, so the width rule alone is enough. The same goes for a [slide-in electric range](/blog/best-slide-in-electric-range/), as long as you are not frying every night.

One warning before you chase a big number. Building codes based on the International Residential Code require a makeup air system once a hood moves more than 400 CFM. A powerful fan in a sealed home can pull air back down a flue, so check with your local inspector before buying a 900 CFM hood for a small kitchen.

## Ducted vs Ductless: What Recirculating Really Does

This is the decision that changes everything else, so it deserves plain language.

A ducted hood captures the plume above your pan and pushes it outside through a pipe. Everything in that plume leaves: grease, smoke, odor, steam, heat, and the water vapor and gases that gas burners produce.

A ductless hood pulls the same plume through a grease filter and a charcoal filter, then blows the cleaned air back into the kitchen. Grease and odor drop out. Heat and moisture do not, because there is nowhere for them to go. Anything that vents steam makes this obvious, and a [pressure cooker](/blog/instant-pot-vs-pressure-cooker/) makes it obvious fastest.

| | Ducted | Ductless (recirculating) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Removes grease | Yes | Mostly, via the mesh |
| Removes odor | Yes | Yes, while the carbon lasts |
| Removes heat | Yes | No |
| Removes steam and moisture | Yes | No |
| Removes gas combustion byproducts | Yes | No |
| Ongoing cost | Wash the filters | Replace carbon every 3 to 6 months |
| Install | Needs a duct route | Screws under the cabinet |

The practical upshot is simple. If you can duct, duct. A recirculating hood is a reasonable fallback in a rental, and a poor choice in a kitchen with a gas range, because moisture and combustion gases are exactly what you want gone. Extra humidity also feeds condensation, which is bad news in a small space where [storage is already tight](/blog/how-to-organize-a-small-kitchen-with-no-pantry/).

## Duct Diameter: The Number That Quietly Ruins Airflow

A hood is only as good as the pipe behind it. Fitting a 500 CFM blower to a skinny duct gets you a loud fan and disappointing results.

The reason is cross-sectional area, and the math is unforgiving because area scales with the square of the radius.

| Duct size | Cross-section | Typically good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 4-inch round | About 12.6 sq in | Nothing. This is dryer duct |
| 5-inch round | About 19.6 sq in | Small hoods up to about 200 CFM |
| 6-inch round | About 28.3 sq in | Up to roughly 400 CFM |
| 3.25 by 10 rectangular | About 32.5 sq in | Broan treats it as equal to 6-inch round |
| 7-inch round | About 38.5 sq in | Roughly 400 to 600 CFM |
| 8-inch round | About 50.3 sq in | 600 CFM and up |

Step down from 6 inches to 4 inches and you lose more than half the opening. The blower still spins at the same speed, but it moves far less air and makes much more noise doing it.

Three more rules keep a duct run honest:

1. **Use smooth rigid metal, not flexible.** Broan is explicit that the ridges in flexible duct reduce airflow. Rigid pipe is cheap and makes a real difference.
2. **Keep the run short and straight.** Every elbow adds resistance, so two tight 90-degree bends can cost you as much as several extra feet of pipe.
3. **Never vent into an attic or a crawlspace.** Grease-laden damp air belongs outside, through a wall cap or a roof cap with a damper.

## Sones: What the Noise Rating Means at the Stove

Sones measure perceived loudness, and unlike decibels they are linear. Two sones is twice as loud as one. Broan gives a plain comparison scale, and it is the most useful thing on any hood spec sheet.

| Sones | Broan's comparison | What it means at the stove |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | A refrigerator running | You forget the hood is on |
| 2 | An office | Easy to hold a conversation |
| 3 | Face-to-face conversation | Noticeable but fine |
| 4 | Television at normal volume | You raise your voice a little |
| 5 | A busy restaurant | Talking over it is work |
| 6 | A sporting event | You switch it off early |

Aim for roughly 1.5 sones at normal speed and accept a louder top speed you use for ten minutes. Manufacturers who quote decibels instead are not lying, but decibels are logarithmic and are usually measured on the lowest setting. When a brand gives you sones certified by HVI, you can compare it with another HVI number and trust the result.

A quiet hood gets used. That is not a small point, since the one that gets switched off leaves grease on your cabinets and film on your [stainless pans](/blog/how-to-clean-stainless-steel-pans/).

## Filters: Mesh, Baffle and Charcoal

Filters do two separate jobs, and mixing them up leads to disappointment.

| Filter | How it works | Upkeep | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Aluminum mesh | Layers of woven metal strain grease droplets out of the airstream | Dishwasher monthly | Everyday cooking |
| Stainless baffle | Forces the air to turn sharply, so heavy grease cannot follow and drips into a trap | Dishwasher, lasts for years | Frying, searing, wok cooking |
| Charcoal or carbon | Adsorbs odor molecules in recirculating mode | Replace every 3 to 6 months | Ductless hoods only |

Mesh and baffle filters both handle grease. Charcoal only handles smell, and only in a ductless setup, so a ducted hood never needs one.

Broan recommends washing aluminum mesh filters roughly monthly, depending on how hard you cook. Skipping that is the most common reason a hood seems to lose power. A loaded filter blocks airflow long before it looks dirty, and the grease it holds is also a fire risk. Baffle filters clog more slowly because the grease runs off rather than sitting in the weave, which is why restaurant kitchens use them. Oil that gets past a filter ends up on your cabinets, and it behaves the same way it does on a [worn nonstick pan](/blog/best-nonstick-pans-with-buying-guide/).

## Width, Depth and Height in a Small Kitchen

Specs on paper mean nothing if the hood does not fit the wall you have.

**Width.** Match your cooktop at a minimum. Smoke rises in a plume that widens as it travels, so a hood three inches wider on each side catches more. In a tight kitchen that is rarely possible, so match the width and mount the hood lower in the allowed range instead.

**Depth.** This is the one people forget. Standard wall cabinets are 12 inches deep. A 22-inch hood therefore projects 10 inches into the room, right where you lean over the front burner. A 17.5 or 19.7-inch hood is much easier to live with in a galley, and the picks above include both. Reclaiming that cabinet space is worth planning too, and our [small kitchen cabinet organization ideas](/blog/small-kitchen-cabinet-organization-ideas/) show how.

**Height.** Broan recommends 24 to 30 inches between the cooktop and the bottom of the hood. Mount it higher and the plume spreads before the hood can catch it. Mount it lower and you risk heat damage, plus knocked heads and scorched filters.

Measure the gap between your cooktop and the underside of the cabinet before you shop. Subtract the hood height from that gap and check the result still sits in the 24 to 30-inch window. A 10-inch-tall hood in a shallow gap can leave you below the minimum clearance, which is a safety issue rather than an inconvenience.

## Installing an Under-Cabinet Range Hood

Most under-cabinet hoods are a two-person afternoon if the duct already exists. A new duct run is a different job.

1. **Kill the power at the breaker.** Then confirm it is dead at the outlet or junction box in the cabinet.
2. **Check the duct route first.** Open the cabinet above and find where the pipe can go before you unbox anything. Clear the shelf properly, the same way you would when [reorganizing kitchen drawers](/blog/how-to-organize-kitchen-drawers-in-a-small-kitchen/).
3. **Dry-fit the hood.** Hold it in place and mark the mounting holes and the duct cutout. A second person earns their keep here.
4. **Cut the cabinet bottom or back for the duct.** Use the template Broan and most brands include, and cut a touch small rather than a touch large.
5. **Mount the hood, then connect the duct.** Seal joints with foil tape, not cloth duct tape, which dries out and falls off.
6. **Wire it or plug it in.** Hardwired models need a junction box; models with a fitted cord just need an outlet inside the cabinet.
7. **Test every speed and both lights.** Hold a tissue near the filter on high to confirm the air is actually moving.

If you are replacing an old hood with a microwave that has its own fan, weigh that up carefully. Those fans are usually weaker, and we go through the tradeoffs in our guide to [over-the-range microwaves](/blog/7-best-over-the-range-microwaves/).

## Getting the Best Out of Any Hood

Technique matters almost as much as the spec sheet.

- **Switch it on before you start cooking.** A hood works on a rising plume, and it catches that plume far better once the airflow is already established.
- **Use the back burners for smoky jobs.** They sit deeper under the canopy, so more of the plume gets captured.
- **Leave it running for five to ten minutes afterwards.** Steam and smell linger after the pan comes off.
- **Wash the grease filters monthly.** A clogged filter is the number one cause of a hood that seems to have died.
- **Wipe the canopy while it is warm.** Grease comes off easily then and turns to varnish once it cools, exactly as it does on [hard anodized cookware](/blog/how-to-clean-hard-anodized-cookware/) or an [electric griddle](/blog/how-to-clean-electric-griddle/).
- **Keep the cooktop itself clean.** Burnt residue smokes, which gives the hood more work to do, and it also marks the glass the way [cast iron can](/blog/how-to-protect-glass-top-stove-from-cast-iron/).
- **Crack a window on high speed.** A powerful hood needs replacement air from somewhere, and an open window is the simplest source.

Running the fan on low for longer beats blasting it on high for two minutes. It is also kinder to your power bill, which fits with the rest of our advice on [reducing kitchen energy use in a small apartment](/blog/how-to-reduce-kitchen-energy-use-in-a-small-apartment/). The same logic runs through our picks for the [most energy-efficient small kitchen appliances](/blog/most-energy-efficient-small-kitchen-appliances/) and our list of [eco-friendly appliance alternatives](/blog/eco-friendly-alternatives-to-common-kitchen-appliances/).

Heat is the other half of the problem in a small kitchen. An oven running for an hour warms the whole room, so it helps to know [how to bake without extra counter space](/blog/how-to-bake-in-a-small-kitchen-without-extra-counter-space/) and when to use a countertop appliance instead.
