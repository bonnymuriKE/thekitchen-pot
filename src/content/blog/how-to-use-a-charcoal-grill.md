Charcoal grilling has a reputation for being fussy. It isn't, once you know three things: how much charcoal to use, how to light it, and how to control the heat once it's going.

This guide walks through the whole process, from an empty grill to food on the table, including the small and portable grills that a lot of people are picking up right now.

## The Short Version

Fill a chimney starter with charcoal, light it with a couple of lighter cubes, and wait about 10 to 15 minutes until the coals are covered in gray ash.

Dump the coals into the grill, piled on one side for a two-zone fire. Put the cooking grate on, let it preheat a few minutes, then start cooking.

Sear over the hot side. Move food to the cooler side to finish cooking gently or to avoid flare-ups.

**Lighter cubes or newspaper.** Either works as the ignition source under the chimney. Lighter cubes light more reliably in wind.

## How Much Charcoal to Use

This is one of the most common beginner questions, and the answer depends on what you're cooking.

A full chimney starter, generally 50 to 100 briquettes depending on its size, covers most grilling sessions. Use a full chimney for high-heat searing or when you want a proper two-zone fire with a hot and a cool side.

Use about half a chimney for lighter cooking, shorter sessions, or a small portable grill that doesn't need to cover as much grate area.

Weber's own chimney guidance breaks this down by grill size, since a larger kettle needs more charcoal spread over more grate space than a compact one does.

## Lighting Charcoal Without Lighter Fluid

Lighter fluid works, but it's the reason a lot of grilled food tastes faintly chemical. A chimney starter solves this.

1. Remove the cooking grate so you have access to the charcoal grate underneath.
2. Place two lighter cubes on the charcoal grate.
3. Set the chimney starter over the cubes and fill it with charcoal.
4. Light the cubes through the holes at the bottom of the chimney.
5. Wait 10 to 15 minutes. The charcoal is ready once it's covered in light gray ash.
6. Using grill mitts, dump the lit coals into the grill.

Weber's own grilling guide describes this same process and notes that piling the coals onto one side of the grill, rather than spreading them evenly, sets you up for the two-zone fire covered next.

If you don't have lighter cubes, crumpled newspaper soaked lightly in vegetable oil works as a substitute, though cubes light more reliably in windy conditions.

## Building a Two-Zone Fire

A two-zone fire is the single most useful technique in charcoal grilling, and it's simple once you've done it once.

Instead of spreading the lit coals evenly across the grate, pile them all on one side. That gives you a hot zone for direct, high-heat cooking and a cool zone with no coals underneath for gentler, indirect cooking.

Sear steaks, burgers, or vegetables directly over the hot side. If a flare-up happens, or if something is browning faster than it's cooking through, slide it over to the cool side to finish.

This setup also works as a basic form of smoking. A lower, more even heat on the cool side, with the lid closed, cooks larger cuts slowly without direct flame underneath them. ## Controlling Heat With the Vents

Once the fire is built, the vents are your only control. Learning them well is what separates confident grilling from guessing.

The bottom vent controls how much air reaches the fire from underneath. Open it wide for more air and higher heat. Close it partway to slow the fire down.

The top vent fine-tunes airflow once the fire is established, and it also helps route smoke over the food when the lid is closed.

Never close both vents completely while cooking. That smothers the fire, and a fire starved of air can produce more smoke and bitterness rather than simply going out.

## How to Tell When the Grill Is Ready

Timing charcoal by the clock is a rough guide. Your hand is a better one.

Hold your palm about 5 inches above the grate. If you can only keep it there for 2 to 3 seconds, that's high heat, good for searing. If you can hold it for 4 to 5 seconds, that's medium heat. Anything longer than 6 or 7 seconds is low heat.

Visually, the coals should glow through a coating of gray ash rather than sitting black with open flame. Flames mean the fire is still building; a food-ready fire has settled into a steady glow.

## Grilling Times by Food

These are starting points. Thickness, wind, and your specific grill all shift the numbers slightly.

* **Burgers:** about 4 to 5 minutes per side over direct high heat.
* **Boneless chicken breasts:** about 6 to 8 minutes per side over direct medium heat.
* **Steaks, 1-inch thick:** about 4 to 5 minutes per side over direct high heat, then rest.
* **Bone-in chicken pieces:** sear directly, then move to indirect heat and close the lid for 20 to 30 minutes.
* **Vegetables:** about 8 to 10 minutes total over direct medium heat, turning occasionally.
* **Corn on the cob:** about 15 to 20 minutes, covered, turning every few minutes. Our full guide on [how to grill corn on the cob](/blog/how-to-grill-corn-on-the-cob/) covers husk, foil, and shucked methods in detail.

Always confirm doneness on meat and poultry with a thermometer rather than time alone.

## Safety Basics

A few habits keep charcoal grilling safe, whatever size grill you're using.

Grill outdoors only, and keep the grill well away from siding, railings, and anything overhanging. Never grill in a garage, on an enclosed porch, or indoors, since charcoal produces carbon monoxide as it burns.

Let ash cool completely, ideally overnight, before disposing of it, and dispose of it in a metal container rather than a plastic bin.

Keep a fire extinguisher rated for grease fires nearby, and never leave a lit grill unattended, especially with kids or pets around.

## Cleaning Up

A quick cleanup after each cook keeps the grill working well and makes the next session easier.

Brush the cooking grate while it's still warm, right after you finish cooking. Residue comes off far easier warm than cold.

Once the ash is completely cool, empty it out. A buildup of old ash restricts airflow to the vents and makes the next fire harder to control.

Check the vents periodically for ash blockage, since clogged vents are a common reason a grill that used to run hot suddenly seems sluggish.

## Briquettes vs. Lump Charcoal

You'll see two main types of charcoal at the store, and either works for everything in this guide.

Briquettes are uniform, pressed blocks. They light predictably, burn at a steady pace, and are the easier choice for a beginner who wants consistent results.

Lump charcoal burns hotter and lights faster, but pieces vary in size, so the burn is less even. Many experienced grillers prefer it for the cleaner flavor and the higher heat it can reach.

Start with briquettes while you're learning heat control. Switch to lump charcoal later if you want a hotter sear or a more natural smoke flavor.

## Adding Wood for Extra Smoke Flavor

Charcoal alone gives good flavor. Wood chunks or chips add more.

Soak wood chips in water for about 30 minutes, then scatter a handful directly onto the lit coals. They'll smolder and add smoke without adding much heat.

Wood chunks work better for longer cooks, since they smolder for longer than chips. Add one or two chunks to the hot side of a two-zone fire when you build it.

Fruit woods like apple and cherry give a milder smoke, while hickory and mesquite are stronger. Match the wood to what you're cooking, since a delicate piece of fish can be overwhelmed by a heavy smoke flavor that works fine on a brisket.

## Direct vs. Indirect Grilling, Explained Simply

These two terms come up constantly, and they're just the practical result of the two-zone fire covered earlier.

Direct grilling means food sits right over the coals. It's fast, and it's what gives a hard sear on a burger or a steak.

Indirect grilling means food sits away from the coals, usually with the lid closed, cooking more like a small oven. It's slower, gentler, and it's how you cook something all the way through without burning the outside first.

Most full meals use both. Sear a chicken thigh directly for color, then move it to the indirect side to finish cooking without the skin burning before the inside is done.

## Adding More Charcoal Mid-Cook

Longer cooks sometimes need a charcoal top-up partway through.

Light a fresh partial chimney of charcoal the same way you lit the first batch. Add the fresh, ashed-over coals to the existing pile once they're ready.

Avoid adding unlit charcoal directly to a fire that's already cooking food. It smokes heavily as it catches and can add a harsh, sooty flavor.

## Grilling in Wind, Cold, or Rain

Weather changes how a charcoal fire behaves, and a few adjustments keep things predictable.

Wind feeds a fire more oxygen, so it burns hotter and faster than usual. Close the vents slightly to compensate, and keep a lid or cover nearby in case a lit chimney needs shelter.

Cold weather slows a fire down and can stretch cooking times slightly. Give the grill a few extra minutes to preheat before you start cooking.

Light rain generally isn't a problem once the grill is going and the lid is on, but keep charcoal itself dry beforehand. Wet charcoal lights poorly and burns unevenly.

## Grilling Vegetables and Sides

Charcoal isn't just for meat. Vegetables pick up real flavor from a hot grate.

Cut vegetables into pieces large enough that they won't fall through the grates, or use a grill basket for smaller pieces.

Toss vegetables in oil before they go on, since it helps prevent sticking and promotes browning. Most vegetables cook well over direct medium heat, turning occasionally.

## What If You Don't Have a Chimney Starter Yet?

A chimney starter is worth buying, but you can still grill without one while you decide.

Stack briquettes in a loose pyramid in the center of the charcoal grate. Tuck a couple of lighter cubes into the gaps near the bottom of the pile and light them.

Leave the grill uncovered while the pile catches. Once the coals are glowing and starting to ash over, spread them out into your two-zone setup.

This method works, but it's slower and less consistent than a chimney. Most grillers who try a chimney once don't go back.

## Storing Charcoal and Your Grill

A little care between cooks keeps both your charcoal and your grill working well.

Store charcoal in a sealed container or its original bag in a dry place. Damp charcoal lights poorly and can develop mold over time.

Cover the grill when it's not in use, especially if it lives outside year-round. A cover keeps rain out of the vents and slows rust on the bowl and grates.

Empty cold ash between uses rather than letting it build up. A layer of old ash blocks airflow and makes the next fire harder to control.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

* **Using too much lighter fluid, or any at all with a chimney available.** It adds a chemical taste that's easy to avoid.
* **Spreading coals evenly instead of building a two-zone fire.** You lose the safety and flexibility of a cool side.
* **Cooking before the coals are ashed over.** Black, flaming coals are still building heat, not holding steady heat.
* **Closing both vents completely.** This smothers the fire instead of controlling it.
* **Skipping the thermometer on meat and poultry.** Color and time are rough guides at best.

## **The Bottom Line**

Charcoal grilling comes down to three things. Light it with a chimney, not lighter fluid. Build a two-zone fire. Control heat with the vents. A full-size kettle and a small portable grill both work the same way. Master these basics once and you'll use them for years.
