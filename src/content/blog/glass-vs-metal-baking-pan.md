Pour the same brownie batter into a glass dish and a light metal pan, and you get two different brownies. One has dark, chewy corners. The other bakes evenly from edge to middle.

The glass vs metal baking pan question is really about heat: how fast a pan takes it in, how long it holds it, and what that does to your crust. Once you know that, picking the pan, the oven setting and the timer becomes easy.

**Quick answer:** Metal heats fast and cools fast, so it gives even browning, crisp edges and good rise. Use it for cakes, brownies, bread, cookies and roasting. Glass heats slowly but holds heat for a long time, which suits casseroles, lasagna, cobblers and pies. When a recipe written for metal goes into glass, lower the oven by 25°F and expect a few extra minutes. Never put glass under the broiler, on a burner or into a toaster oven.

## How Glass and Metal Pans Handle Heat

Metal is a strong heat conductor. That means heat moves through it quickly. Aluminum moves heat many times faster than glass. When you slide a metal pan into a hot oven, it reaches oven temperature in a few minutes and starts cooking the batter from the sides and bottom right away.

Glass is the opposite. It is an insulator, so it takes a while to warm up. Once it is hot, though, it stays hot. It keeps pouring heat into the food long after the oven has stopped warming it.

That one difference explains almost everything else:

- **In metal,** the batter at the edges and the batter in the center cook at close to the same pace. You get even rise, flat tops and uniform color.
- **In glass,** the edges get a slow start, then take in a lot of stored heat. By the time the center is set, the edges and bottom are darker and firmer.
- **Out of the oven,** a metal pan cools within minutes. A glass dish keeps cooking the food for 10 minutes or more. This is called carryover cooking, and it is why bars baked in glass often taste overbaked even when they looked right at the toothpick test.

Clear glass also lets the oven's radiant heat pass through to the food. Radiant heat is the heat that shines off the oven walls and elements, like sunlight on your skin. That is part of why glass browns the bottom of a pie crust so well, and part of why it can scorch the bottom of a cake.

## Light Metal vs Dark Metal

Light, shiny pans reflect radiant heat. They bake gently and give golden, even crusts. Most recipes assume one. The [Nordic Ware Naturals Half Sheet](https://www.amazon.com/Nordic-Ware-half-sheet-pan/dp/B000G0KJG4/?tag=kitchenpot-20) is the classic example: bare aluminum, rolled edges and a steel rim that helps it stay flat. For a smaller oven, the [Nordic Ware Naturals Quarter Sheet](https://www.amazon.com/Nordic-Ware-Natural-Aluminum-Quarter-Sheet/dp/B000RYPHRK/?tag=kitchenpot-20) bakes the same way in a 9x13-inch footprint.

Dark and nonstick pans absorb radiant heat. They run hotter at the same oven setting, so crusts brown faster and deeper. That is great for crusty bread and pizza. It is a problem for a delicate vanilla cake. Our guide to [what nonstick cookware is](/blog/what-is-nonstick-cookware/) explains the coatings on dark bakeware and how to keep them from wearing out.

With a dark pan, lower the oven 25°F for cakes, cookies and quick breads, or start checking 5 to 10 minutes early. If dark pans are what you already own, that one change fixes most burnt bottoms.

Thin metal has its own issue: it warps. See [why baking sheets warp in the oven](/blog/why-baking-sheets-warp-in-the-oven/) for the cause, and [how to fix a warped pan](/blog/how-to-fix-a-warped-pan/) to flatten one.

## Baking in a Glass Pan Instead of Metal: The 25°F Rule

This is the rule most people half remember. Here is when it applies and when it does not.

**Lower the oven by 25°F when:**

- The recipe was written for a metal pan (most cake, brownie and bar recipes are).
- You are baking batter or dough that needs to rise and set evenly: cakes, brownies, blondies, bars, quick breads, cornbread, coffee cake.

**Keep the oven where the recipe says when:**

- The recipe already calls for a glass or ceramic dish. The writer has already allowed for it.
- You are making a casserole, lasagna, baked pasta, mac and cheese or a gratin. These are wet, dense dishes that need steady heat all the way through. Dropping the oven only stretches the time.
- You are baking a pie in a glass pie plate. Most pie recipes expect glass or a metal plate at the same temperature, and the bottom crust needs that full heat to cook through.
- You are roasting meat to a safe internal temperature. Use a thermometer and the recipe's temperature.

Why 25°F? It slows the outside enough for the center to catch up.

One more check before you blame the pan: many home ovens run 15 to 25°F off. A hot oven plus a glass pan is the usual cause of dark, dry edges. A [Taylor Precision Oven Thermometer](https://www.amazon.com/Taylor-3506-Precision-Oven-Thermometer/dp/B000BQWMTK/?tag=kitchenpot-20) sits on the rack and tells you the real number, so you adjust for the oven and the pan separately.

## Glass vs Metal Baking Pan Cooking Time

Glass warms up slowly, then cooks hard, so the two effects partly cancel out. Most bakes in glass take the same time to about 10 minutes longer than the metal-pan time, after the temperature drop. Start checking at the original time. Use doneness signs, not the clock:

- **Cakes:** a toothpick in the center comes out clean or with a few dry crumbs, and the top springs back when pressed.
- **Brownies:** a toothpick shows moist crumbs, not wet batter. Pull them a little early in glass because carryover heat keeps cooking them.
- **Quick breads:** a skewer in the thickest part comes out clean, and the center reads about 200 to 205°F.
- **Yeast bread:** an internal temperature of 190 to 210°F.

## Glass or Metal Pan for Brownies

Metal, if you want every square the same. A light metal pan gives a fudgy center and edges only slightly firmer. Glass gives chewier, darker corners. If you love those, glass is fine. Lower the oven 25°F and pull the pan when the toothpick still shows moist crumbs.

For a 9x13 metal pan that releases cleanly, the [USA Pan Nonstick Rectangular Cake Pan, 9 x 13-inch](https://www.amazon.com/USA-Pan-Bakeware-Rectangular-Aluminized/dp/B0029JOC6I/?tag=kitchenpot-20) is a solid choice. It is aluminized steel, which means steel with a thin aluminum coating, and the ridged bottom lets air move under the batter. If you would rather buy from a baking supply shop, the [Winco Aluminized Steel Rectangular Cake Pan, 13 x 9 x 2-1/4"](https://www.awin1.com/cread.php?awinmid=88997&awinaffid=1956629&clickref=glass-vs-metal-baking-pan&ued=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.bakedeco.com%2Fdetail.asp%3Fid%3D68319) is a commercial-style pan. Its extra depth helps with tall cakes and layered bars.

For half batches, an 8-inch square does the job. The [USA Pan 8-Inch Square Cake Pan](https://www.amazon.com/USA-Pan-Bakeware-Square-Cake-Pan/dp/B0029JMMRE/?tag=kitchenpot-20) holds about half of a 9x13 recipe.

## Glass or Metal Pan for Cake

Metal for cake, almost every time. Cakes depend on even heat so the batter rises before the outside sets. In glass, the edges set early and the middle keeps rising. That gives you a domed top, a darker crust and dry edges.

If glass is all you have, lower the oven 25°F, line the bottom with parchment and fill it no more than two-thirds full. Start checking at the original time. Turn the cake out after 10 minutes so carryover heat does not dry it. For layer cakes, always use round metal pans.

## Glass or Metal Pan for Bread and Banana Bread

Yeast bread wants metal. A metal loaf pan helps the dough rise fast in the first minutes of baking, which is called oven spring. It also gives a thin, golden crust on the sides.

Banana bread and other quick breads can go either way. In a glass loaf dish, lower the oven 25°F and expect up to 10 extra minutes. The sides will be darker and slightly crisper. Because glass is clear, you can also check how brown the bottom is without turning the loaf out.

For frequent loaves, see our [best bread machine](/blog/best-bread-machine/) guide. For crusty free-form loaves and pizza, a [pizza stone](/blog/best-pizza-stone/) beats any pan.

## Glass or Metal Pan for Lasagna and Casseroles

Glass or ceramic wins here. Lasagna, baked ziti and casseroles are thick and wet. They need steady heat that soaks to the center, not a fast blast at the edges.

There is a second reason: tomato sauce. Acidic foods react with bare aluminum. Over a long bake, tomato, wine, vinegar or lemon can darken the pan, pit the surface and give the food a metallic taste. Glass, ceramic and coated metal do not react.

The [Pyrex Easy Grab 3-Quart Oblong Baking Dish with Lid](https://www.amazon.com/Pyrex-Easy-3-Quart-Oblong-Baking/dp/B005JCZ0HC/?tag=kitchenpot-20) is the standard 9x13 glass dish. The wide handles help when the dish is full and heavy. The plastic lid is for the fridge only, never the oven. For two people, the [Pyrex Deep 8-Inch Square Baking Dish with Lid](https://www.amazon.com/Pyrex-Deep-8-Inch-Square-Glass-Baking-Dish-Sage-Lid/dp/B087TNQPGL/?tag=kitchenpot-20) is deep enough for a small lasagna with four layers.

One more lasagna tip. If you cover the dish with foil and the foil touches tomato sauce, small holes can form in the foil over a day or two in the fridge. Lay a sheet of parchment on the food first, then the foil. Or use the dish's own lid.

## Glass vs Metal Pan for Chicken and Roasting

It depends on the result you want.

**For crisp skin and browned vegetables, use metal.** A light metal sheet pan at 400 to 425°F lets moisture escape, so food roasts instead of steaming. Spread food in one layer with space between pieces. This is the same idea behind most of our [one-pan meals for one](/blog/one-pan-meals-for-one/) and why we roast [sausages in the oven](/blog/how-to-cook-sausages-in-the-oven/) on a sheet pan.

**For saucy or braised chicken, glass or ceramic works well.** Chicken thighs baked in sauce, chicken with rice or a creamy bake all benefit from gentle, even heat. Add a little liquid to the bottom of a glass dish before it goes in. That keeps hot fat from spitting onto dry, very hot glass.

Either way, cook chicken to 165°F in the thickest part. Leftovers keep 3 to 4 days in the fridge; our guide on [how long chicken lasts in the fridge](/blog/how-long-does-chicken-last-in-the-fridge/) covers raw and cooked.

For larger roasts, a heavy pot with a lid often beats both. A good [Dutch oven](/blog/best-dutch-oven/) braises and roasts at the same time.

## Glass or Metal for Pie and Cheesecake

### Pie

Pie is where glass shines. A glass pie plate heats steadily and holds that heat, so the bottom crust cooks through. You can also lift the plate and see whether the bottom is golden or still pale. That one view prevents most soggy bottoms.

The [Pyrex Easy Grab 9.5-Inch Glass Pie Plate](https://www.amazon.com/Pyrex-Easy-Grab-Glass-Plate/dp/B0069KDUXO/?tag=kitchenpot-20) has small handles on the rim, which makes a heavy fruit pie much easier to move. Bake it on the lowest rack for the first part of the bake so the bottom crust gets the most heat. Do not lower the oven for a pie in glass unless the recipe tells you to.

Metal pie pans brown faster and suit blind baking, which means baking the empty crust first. Dark metal can burn the edges, so add a foil ring or crust shield after 20 to 25 minutes.

### Cheesecake

Use metal. Cheesecake bakes low, often 300 to 325°F, and sits in a water bath. That is a pan of hot water around the cake pan, which keeps the custard from cracking. Pouring water around a hot glass dish is a thermal shock risk, so glass is the wrong tool here.

For cheesecake bars, the [Fat Daddio's Anodized Aluminum Sheet Cake Pan with Removable Bottom, 9 x 13 x 2](https://www.amazon.com/Fat-Daddios-Anodized-Cheesecake-Removable/dp/B001BQ7CEO/?tag=kitchenpot-20) lets you push the whole slab out and cut clean squares. Anodized aluminum is aluminum treated to make a hard, sealed surface, so it does not react with lemon or fruit toppings. Wrap the bottom in heavy foil before a water bath.

## Ceramic and Stoneware: The Third Option

Ceramic and stoneware bake a lot like glass. They heat slowly, hold heat even longer and brown gently. Food stays hot at the table, which suits gratins, cobblers and baked potatoes. They are opaque and heavier than glass. Good stoneware also handles temperature swings better than glass, but check the label of the dish you own.

It is made to handle freezer-to-oven moves, it can go under the broiler for a cheesy top, and the glaze does not react with acidic sauces.

## Glass Bakeware Safety: How to Avoid Thermal Shock

Thermal shock is what happens when one part of a glass dish heats or cools much faster than another. The glass expands unevenly and can crack or shatter. Most glass bakeware sold in the US is tempered soda-lime glass. It is tough against bumps, but it does not like sudden temperature changes.

Follow these rules every time:

1. **No broiler.** The direct, intense heat on one side can break the dish.
2. **No stovetop, grill or open flame.** Glass is for the oven, not a burner.
3. **No toaster oven.** The elements sit close to the dish and act like a broiler. If you bake in a small oven, use metal; see [can you bake in a toaster oven](/blog/can-you-bake-in-a-toaster-oven/) for what works.
4. **Always preheat first.** Put glass into a fully preheated oven, not one that is still heating with its elements on full.
5. **No cold liquid into a hot dish.** Adding cold broth to a hot glass dish, or pouring water around it for a water bath, can crack it. Warm the liquid first, or use a metal pan.
6. **No fridge to hot oven unless the dish is labeled for it.** Let a chilled dish sit on the counter for 20 to 30 minutes first.
7. **No hot dish onto a cold or wet surface.** Set it on a dry towel, a trivet or a wooden board. Never on a wet counter, a cold stone surface, a metal sink or the stovetop.
8. **Add some liquid when roasting.** A thin layer in the bottom keeps fat and juices from hitting dry, very hot glass.
9. **Retire chipped or scratched dishes.** A chip is a weak point where cracks start.

If a dish breaks in the oven, turn it off, let it cool fully and throw away the food. Our guide on [how to clean an oven](/blog/how-to-clean-an-oven/) helps with the burnt-on mess afterward.

## Storage, Lids and Leftovers

Glass earns its spot here. A glass dish with a lid goes from oven to table to fridge, so you wash one dish instead of three.

- **Glass with a lid** is the best choice for bake-ahead meals and leftovers. Let it cool before the lid goes on. Cooked leftovers keep 3 to 4 days in the fridge; see [how long leftovers last](/blog/how-long-do-leftovers-last/).
- **Freezing in glass** is fine if the dish is freezer safe. Leave an inch of space at the top. To bake from frozen, thaw overnight in the fridge, then rest it on the counter before it goes into a preheated oven. Our guide to [freezing single portions](/blog/how-to-freeze-single-portions/) has the rest.
- **Metal pans are not storage containers.** Bare aluminum reacts with acidic leftovers, and most metal pans have no airtight lid. Move leftovers into a sealed container.

Stand glass dishes on edge with a divider and keep lids in a bin. Our guides on [storing pots and pans in a small kitchen](/blog/store-pots-and-pans-in-a-small-kitchen/) and [organizing pot lids](/blog/how-to-organize-pot-lids-in-a-small-kitchen/) show setups that work in tight cabinets.

## Acidic Foods and Aluminum Pans

- **Bare aluminum** reacts with acidic foods: tomato sauce, citrus, vinegar, wine and some fruit fillings. A short fruit crumble is fine. A long lasagna bake, or storing sauce in the pan overnight, causes discoloring and a metallic taste.
- **Anodized aluminum** has a hard, sealed surface. It does not react, so it is safe for acidic foods.
- **Nonstick-coated pans** do not react, as long as the coating is not scratched through.
- **Aluminized steel** is steel with an aluminum coating. Treat uncoated aluminized steel like bare aluminum for long, acidic bakes.

Hand wash bare aluminum, since the dishwasher turns it gray. For anodized pans, see [how to clean hard-anodized cookware](/blog/how-to-clean-hard-anodized-cookware/) for what to use and what to avoid.

## Glass vs Metal Baking Pan: Our Verdict

Pick metal when the bake needs to rise, brown evenly or crisp: cakes, brownies, bread, cookies, cinnamon rolls, cheesecake and roasted food. A light-colored pan is the safest default, because most recipes are written for it.

Pick glass when the dish is wet, dense or acidic, or when you want to watch the bottom crust: lasagna, casseroles, cobblers and pies. Glass also wins for anything you plan to store or reheat in the same dish.

Pick ceramic when you want glass-style baking with better heat holding and a dish that looks good on the table.

When you switch a metal recipe into glass, lower the oven 25°F, start checking at the original time and let carryover heat do some of the work. Keep glass away from the broiler, the burner and sudden cold, and it will last for decades.
