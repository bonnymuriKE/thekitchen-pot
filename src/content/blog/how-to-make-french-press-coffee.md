A French press has no paper filter and no machine to cover for you. Grind, water and timing decide the cup, and that is the whole trick to making French press coffee well.

This method fixes the two complaints people have most: a bitter edge and gritty sludge at the bottom of the mug. Below you will find how much coffee to use for any size of press, how coarse to grind, how long to steep, and how to clean the press without clogging your sink.

**Bottom line:** Use 1 gram of coffee for every 15 grams of water, which is about 3 level tablespoons of coarse grounds per 8 ounces. Grind as coarse as rough sea salt. Pour water about 30 seconds off the boil, steep 4 minutes, skim the crust, then press slowly and pour all the coffee out right away. If it tastes bitter, grind coarser. If it tastes thin, add more coffee.

## What You Need to Make French Press Coffee

The kit is small, which is part of the appeal in a tight kitchen. A press, a grinder, a kettle and a spoon will do. A scale is optional but makes every pot taste the same.

**The press.** A glass press lets you see the grounds rise and settle, which helps while you are learning. The [Bodum Chambord 8-Cup French Press](https://www.amazon.com/Bodum-Chambord-French-Coffee-Chrome/dp/B00008XEWG/?tag=kitchenpot-20) is the classic shape: a 34-ounce heat-proof glass beaker in a stainless steel frame, with a mesh filter you can take apart. One thing confuses almost everyone. A "cup" on a French press means 4 ounces, not a mug. An 8-cup press makes about three normal mugs, and a 3-cup press makes one.

**A burr grinder.** Fresh, even grounds matter more than the press. More on this in the grind section below.

**A kettle.** Any kettle works. One with temperature control takes the guessing out of "just off the boil."

**A scale.** A small coffee scale such as the [Greater Goods Digital Coffee Scale](https://www.amazon.com/GreaterGoods-Digital-Pocket-Letter-Accuracy/dp/B07SJTXGN7/?tag=kitchenpot-20) weighs to a tenth of a gram and fits in a drawer. ## French Press Coffee Ratio by Press Size

Our standard ratio is 1:15. That means 1 gram of coffee for every 15 grams of water, and 1 gram of water is 1 milliliter. It makes a full, rich cup without being harsh.

Do not fill the press right to the top. Leave about an inch of space so the crust has room and the plunger does not push coffee out of the spout. The water amounts below already allow for that.

| Press size | Full capacity | Water to pour | Coffee (grams) | Coffee (level tablespoons) | Makes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3-cup | 12 oz (350 ml) | 10 oz (300 ml) | 20 g | 4 tbsp | 1 mug |
| 8-cup | 34 oz (1 liter) | 30 oz (900 ml) | 60 g | 12 tbsp (3/4 cup) | 2 to 3 mugs |
| 12-cup | 51 oz (1.5 liters) | 46 oz (1.35 liters) | 90 g | 18 tbsp (1 cup plus 2 tbsp) | 4 mugs |

The tablespoon numbers assume about 5 grams per level tablespoon of coarse grounds. Whole beans and different roasts weigh a little differently, so treat spoons as a starting point.

### How to Make It Stronger or Lighter

Change the amount of coffee, not the steep time. More time mostly adds bitterness. More coffee adds flavor and body.

| Strength | Ratio | 8-cup press (900 ml water) | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strong | 1:12 | 75 g | Coffee with lots of milk |
| Standard | 1:15 | 60 g | Most people, black or with a splash of milk |
| Lighter | 1:17 | 53 g | Light roasts, long sipping |

If you add a lot of milk, go strong. Our guide to [milk substitutes](/blog/best-substitutes-for-whole-milk/) covers oat, soy and other options that hold up in hot coffee.

### How Much Coffee for One Cup

For one 10-ounce mug, use 20 grams of coffee and 300 milliliters of water. That is 4 level tablespoons of coarse grounds and 1 1/4 cups of water. For two mugs, double both numbers.

You can make one mug in a big press, but the small amount of water cools faster in all that glass. A 3-cup press holds the heat better and leaves less to wash.

### French Press Coffee Ratio Without a Scale

No scale is fine. Use 3 level tablespoons of coarse grounds for every 8 ounces (1 cup) of water. Use the same spoon every time and level it with a knife.

Measure the water with a measuring cup the first few times. After that, note where the water line sits on your press and pour to that mark.

## The Right Grind Size for French Press

French press coffee needs a coarse grind. It should look like rough sea salt or dry breadcrumbs, with pieces you can see clearly.

There are two reasons. First, the grounds sit in water for 4 minutes. Big pieces give up their flavor at the right speed over that time, while fine powder over-brews and turns bitter. Second, the press filters through metal mesh, not paper. Fine particles slip through the mesh and end up as sludge in your mug.

### Why a Burr Grinder Beats a Blade Grinder

A blade grinder chops beans like a blender. You get big chunks and fine dust in the same batch. The chunks taste sour and weak, and the dust tastes bitter and clouds the cup.

A burr grinder crushes beans between two rough plates set a fixed distance apart, so the pieces come out close to the same size. That evenness is the single biggest upgrade for a French press.

### Using Pre-Ground Coffee

You can make French press coffee with pre-ground coffee. Buy a bag labeled coarse or "French press," or ask the shop to grind it that way. Most supermarket ground coffee is a medium grind made for drip machines. It works, but expect more sediment and a slightly bitter edge. Cut the steep to 3 minutes to help.

### Keep Beans Fresh

Whole beans taste best within about two to four weeks of the roast date. Ground coffee goes flat within days, which is why grinding right before brewing makes such a difference.

Store beans in an opaque, airtight container at room temperature, away from the stove and sunny windows. ## Water Temperature: Just Off the Boil

The best water temperature for French press coffee is about 195°F to 205°F. That is just under boiling.

Without a thermometer, bring the water to a full boil, then turn it off and wait about 30 seconds. That is close enough. With very dark roasts, wait a full minute, since they turn bitter more easily.

## How to Make French Press Coffee, Step by Step

This is the full method for an 8-cup press. For other sizes, use the amounts in the ratio table.

1. **Heat the water.** Bring about 1.2 liters to a boil. You need 900 milliliters for the coffee and a little extra to warm the press.
2. **Warm the press.** Pour some hot water into the empty press, swirl it for a few seconds and tip it out. A warm beaker keeps the brew from cooling too fast.
3. **Weigh and grind.** Weigh 60 grams of beans and grind them coarse. Tip the grounds into the press and shake it gently to level them.
4. **Bloom.** Start a 4-minute timer. Pour about 120 grams of water, twice the weight of the coffee, so all the grounds get wet. Wait 30 seconds.
5. **Pour the rest.** Add water up to 900 grams total. Pour in a circle so the grounds mix in. Set the lid on top with the plunger pulled all the way up.
6. **Wait.** Let it steep until the timer reads 4 minutes from the first pour.
7. **Break the crust and skim.** Stir the floating layer gently with a wooden spoon. Spoon off the foam and any bits still floating.
8. **Press slowly.** Push the plunger down with steady, light pressure. It should take about 20 to 30 seconds.
9. **Pour it all out.** Serve every drop right away, or pour the extra into a thermal carafe.

### Why the Bloom Matters

Freshly roasted coffee holds carbon dioxide gas. When hot water hits the grounds, the gas rushes out and makes them bubble and swell. That is the "bloom."

The gas pushes water away from the coffee. A short bloom lets it escape first, so the rest of the water can reach every piece evenly. Very fresh beans bloom a lot; old beans barely move.

### Breaking the Crust

After a few minutes, the grounds form a thick crust on top. Under it, the water has been brewing evenly, but the crust itself has been sitting half dry.

Stirring the crust at the 4-minute mark lets those grounds sink. Skimming the foam removes the fine bits and bitter oils that collect there. This one small step makes the cup noticeably cleaner.

### Plunge Slowly

Pressing hard forces fine grounds around the edges of the filter. Use light, steady pressure. If the plunger is very hard to push, the grind is too fine. Pull it up a little and press again gently, then grind coarser next time.

### Pour It Out Right Away

This is the step most people skip. The plunger does not stop the brew. The grounds at the bottom still sit in coffee, and it keeps getting more bitter. Pour every cup as soon as you press, and move any extra into a thermal carafe.

## How Long to Steep French Press Coffee

Four minutes is the standard steep time for French press coffee, and it is where you should start. Count it from the first pour of water, not from when you finish pouring.

Use these as your adjustments:

- **3 minutes** for a medium grind or pre-ground drip coffee.
- **4 minutes** for a normal coarse grind with medium or dark beans.
- **5 to 6 minutes** for a very coarse grind or light roasts that taste sour at 4 minutes.

Decaf is roasted and processed a little differently, but 4 minutes still works for most decaf beans. Taste and adjust in small steps.

Remember that steeping longer does not make coffee much stronger. It mostly makes it more bitter. For a stronger cup, add more coffee.

## The No-Plunge Method for Less Sediment

A French press always leaves a little silt in the cup. That is part of its heavy texture. If you want a much cleaner cup without buying anything, try this slower method.

1. Brew exactly as above for 4 minutes.
2. Stir the crust gently so it sinks.
3. Skim off all the foam and floating bits with two spoons.
4. Put the lid back on and wait another 5 to 8 minutes. Do not press yet. The fine particles sink to the bottom in that time.
5. Push the plunger down only until it touches the surface of the coffee. It now works as a strainer, not a press.
6. Pour slowly and steadily. Leave the last ounce or so in the press, since that is where the silt sits.

The coffee is a little cooler, but it tastes sweeter and clearer, with very little grit. The longer wait does not make it bitter, because the grounds have settled and are barely brewing.

## Fixing Bitter, Weak or Muddy French Press Coffee

Change one thing at a time, then taste again.

| What it tastes like | Most likely cause | What to change |
|---|---|---|
| Bitter, dry, harsh | Grind too fine, or coffee left on the grounds | Grind coarser; pour out right after pressing |
| Sour, thin, weak | Too little coffee or water too cool | Use 1:15 or stronger; pour 30 seconds off the boil |
| Muddy, gritty | Grind too fine or uneven; pressed too fast | Use a burr grinder; press slowly; try the no-plunge method |
| Flat, dull | Stale beans or pre-ground coffee | Buy fresh whole beans; grind just before brewing |
| Oily, stale aftertaste | Dirty filter holding old oils | Take the filter apart and wash it |
| Strong but bitter | Steeped too long | Keep 4 minutes; add coffee instead of time |

### How to Make French Press Coffee Less Bitter

Grind coarser first. That fixes most bitter pots. Next, make sure you pour the coffee off the grounds right after pressing. Then clean the filter, because old oils trapped in the mesh taste rancid and bitter.

### How to Make French Press Coffee Stronger

Add coffee. Move from 60 grams to 70 or 75 grams in an 8-cup press. If it is strong but still tastes thin, grind one step finer.

## Iced Coffee and Cold Brew in a French Press

A French press does both well. Here is the short version of each.

**Quick iced coffee.** Make it hot, but stronger. In an 8-cup press, use 60 grams of coffee with only 450 milliliters of hot water. Steep 4 minutes, press, and pour it straight over a large glass packed with about 450 grams of ice. The ice melts and brings it back to normal strength while chilling it fast. A countertop [ice maker](/blog/best-ice-maker-for-home-use/) helps if you do this daily in summer.

**Cold brew.** Stir 1 part coarse coffee into 8 parts cold water, about 100 grams of coffee to 800 milliliters of water. Put the lid on, with the plunger up, and leave it in the fridge for 12 to 18 hours. Press slowly, then pour the concentrate into a jar. Mix it about half and half with water or milk, and keep it in the fridge for up to a week.

If cold brew becomes a habit, a dedicated jar or tower makes bigger batches with less mess. Our [cold brew coffee maker guide](/blog/best-cold-brew-coffee-maker/) covers those.

## How to Clean a French Press

A clean press is a better press. Old coffee oils cling to the mesh and turn rancid, and they make every new pot taste stale.

### Never Pour Grounds Down the Drain

Coffee grounds do not dissolve. They settle in pipes, mix with grease and build into clogs. Garbage disposals do not fix this.

Instead, use a spoon or spatula to scoop most of the grounds out. Then add a little water, swirl, and pour the rest through a fine sieve over the trash or compost bin. Grounds make good compost, and an [electric kitchen composter](/blog/best-electric-kitchen-composter/) handles them well in an apartment. Keep the trash sealed so wet grounds do not draw fruit flies; our guide to [keeping pests out of a small kitchen](/blog/how-to-keep-pests-out-of-a-small-kitchen/) has more on that.

### Daily Rinse

After you empty the grounds, rinse the beaker and the plunger with hot water. Pump the plunger up and down a few times in warm, soapy water to flush the mesh. Rinse well and leave it to dry with the lid off. A compact rack from our [dish drying rack ideas](/blog/dish-drying-rack-ideas-for-small-kitchens/) gives the beaker a safe spot to drip.

### Taking the Filter Apart

Every few days, take the filter apart. On most presses, including the Bodum Chambord, you hold the plunger rod and unscrew the small nut or base at the bottom. Three pieces come off: a cross plate, the mesh screen and a spiral plate that presses the mesh against the glass.

Wash each piece in warm, soapy water with a soft brush. Look closely at the mesh. Any brown film or trapped grounds are what make coffee taste stale. Put the pieces back in the same order, with the spiral plate on top, and screw the base back on firmly.

### Deep Clean With Vinegar or Baking Soda

Once a week, or when the glass looks cloudy, soak the filter parts in equal parts white vinegar and warm water for 20 minutes. Rinse well. For stubborn oil stains in the beaker, rub with a paste of baking soda and water, then rinse.

Most glass beakers and steel parts can go in the dishwasher, but check the manual for your press first. Do not pour boiling water into a glass beaker that is cold from a chilly room or the fridge. Replace a chipped or cracked beaker right away, since it can break under heat.

## Habits That Ruin a Pot of French Press Coffee

These are the mistakes behind most disappointing pots.

- **Leaving coffee in the press.** It keeps brewing. Pour it all out or into a thermal carafe as soon as you press.
- **Grinding too fine.** It makes bitter, muddy coffee and a plunger that fights back.
- **Using a blade grinder.** The mix of dust and chunks gives sour and bitter flavors in the same cup.
- **Guessing the coffee amount.** A heaped scoop one day and a level one the next means a different pot every day.
- **Pressing hard and fast.** It pushes grit past the filter and can splash hot coffee.
- **Never taking the filter apart.** A quick rinse does not reach the oils trapped between the layers.
- **Using old beans.** No method can rescue coffee that went stale months ago.

Each method makes a different style of cup.
