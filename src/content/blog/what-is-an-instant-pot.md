An Instant Pot is not a slow cooker with extra buttons. It is an electric pressure cooker with a computer in the base, and that computer is the whole point.

The lid locks and the steam has nowhere to go. Pressure climbs, water gets hotter than it ever could in an open pan, and a three-hour pot roast is done in about an hour.

> **Short answer:** An Instant Pot is a countertop electric pressure cooker with preset programs. A sealed lid traps steam, so pressure rises and water boils well above 212°F. Sensors and a microprocessor hold that pressure for the time you set, then drop the pot to Keep Warm. The same machine also sautés, steams, slow cooks, makes rice and ferments yogurt.

## What an Instant Pot Actually Is

Lift the lid off and only four parts really matter.

- **The inner pot.** A removable stainless steel liner that holds the food.
- **The heating element.** A flat electric plate in the base, sitting under the liner.
- **The lid.** It twists into place and locks, and a silicone ring seals the gap.
- **The control board.** Sensors, a microprocessor, and the buttons on the front.

That control board is the difference between this and the jiggle-top pot your grandmother kept under the sink. Sensors read the temperature at the base and the pressure inside the chamber. The board then switches the element on and off to hold a target. You never stand over a burner adjusting the flame, and the pot will not open while pressure remains inside.

The first Instant Pot went on sale in 2010, and the name soon stuck to the whole category. Plenty of other brands build the same kind of machine now. The best-known rival gets a full comparison in [Instant Pot vs Ninja Foodi](/blog/instant-pot-vs-ninja-foodi/), and the older stovetop design is covered in [Instant Pot vs pressure cooker](/blog/instant-pot-vs-pressure-cooker/).

Most 6-quart models pull around 1,000 watts while they heat. That sounds like a lot, but the element cycles off once pressure is reached. The running cost is worked out properly in [how many watts an Instant Pot uses](/blog/how-many-watts-does-an-instant-pot-use/).

## Why Pressure Makes Food Cook Faster

Water boils at 212°F at sea level. An open pan cannot get hotter than that, no matter how high you crank the burner. Extra heat just makes more steam, and the steam leaves.

Seal the lid and the steam is trapped. Pressure builds, and higher pressure pushes the boiling point up with it. Instant Pot [publishes the numbers](https://instantpot.com/pages/frequently-asked-questions): high pressure runs 10.2 to 11.6 psi above the surrounding air, and low pressure runs 5.8 to 7.2 psi. A few models add a Max setting at 15 psi. The steam tables used in pressure canning put 10 psi at roughly 240°F.

Those extra thirty degrees change everything about tough food. Collagen in a chuck roast melts into gelatin far sooner. Dried beans soften without an overnight soak. Bones give up their flavour in an hour instead of six, which is why stock is the single best argument for owning one.

Two other things happen inside a sealed pot. Nothing evaporates, so flavour stays in the liquid instead of drifting off with the steam. Pressure also drives liquid into the food, so grains and beans hydrate evenly rather than blowing out at the edges.

There is one catch that surprises new owners. The timer does not start until the pot has reached pressure. A recipe labelled ten minutes can take twenty-five from plug to plate, and cold food or a full pot stretches that preheat further.

| Setting | Pressure above air | Found on | What it suits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Low | 5.8 to 7.2 psi | Most models | Eggs, delicate fish, soft vegetables |
| High | 10.2 to 11.6 psi | Every model | Meat, beans, stock, grains, most recipes |
| Max | 15 psi | A small number of models | Shaving a few more minutes off beans and stock |

## What Each Button Really Does

Here is the thing nobody tells you at the shop: most of the buttons are saved settings, not different technology. Pressure Cook is the engine. The presets simply load a time and a pressure level so you do not have to.

| Program | What the pot actually does | Reach for it when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pressure Cook (or Manual) | Seals, builds pressure, holds it for your set time | Braises, stews, beans, stock, grains |
| Sauté | Runs the element with the lid off, at three heat levels | Browning meat, softening onions, reducing a sauce |
| Slow Cook | Gentle, steady heat with no pressure at all | Recipes written for a crock pot |
| Rice | Senses when the water has been absorbed, then stops | White rice and similar short-grain types |
| Steam | Full heat with no cycling, food held above the water | Vegetables, dumplings, fish fillets |
| Yogurt | Holds a low warm temperature for hours | Homemade yogurt, and scalding the milk first |
| Sous Vide | Holds the water bath at a precise low temperature | Duo Plus, Pro and Ultimate models only |
| Keep Warm | Light heat to hold food at serving temperature | The end of any other program |

Sauté is the program people use least and should use most. Browning meat in the same pot before you seal it builds a base of flavour that pressure alone cannot make. Scrape the browned bits loose with a splash of stock before the lid goes on, the same move you would use in a [sauté pan](/blog/best-saute-pan/).

Rice is the one preset that divides people. It works fine on white rice, but brown rice needs longer and firmer control, so a dedicated machine still wins there. Our guide to the [best rice cooker for brown rice](/blog/best-rice-cooker-for-brown-rice/) explains why, and [how to cook rice in a rice cooker](/blog/how-to-cook-rice-in-a-rice-cooker/) covers the ratios.

Steam needs a rack or a basket, because food sitting in the water is boiling, not steaming. A wire trivet comes in the box, but a proper basket holds far more. There are options in our roundup of the [best steamer basket for an Instant Pot](/blog/best-steamer-basket-for-instant-pot/), and the same rules apply to a [bamboo steamer](/blog/how-to-use-a-bamboo-steamer/).

Slow Cook is the weakest program on the machine. The element heats from the base only, while a real crock pot heats from the sides too. Owner reviews regularly mention that dishes take longer than the original recipe says. Treat the setting as a backup, not a reason to sell your slow cooker.

## Natural Release vs Quick Release

When the timer ends, the pot is still full of pressure. How you let that pressure out changes the food, not just the wait.

**Natural release** means doing nothing. The pot cools on its own and pressure falls slowly, usually over 10 to 30 minutes. The food keeps cooking gently that whole time, and the liquid settles instead of churning.

**Quick release** means flipping the valve to vent. Steam shoots out, pressure drops in a minute or two, and the cooking stops almost at once. That sudden drop is exactly why some dishes misbehave.

The reason is physics, not luck. Liquid under pressure is sitting above its normal boiling point. Drop the pressure fast and that liquid flashes into steam all at once, so a starchy soup foams up and spits through the valve. Meat does something similar on the inside, and the fibres tighten as the moisture rushes out.

| Dish | Use this release | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pot roast, pulled pork, ribs | Natural, at least 10 minutes | Fibres relax, juice stays in the meat |
| Dried beans, split peas, lentils | Full natural | Starchy foam clogs and spits on quick release |
| Soup or stock filled near the line | Full natural | Liquid volume makes foaming worse |
| Rice and grains | Natural for 10 minutes, then vent | Grains finish absorbing, then stop |
| Broccoli, green beans, carrots | Quick | Seconds decide the texture |
| Fish fillets and shrimp | Quick | They overcook almost instantly |
| Hard-boiled eggs | Quick, then straight into ice water | Stops the yolk turning grey |

A halfway option works well for most weeknight meals. Let the pot sit for ten minutes, then vent the rest. Recipes write this as a 10-minute natural release, and it covers meat and rice nicely.

Whatever you use, keep your hand and face clear of the valve, and never put the pot under a low cabinet when venting. The steam leaves hot enough to scald.

## The Burn Message and How to Clear It

Nothing kills confidence in a new pot faster than the word BURN flashing while dinner sits half cooked inside. The message is a safety trip, not a breakdown.

The sensor under the inner pot watches the base temperature. If the base runs hotter than a pot of boiling liquid should, the board assumes something is scorching and stops heating. America's Test Kitchen describes the same trigger and the same fix: check the machine first, then deal with the food.

Common causes, in the order they usually happen:

1. **Not enough thin liquid.** Steam needs water. Tomato sauce, coconut milk and barbecue sauce are too thick to make it on their own.
2. **Stuck fond after sautéing.** Browned bits glued to the base read as a hot spot. Deglaze before the lid goes on.
3. **Thick sauce stirred through.** Layer heavy sauce on top and leave it there. Do not mix it in until after cooking.
4. **A dirty or missing sealing ring.** Steam escapes, pressure never builds, and the base keeps heating.
5. **Something under the inner pot.** A stray grain of rice between the liner and the element is enough.

To clear it: press Cancel, release the pressure safely, and open the lid. Scrape the base until it is clean, add about half a cup of water, and restart the program. The inner pot is bare stainless steel, so anything really stuck on comes off with the same tricks in [how to clean stainless steel pans](/blog/how-to-clean-stainless-steel-pans/). If you are unsure what the liner is made of, [what stainless steel cookware is](/blog/what-is-stainless-steel-cookware/) explains how to tell.

## How Much Liquid an Instant Pot Needs

Every pressure cooker needs a minimum amount of thin liquid to make steam. Instant Pot lists these by pot size.

| Pot size | Minimum thin liquid |
| --- | --- |
| 3 quart | 1 cup |
| 6 quart | 1½ cups |
| 8 quart | 2 cups |
| 10 quart | 2½ cups |

Two fill limits matter just as much. Never fill past the two-thirds mark on the liner. For anything that swells or foams, such as rice, oats, pasta or dried beans, stop at half. Going past those lines is how the steam pipe gets blocked.

Cups and quarts trip people up here, so keep [how many ounces are in a quart](/blog/how-many-ounces-in-a-quart/) handy if you are scaling a recipe up or down.

## What an Instant Pot Is Bad At

A fair explainer has to say where the machine falls down, and this one falls down in predictable places.

- **Anything crisp.** Wet steam and crisp skin are opposites. Sear before, or finish under a grill.
- **Quick vegetables.** Broccoli goes from bright to mush in about a minute of overcooking, and you cannot peek.
- **Pasta.** Starchy foam clogs the valve, and the texture is hard to control.
- **Delicate fish.** Low pressure helps, but a pan or a steamer basket is easier.
- **Steak.** Pressure is the wrong tool for anything you want pink in the middle.
- **Baking.** Cheesecake works because it likes a moist environment. Bread and cookies do not. Use a small oven instead, and our guide to the [best small ovens and toaster ovens for baking](/blog/best-small-ovens-and-toaster-ovens-for-baking-in-small-kitchens/) covers the compact options.
- **Recipes you taste and adjust.** The lid stays shut, so seasoning happens at the start or at the end.
- **Home canning.** The National Center for Home Food Preservation says electric multi-cookers [should not be used for pressure canning](https://nchfp.uga.edu/newsflash/canning-in-electric-multi-cookers), because they are not tested to hold a verified pressure for a verified time.

It is also worth knowing that slow, gentle cooking is not its strength. If you mostly simmer sauces and melt chocolate, a [double boiler](/blog/how-to-use-a-double-boiler/) or a heavy [stockpot with a lid](/blog/best-stockpot-with-a-lid/) will serve you better than any preset.

## Is an Instant Pot an Air Fryer?

No, and this is the single most common mix-up in the whole category.

A pressure cooker works with wet steam. An air fryer works with dry air moving fast. You cannot get both from one sealed lid, so the Crisp models solve it with two lids. You pressure cook with the first, then swap on the air fryer lid to brown the top.

If crisping is what you actually want, a [Duo Crisp](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08WCLJ7JG/?tag=kitchenpot-20) makes sense. If you want crisping and nothing else, a standalone machine is cheaper and usually better at it. Two practical notes on the Crisp lids. They need somewhere to sit while hot, which small kitchens rarely have spare. They also need cleaning after every use, and the basket collects grease quickly, so [cleaning an air fryer basket](/blog/how-to-clean-an-air-fryer-basket/) becomes a weekly job. ## Is It Worth the Counter Space?

A 6-quart Instant Pot takes up about as much room as a large stockpot and cannot be stacked. In a small kitchen that is a real cost, not a detail.

It earns the space if you cook beans and pulses from dry, make stock, braise cheap cuts, or batch cook on Sundays. Those jobs are slow on a stove and quick under pressure. It does not earn the space if you mostly cook quick fresh food. In that case a good pan and a sharp knife will beat it every night of the week.

## Which Instant Pot Should You Buy?

Skip the model grid and use these four rules instead.

- Cooking for one or two, tight counter: a 3-quart or 4-quart Mini.
- Standard household, no interest in gadgets: a 6-quart RIO or Duo.
- You want sous vide or a quieter release: a 6-quart Duo Plus or Pro.
- You want one machine that also crisps and you have room: a Duo Crisp.

Buy the pot that matches how you already cook, not the one with the most programs. Half the buttons on a 13-in-1 model are time-and-pressure presets you could dial in yourself.
