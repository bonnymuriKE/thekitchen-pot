HexClad pans are not nonstick pans with a pattern stamped on top. They are stainless steel pans with the nonstick sunk into the gaps, and that one detail changes how you have to cook in them.

Miss that, and the pan feels like an expensive disappointment. Get it, and it does something most pans can't.

This review covers how the hybrid surface works, the specs HexClad publishes, what buyers praise and complain about, and three cheaper pans that use the same idea.

## How the HexClad Surface Actually Works

Picture the cooking surface of a normal [nonstick pan](/blog/what-is-nonstick-cookware/): one flat, continuous layer of coating. Food never touches metal, which is why eggs slide, and why a metal spatula ruins it.

HexClad starts from the opposite end. The pan body is tri-ply, the same sandwich used in good [stainless steel cookware](/blog/what-is-stainless-steel-cookware/): magnetic stainless on the outside, an aluminum core in the middle to spread heat sideways, and stainless on the inside where food sits.

Then a laser etches a honeycomb of hexagons into that inner steel. The etching leaves raised steel ridges standing slightly proud of the surface, with shallow valleys between them. Nonstick coating goes into the valleys. The steel peaks stay bare.

So the cooking surface is a grid of tiny steel islands in a sea of nonstick, and each part does a different job:

- **The raised steel peaks** touch the food directly and get hot enough to brown it. That is why a HexClad pan can put a real crust on a steak or a salmon fillet, something a fully coated pan struggles to do.
- **The recessed nonstick valleys** hold oil and stop the food welding itself to the pan, so cleanup is closer to nonstick than to bare steel.
- **The height difference** is the durability trick. A flat metal spatula rides across the steel ridges and never reaches the coating below, which is why HexClad can call its pans metal-utensil safe when most nonstick brands cannot.

Two things follow from that design, and they explain almost every complaint you will read.

First, part of the surface your food touches is bare stainless. Steel sticks when it is dry. That is why HexClad tells you to season the pan, and why a dry egg will grab where a fully coated pan would release. If you already know [how to season stainless steel pans](/blog/how-to-season-stainless-steel-pans/), the routine is the same idea: a thin film of polymerised oil filling the microscopic texture of the metal.

Second, the pan runs hot. Steel ridges conduct heat straight into the food, so HexClad's own guidance is that medium on a HexClad equals medium-high on another pan. People who cook at their usual setting scorch things and then blame the coating.

> **The one-line version:** HexClad is a searing pan that cleans up easily, not an egg pan that also sears.

## Is HexClad Worth the Money?

Strip away the celebrity endorsements and the question is narrow: does a part-steel, part-nonstick surface solve a problem you actually have?

It does if you own a nonstick pan you are afraid to get properly hot, and a stainless pan you are afraid to cook eggs in. A hybrid pan collapses those two into one and removes the metal-utensil anxiety. Over years, a pan you do not replace every two or three years also changes the maths.

It does not if your cooking is mostly eggs, pancakes, crepes and delicate fish. For that, the [best nonstick pans](/blog/best-nonstick-pans-with-buying-guide/) release better and cost a fraction as much, and replacing one every few years is still cheaper. It also does not if you want a searing pan and nothing else. A plain tri-ply skillet, like the ones in our [All-Clad D3 review](/blog/all-clad-d3-cookware-set-review/), sears just as hard for less.

## Getting the Most Out of a Hybrid Pan

These pans reward a handful of habits and punish their opposites.

1. **Season it before the first cook, then every few weeks.** A teaspoon of avocado or another high smoke point oil, wiped thin, two to three minutes on medium-low, cooled and wiped out. You are filling the etched texture on the exposed steel.
2. **Turn the dial down one notch.** HexClad's own guidance is that medium on a hybrid pan behaves like medium-high on a normal pan. Most scorching complaints start here.
3. **Preheat, then add fat, then add food.** The same order that stops food welding to a steel pan. If you have read our guide on [seasoning stainless steel pans](/blog/how-to-season-stainless-steel-pans/), this is familiar ground.
4. **Use fat, not spray.** Aerosol sprays leave a gummy residue that builds up in the valleys and gets harder to remove each time.
5. **Deglaze while it is warm.** Water or stock in a still-warm pan lifts stuck bits out of the hexagon grid in seconds. Cold water on a screaming-hot pan risks warping.
6. **Scrub the grid, don't wipe it.** A nylon brush or the rough side of a sponge gets into the valleys. For burnt-on patches, the baking soda approach in our guide to [cleaning ceramic pans](/blog/how-to-clean-ceramic-pan/) works well.
7. **Store it protected.** Stacking pans on bare metal scratches everything eventually. Our [pots and pans storage guide for small kitchens](/blog/store-pots-and-pans-in-a-small-kitchen/) covers pan protectors and rail systems.

## Who Should Skip HexClad

Not every kitchen needs this pan, and it is fairer to say so.

- **You cook eggs most mornings and want zero effort.** Buy a cheap coated skillet and replace it when it dies.
- **Weight matters to you.** If lifting a full 3.4 lb pan is uncomfortable, a lighter hard-anodized pan is the kinder choice.
- **You have a glass cooktop and a heavy hand.** Steel ridges plus dragging equals scratches. A [nonstick pan with a flat, smooth base](/blog/what-is-nonstick-cookware/) is safer.
- **Your budget would be better spread out.** If you have two working pans and no saucepan, a [complete set under $200](/blog/best-cookware-set-under-200/) does more for your cooking than one premium skillet.
- **You cook a lot of acidic, long-simmered dishes.** A plain stainless pan or an enamelled pot handles tomato and wine better than any coated surface.
