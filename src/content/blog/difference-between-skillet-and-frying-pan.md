In modern American kitchens, a skillet and a frying pan are the same pan. Not similar. The same thing, with two names.

Most pages on this question invent a difference anyway. One claims the skillet is deeper. Another says the frying pan has straighter sides. A third decides skillets are always cast iron.

**None of that holds up.** Cookware makers, who have every commercial reason to split their catalogues into as many product lines as possible, do not split these two. They sell one pan and print both words on it.

So what is worth knowing instead? Where the two words came from, and which pan shapes really do differ. Those differences change how your food cooks.

## What Cookware Brands Actually Call the Pan

The quickest way to settle this is to look at what manufacturers sell.

All-Clad files the whole category under a single page headed ["Fry Pans & Skillets"](https://www.all-clad.com/cookware/categories/fry-pans-skillets.html), with no dividing line between the two. Tramontina lists one product as a "10-Inch Frying Pan" and, in the same title, an "Induction-Ready Skillet". Made In puts it in writing: ["There's no difference between frying pans and skillets, except where one word is used more predominantly over the other."](https://madeincookware.com/blogs/skillet-vs-pan)

Lodge calls every one of its round cast iron pans a skillet. Stainless brands lean towards fry pan. The pan in your hand does not change when the label does.

If a page tells you a skillet has a 20 to 30 percent smaller cooking surface than a frying pan, ask which two products it measured. There is no standard behind that number, because there are not two products to measure.

## Where the Two Words Came From

The words have different histories, which is the only reason we have both.

**Frying pan** is the plain description. "Pan" comes from Old English *panna*, and the compound simply names a pan you fry in. It is the older, more literal term, and it stayed the default across Britain and most of the Commonwealth.

**Skillet** is stranger. It shows up in English [around 1400, spelled *skellet*](https://www.etymonline.com/word/skillet), and nobody is certain where it came from. One theory traces it to Old French *escuelette*, a little dish. Another points to Old Norse *skjola*, a pail. Either way it started as a vessel name, not a cooking method.

In America the word traveled a different road. Before kitchen stoves were common, cooks used three-legged cast iron pans that sat over coals, nicknamed spiders. Flat-bottomed cast iron pans arrived in the late 19th century when flat stovetops did. Skillet stuck to those cast iron pans, then spread to the whole shape, and it stayed strongest in the South.

That is the real story. Two words, one pan, different roots.

## The Pans That Really Are Different

Here is where a comparison earns its place. These shapes are not naming quirks. Each one changes what happens to your food.

| Pan | Sides | Depth | Lid and handles | What it does best |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Skillet / frying pan | Sloped, flared | Shallow | No lid, one long handle | Searing, eggs, browning, anything you flip |
| Sauté pan | Straight, vertical | Deeper | Lid plus a helper handle | Braising, pan sauces, shallow frying |
| Saucier | Curved, rounded into the base | Medium, bowl-like | Lid, one handle | Whisking, custards, risotto, reductions |
| Sauteuse | Sloped but tall | Medium | Often a lid | A middle ground between skillet and sauté pan |
| Grill pan | Low, with raised ridges | Shallow | No lid, sometimes two handles | Grill marks and draining fat away from meat |
| Wok | Steeply curved | Deep | No lid in Western use | Stir-frying over very high heat |

The sauté pan is the one people really mean when they sense a difference. It is a different tool, and our guide to the [best sauté pans with lids](/blog/best-saute-pan/) covers what to look for. The saucier is the other underrated shape, because its rounded corners let a whisk reach everywhere.

Grill pans sit in their own category. They are not for frying at all, since the ridges lift food off the surface. If pancakes and eggs are the goal, a flat [griddle pan](/blog/best-griddle-pan-for-pancakes/) is the better buy. For high-heat stir-frying, see our picks for the [best wok for an electric stove](/blog/best-wok-for-electric-stove/).

## Sloped Sides vs Straight Sides: What Really Changes

Pan walls do more than hold food in. Their angle decides how fast moisture leaves and how easily you can move food around.

**Sloped sides let steam out.** A wide, open mouth gives water vapour an easy exit. That keeps the pan surface dry, and a dry surface browns. Crowd wet mushrooms into a covered pan and they turn grey. Spread them in a sloped skillet and they color.

**Straight sides hold liquid in.** A sauté pan of the same width holds noticeably more volume, so you can add stock and let it simmer. The Institute of Culinary Education makes the same point about the two shapes: [sloped walls speed evaporation, straight walls hold liquid and give more flat surface](https://www.ice.edu/blog/sloped-vs-straight-sided-pans).

**Sloped sides let you toss.** The flare gives food a ramp. A flick of the wrist sends vegetables up the wall and back down, with no spatula. Straight walls stop that motion dead, which is why you stir a sauté pan instead.

**Straight sides give a bigger flat floor.** For the same rim diameter, a straight-sided pan has more usable bottom. Four chicken thighs fit in a 12-inch sauté pan that would crowd in a 12-inch skillet.

There is a simple rule buried in all this. If you want moisture gone, use a sloped pan. If you want moisture kept, use a straight one. A [metal spatula](/blog/best-metal-spatula-set/) with a thin flexible edge helps in either shape.

## Material Changes More Than the Name Does

Two pans of the same shape can behave nothing alike. What they are made of matters far more than whether the box says skillet or fry pan.

**Cast iron** is heavy and slow to heat, then holds that heat hard. It is the best choice for a steak crust, and it shrugs off oven heat. It needs seasoning and it rusts if left wet. Our [Lodge cast iron skillet review](/blog/lodge-cast-iron-skillet-review/) covers the cheap workhorse, while the [Stargazer skillet](/blog/stargazer-cast-iron-skillet/) and vintage [Griswold pans](/blog/griswold-cast-iron-skillet-review/) show what smoother machined surfaces buy you. Enamelled cast iron, such as a [Le Creuset skillet](/blog/le-creuset-cast-iron-skillet/), skips the seasoning step.

**Stainless steel** does not react with acid and can be scrubbed without worry. On its own it conducts heat poorly, so good pans bond a layer of aluminum between steel layers. That is what tri-ply means, and it is why an [All-Clad D3 pan](/blog/all-clad-d3-cookware-set-review/) heats evenly while a thin steel pan burns in rings. Our explainer on [what stainless steel cookware is](/blog/what-is-stainless-steel-cookware/) goes deeper into the layers.

**Nonstick** is aluminum with a coating, so it is light and it releases eggs with almost no fat. The coating wears out, which is why nobody should pay a lot for one. Our [nonstick pan buying guide](/blog/best-nonstick-pans-with-buying-guide/) explains the trade-off, and [what nonstick cookware is](/blog/what-is-nonstick-cookware/) covers how the coating works.

**Ceramic coatings** are the newer alternative, and independent lab testing has repeatedly found they lose their slickness within a few years of regular use. Compare them in [stainless steel vs ceramic cookware](/blog/stainless-steel-vs-ceramic-cookware/), or see the current options in our [ceramic cookware set](/blog/best-ceramic-cookware-set/) roundup.

If you cook on induction, the pan needs a magnetic base. Cast iron and most stainless qualify, while plain aluminum does not. Our guide to the [best cookware for induction cooktops](/blog/best-cookware-for-induction-cooktop/) lists what works, and there is a workaround for [using non-induction cookware on an induction cooktop](/blog/how-to-use-non-induction-cookware-on-induction-cooktop/).

## How to Pick the Right Size

Size is the decision that actually affects your cooking every night. Pans are measured across the top rim, not across the base, so a 10-inch pan has a cooking floor closer to 8 inches.

| Size | Cooking floor | Fits | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 8-inch | About 6 inches | One or two eggs, toasting spices, one small fillet | Too small for a full meal |
| 10-inch | About 8 inches | Two chicken breasts, three eggs, veg for two | The best all-round size for most homes |
| 12-inch | About 9 to 10 inches | Four portions, a full sear, family stir-fries | Heavy, slow to heat, overhangs small burners |
| 14-inch | About 11 inches | Batch cooking, big roasts | Will not fit most home ovens or sinks |

A 12-inch cast iron skillet can pass 8 pounds when loaded. That matters if you lift it one-handed or if you have a [glass-top stove](/blog/how-to-protect-glass-top-stove-from-cast-iron/), where dropping a heavy pan cracks the surface.

Cooking for one or two changes the answer. A 10-inch pan is often still too big, and food spreads thin and dries out. Our guide to [cookware sizes for cooking for one](/blog/best-cookware-sizes-for-cooking-for-one/) works through the numbers, and the [small kitchen appliances for solo cooking](/blog/best-small-kitchen-appliances-for-cooking-for-one/) list covers what else fills the gap.

Burner size is the other limit. A pan much wider than the burner heats in a bullseye pattern, hot in the middle and cool at the rim. Match the pan to the burner and you avoid most uneven cooking complaints.

## Care Depends on the Pan, Not the Word

Cleaning rules follow the material. Getting this wrong ruins pans faster than any cooking mistake.

- **Cast iron:** hot water, a stiff brush, dry on the burner, then a thin wipe of oil. Soap in small amounts is fine on a well-seasoned pan.
- **Stainless:** a powdered cleanser cuts burnt oil that soap cannot touch. Never shock a hot pan under cold water, since the temperature jump warps the base.
- **Nonstick:** soft sponge only, medium heat only, and no metal tools. Abrasives shorten the coating's life sharply.
- **Ceramic:** treat it like nonstick, and see [how to clean a ceramic pan](/blog/how-to-clean-ceramic-pan/) for the specifics.
- **Hard anodized:** dark grey and matte, with its own rules in [how to clean hard anodized cookware](/blog/how-to-clean-hard-anodized-cookware/).

Storage is the other place pans get damaged. Stacking bare pans scratches the insides, so slide a cloth or pan protector between them. In a tight kitchen, the ideas in [how to store pots and pans in a small kitchen](/blog/store-pots-and-pans-in-a-small-kitchen/) and [how to organize pots and pans](/blog/how-to-organize-pots-and-pans/) help. A wall-mounted [pot rack](/blog/pots-and-pans-rack-reviews/) keeps heavy skillets off your shoulders, and [stackable pots and pans](/blog/7-best-stackable-pots-and-pans/) are built for cupboards that cannot take a rack.

## What About Electric Skillets and Hybrid Pans?

Two other things carry the word skillet, and both are worth a line.

An **electric skillet** is a plug-in pan with its own heating element and a thermostat. Its sides are usually straighter than a stovetop pan, and it holds a set temperature better than a burner does. That makes it useful in a room with no stove or as an extra cooking surface. Our guide to [what you can cook in an electric skillet](/blog/what-can-i-cook-in-an-electric-skillet/) covers the range.

**Hybrid pans** put a raised steel grid over a nonstick surface, promising searing and easy release together. The idea is clever and the results are mixed. Our [HexClad review](/blog/hexclad-hybrid-cookware-review/) works through what the design does and does not deliver.

One more thing worth watching is energy. A heavy pan on a small burner wastes a lot of heat, and matching the two saves more than most gadgets do. That logic runs through our guide to [choosing energy-efficient cookware for a small kitchen](/blog/how-to-choose-energy-efficient-cookware-for-a-small-kitchen/).

## The Simple Rule to Remember

Stop asking about skillets versus frying pans. Ask about sides instead.

Sloped sides for anything you want dry and browned. Straight sides for anything with liquid in it. Everything else is a question of material and size, and both of those change your cooking far more than a name on a box ever will.

If you are buying one pan today, make it a 10-inch skillet. Call it whatever you like.
