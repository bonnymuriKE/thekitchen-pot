You're halfway through a soup recipe and it asks for "1 quart of chicken stock." Your carton says 32 fl oz, your measuring jug is marked in cups, and suddenly you're doing math with wet hands. Or you're scaling down a big-batch recipe that uses quarts and you just want to know how much to pour for one person.

The answer to how many ounces are in a quart is simple once you have it: **32**. But there are a few traps. The US has two kinds of quarts, the UK has its own, and "ounces" can mean weight or volume. This guide sorts all of that out with clear charts you can use in the kitchen.

> **Quick answer:** There are 32 fluid ounces in a US liquid quart. That's the same as 4 cups, 2 pints, or 946 milliliters, and 4 quarts make a gallon (128 fluid ounces). A UK imperial quart is bigger, at 40 imperial fluid ounces (about 1.14 liters). A US dry quart, used for berries and produce, is about 37.2 fluid ounces.

## How Many Ounces in a Quart?

In the US customary system, which almost every American recipe uses:

**1 quart = 32 fluid ounces = 4 cups = 2 pints = 1/4 gallon**

The easy way to remember it is to work up from a cup:

- 1 cup = 8 fluid ounces
- 2 cups = 1 pint = 16 fluid ounces
- 2 pints = 1 quart = 32 fluid ounces
- 4 quarts = 1 gallon = 128 fluid ounces

Every step doubles, except the jump from quarts to gallons, which multiplies by four. That's actually where the word comes from. A quart is a "quarter" of a gallon.

In metric terms, a US liquid quart is 946.35 milliliters. For everyday cooking, you can round that to 950 mL, or just think of a quart as "a little less than a liter."

To convert any number of quarts to fluid ounces, **multiply by 32**. So 2.5 quarts × 32 = 80 fluid ounces.

## Fluid Ounces vs Ounces: Why It Matters

This is where most kitchen measuring mix-ups happen. The word "ounce" is used for two different things.

- **Fluid ounces (fl oz)** measure **volume**, meaning how much space something takes up. Use them for water, milk, stock, oil and other liquids. A liquid measuring cup measures fluid ounces.
- **Ounces (oz)** measure **weight**. A pound is 16 ounces. A kitchen scale measures ounces.

When you ask how many ounces are in a quart, the answer of 32 is about **fluid ounces**. A quart of anything, whether it's water or [popcorn](/blog/best-hot-air-popcorn-popper/), holds 32 fluid ounces of space. But a quart of flour and a quart of honey weigh very different amounts.

Water is the handy exception where the two line up closely. One cup of water (8 fluid ounces) weighs a little over 8 ounces. That's where the old saying "a pint's a pound the world around" comes from. It's close enough for water and milk, but it falls apart for most other ingredients.

## US Liquid Quart vs US Dry Quart vs UK Quart

There are actually three different quarts you might run into. Only one of them matters for most recipes, but the other two explain a lot of confusion online.

| Type of quart | Where it's used | Size in mL | In US fluid ounces |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| US liquid quart | US recipes, milk, stock, soups | 946 mL | 32 fl oz |
| US dry quart | Berries, produce at farm stands | About 1,101 mL | About 37.2 fl oz |
| UK imperial quart | Older British recipes | About 1,137 mL | About 38.4 US fl oz (40 imperial fl oz) |

### The US liquid quart

This is the quart in almost every American recipe, on cartons of broth, and in the markings on pots and slow cookers. It's exactly one quarter of a US gallon, which is defined as 231 cubic inches. When a recipe says "quart," this is the one it means.

### The US dry quart

The dry quart is about 16% bigger, at 67.2 cubic inches. It's used for loose produce. When a farmers' market sells a "quart of strawberries" in a little green basket, that's a dry quart. You'll rarely, if ever, see it in a recipe. If you need to know how many berries a recipe wants, it's more reliable to go by cups or weight.

### The UK imperial quart

The imperial quart is used in the UK and in some older recipes from Britain, Canada, Australia and elsewhere. It holds 40 imperial fluid ounces, or about 1.14 liters. To add to the fun, an imperial fluid ounce (28.4 mL) is slightly smaller than a US fluid ounce (29.6 mL). The result: an imperial quart is about 20% bigger than a US quart.

For most modern recipes from those countries, you won't need this. They generally use milliliters and liters now. But if you're working from a vintage British cookbook and a soup seems too thick, the quart size might be why.

## How to Measure a Quart Without a Quart Measure

You don't need a special quart container. Here are the easiest ways to measure one.

1. **Use a 4-cup liquid measuring cup.** A 4-cup glass measuring jug holds exactly one quart when filled to the 4-cup line. Many, like the classic [Pyrex 4-cup measuring cup](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=pyrex+4+cup+measuring+cup&tag=kitchenpot-20), also show ounces and milliliters on the side.
2. **Fill a 1-cup measure four times.** Set the cup on the counter, pour to the line, and check at eye level. Repeat four times.
3. **Use a 2-cup measure twice.** Two full 2-cup measures make a quart.
4. **Use a quart container you already have.** A 32-ounce deli container, an empty stock carton, or a quart-size mason jar all hold one quart.
5. **Weigh water.** If you have a scale, 946 grams of water is one quart. This is handy for filling a pot for pasta or brining.

### Tips for measuring liquids accurately

- Use a **liquid measuring cup** (the kind with a spout and markings below the rim) for liquids, and dry measuring cups for dry ingredients. Dry cups are meant to be leveled off, which you can't do with milk.
- Set the cup on a flat surface and **read it at eye level**. Looking down from above makes you pour too much.
- For sticky liquids like honey, lightly oil the cup first so everything slides out.
- Nutrition labels round a little. FDA labeling rules treat 1 cup as 240 mL and 1 fluid ounce as 30 mL, so label numbers can differ slightly from exact kitchen conversions. The difference won't matter for cooking.

## Quart, Cup, Tablespoon and Teaspoon Conversions

When you're scaling a recipe way down, you often need to go from quarts all the way to spoons. Here's the full ladder:

- 1 quart = 4 cups = 32 fluid ounces
- 1 quart = 64 tablespoons
- 1 quart = 192 teaspoons
- 1 cup = 16 tablespoons = 48 teaspoons
- 1 fluid ounce = 2 tablespoons = 6 teaspoons
- 1 tablespoon = 3 teaspoons

So if you're dividing a 1-quart recipe by 8 to make a single serving, you'd use 1/2 cup, which is 8 tablespoons or 4 fluid ounces.

## Using Quarts When You Cook for One

Many soup, stock and sauce recipes are written in quarts because they're meant to feed a crowd. That's a lot of food for one person, and a lot of fridge space in a small kitchen.

A few ways to make quart-based recipes work:

- **Halve or quarter the recipe.** A quart of stock becomes 2 cups (half) or 1 cup (quarter). Use the conversion chart above to scale everything evenly.
- **Pick the right pot.** If you cook a half recipe in a giant pot, liquids evaporate faster and things can scorch. A 2- to 3-quart saucepan handles most single-person soups. Our guide to the [best cookware sizes for cooking for one](/blog/best-cookware-sizes-for-cooking-for-one/) has a full size chart.
- **Freeze in 1-cup or 2-cup portions.** If you do make the full quart, freeze it in single servings so you can thaw one bowl at a time. Label everything with the date. Leftovers with chicken keep 3 to 4 days in the fridge, as covered in our guide on [how long chicken lasts in the fridge](/blog/how-long-does-chicken-last-in-the-fridge/).
- **Use a small multicooker.** A 3-quart pressure cooker or rice cooker is plenty for one or two people and takes up much less counter space. Our list of the [best small kitchen appliances for cooking for one](/blog/best-small-kitchen-appliances-for-cooking-for-one/) covers compact options.
- **Plan your prep around quart batches.** One quart of cooked grains, beans or soup is about four meals. Our [easy meal prep ideas for one person in a small kitchen](/blog/easy-meal-prep-ideas-for-one-person-in-a-small-kitchen/) show how to rotate them through the week.

## Common Quart Conversion Mistakes

| Mistake | What goes wrong | How to avoid it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mixing up ounces and fluid ounces | Weighing 32 oz of flour gives almost 2 quarts, not 1 | Use fluid ounces for liquids, a scale for weight |
| Using a UK recipe with US measures | Soups and sauces come out too thick or too salty | Check if the recipe is British; convert to mL |
| Measuring liquids in dry cups | Spills and short measures | Use a spouted liquid measuring cup |
| Reading the cup from above | You over-pour | Read at eye level on a flat counter |
| Assuming a berry "quart" equals 4 cups | Dry quarts are about 16% bigger | Go by cups or weight for produce |

## The Bottom Line on Ounces in a Quart

A US quart holds 32 fluid ounces, which is 4 cups, 2 pints, or about 946 milliliters. Multiply quarts by 32 to get fluid ounces, and divide fluid ounces by 32 to get quarts. The only times the answer changes are with a UK imperial quart (40 imperial fluid ounces) or a dry quart of produce (about 37.2 fluid ounces), and neither shows up in most modern American recipes.

Keep a 4-cup liquid measuring cup handy, remember that fluid ounces measure space while ounces measure weight, and use a scale for dry baking ingredients. If a recipe also calls for a certain amount of chicken, here's [how much a chicken breast weighs](/blog/how-much-does-a-chicken-breast-weigh/).
