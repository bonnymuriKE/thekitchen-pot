Learning how to make a small kitchen look bigger is mostly about light, color and clutter. You cannot move the walls, but you can change what the eye sees.

Most small kitchens feel cramped for the same few reasons: dark corners, busy counters, heavy cabinets and too many colors fighting each other. Fix those, and the same square footage feels open. Below are the 15 tricks we recommend, in the order we would do them.

**The short version:** Clear the counters first, because that alone changes the room. Then go light and consistent on color, and add under-cabinet lighting so no corner sits in shadow. Swap busy hardware, window treatments and rugs for slim, simple ones. Finally, right-size your appliances and set up work zones so the kitchen works as well as it looks.

## How to Make a Small Kitchen Look Bigger: The Three Rules

A room feels big or small based on three things: how much light reaches every surface, how many times your eye has to stop, and how far you can see. Square footage matters less than you think.

Every object on a counter and every color change is a stop for the eye. Every dark corner reads as a wall closing in. A 7-by-10-foot galley with bare counters, one wall color and bright, even light feels larger than a 10-by-12 kitchen with crowded counters and a single ceiling fixture.

So our rule of thumb is simple: **fewer stops, more light, longer views.** Every trick below serves one of those goals, and none of them need a contractor.

## Start With Clutter: The Free Trick That Does the Most

### Trick 1: Clear Everything Off the Counters

This is the single biggest change you can make, and it costs nothing. Take every item off the counter. Put back only what you use every single day. For most people that is a coffee maker, a knife block or strip, and maybe a utensil crock.

Everything else needs a home. The toaster you use twice a week can live in a lower cabinet. The stand mixer you use once a month belongs on a high shelf or in a closet. Our full method is in [countertop organization ideas for a small kitchen](/blog/countertop-organization-ideas-for-a-small-kitchen/).

Aim for at least one clear stretch of counter that is 24 inches deep and 36 inches wide. That clear zone does two jobs. It gives you room to cook, and it shows a long, uninterrupted surface that makes the whole room look wider.

### Trick 2: Give Everything a Home Behind a Door

Clutter comes back when things have nowhere to go. Empty one cabinet at a time and sort by use. Daily items go at waist to shoulder height. Monthly items go up high or down low.

If you have no pantry, food tends to spill onto the counter. Our guide on [how to organize a small kitchen with no pantry](/blog/how-to-organize-a-small-kitchen-with-no-pantry/) shows where it can go instead. For cabinets, start with these [small kitchen cabinet organization ideas](/blog/small-kitchen-cabinet-organization-ideas/), then tackle the junk drawer with [how to organize kitchen drawers in a small kitchen](/blog/how-to-organize-kitchen-drawers-in-a-small-kitchen/).

Pots and pans are the bulkiest problem. Nest them and file the lids upright, as shown in [how to store pots and pans in a small kitchen](/blog/store-pots-and-pans-in-a-small-kitchen/) and [how to organize pot lids](/blog/how-to-organize-pot-lids-in-a-small-kitchen/).

## What Color Makes a Small Kitchen Look Bigger?

Light colors make a small kitchen look bigger. They reflect more light, so the room looks brighter and the walls seem to sit farther back. Dark colors absorb light and pull walls closer.

### Trick 3: Choose a Light Color With a High LRV

Paint chips list a number called LRV, short for light reflectance value. It runs from 0 (pure black) to 100 (pure white). It tells you how much light a color bounces back into the room.

For a small kitchen, we recommend an LRV of 70 or higher on the walls. That still leaves plenty of choice. Warm whites, soft greige (a gray-beige mix), pale gray and very light sage all sit in that range.

A north-facing kitchen gets cool, bluish daylight, so pick a warm white there. A south-facing kitchen can take a cooler white.

| Color family | Effect in a small kitchen | Best with |
|---|---|---|
| Warm white | Brightest feel, soft and welcoming | North-facing rooms, wood counters |
| Pale gray or greige | Clean and calm, hides scuffs better than white | White or gray counters, stainless appliances |
| Very light sage or blue | Fresh, recedes visually, adds color without weight | White cabinets, brass or black hardware |
| Mid-tone colors (LRV 40 to 60) | Cozy but makes walls feel closer | Large windows only |
| Dark navy, green or charcoal | Dramatic, shrinks the room unless light is strong | Lower cabinets only, paired with light uppers |

### Trick 4: Use One Color From Counter to Ceiling

Color changes are stops for the eye. The fewer you have, the bigger the room feels. Paint the walls the same color as the cabinets, or a shade or two lighter. Paint the ceiling white or the wall color lightened by half.

If you can paint cabinets, this is the most dramatic change on the list. If you rent, match the wall paint to the existing cabinet color instead. A white kitchen with white walls reads as one big bright box.

Pay attention to sheen, meaning how shiny the paint is. Satin or eggshell on walls reflects light and wipes clean of splatter. Flat paint looks dull in a kitchen and marks easily. Semi-gloss works for trim and cabinet doors.

## Do Dark Cabinets Make a Small Kitchen Look Smaller?

Dark cabinets on every wall usually do make a small kitchen look smaller, especially if it has one small window or none. The room loses light, and the cabinets read as heavy blocks.

That does not mean you have to skip dark colors.

### Trick 5: Go Two-Tone, Dark Below and Light Above

Put the dark color on the lower cabinets and keep the upper cabinets and walls light. Your eye reads the bottom of the room as solid and grounded, and the top as open and bright. The room feels taller.

Dark lowers also hide kicks, splashes and fingerprints better than white.

Skip dark uppers in a small kitchen. Upper cabinets sit at eye level, so dark ones feel like they lean over you. If you love a dark color and the kitchen has strong daylight, use it on an island or a single run of lower cabinets.

## Layer Your Lighting So No Corner Hides in Shadow

Lighting is the most underrated fix on this list. A single ceiling fixture lights the middle of the room and leaves the counters in your own shadow. The corners go dark, and dark corners make a room look smaller.

### Trick 6: Add Under-Cabinet Lights

Under-cabinet lights wash the counter and backsplash with light. That makes the back wall look farther away and lights the place where you actually work. Plug-in or battery LED strips and puck lights install in under an hour, with no wiring and no landlord approval.

Mount strips toward the front edge of the cabinet bottom, not against the wall. Front mounting spreads light across the whole counter and keeps the glare off shiny backsplashes. Run them the full length of each cabinet run, not just one section.

### Trick 7: Match Your Bulbs

Mixed bulb colors make a kitchen look patchy and smaller. Use the same color temperature everywhere. Color temperature is measured in kelvins (K). Lower numbers look warm and yellow. Higher numbers look cool and blue.

| Light layer | What it does | Our pick |
|---|---|---|
| Ceiling (ambient) | Fills the whole room | 2700K to 3000K, flush or semi-flush fixture |
| Under-cabinet (task) | Lights the counter, pushes the back wall away | 3000K LED strips, front-mounted |
| Above sink or window | Brightens the darkest wet zone | Small pendant or flush light, same K as ceiling |
| Inside open shelves or glass cabinets | Adds depth at eye level | Battery puck lights, same K |
| All bulbs | Show true food and paint colors | CRI 90 or higher on the box |

CRI stands for color rendering index. It shows how true colors look under the bulb. A CRI of 90 or higher keeps your paint from looking muddy and your food from looking gray.

Swap a heavy, low-hanging ceiling fixture for a flush one so the ceiling feels higher. LED bulbs also cut your bill, as we explain in [how to reduce kitchen energy use in a small apartment](/blog/how-to-reduce-kitchen-energy-use-in-a-small-apartment/).

## Use Reflective Surfaces to Bounce Light Around

### Trick 8: Pick a Backsplash With Some Shine

Glossy tile, glass tile and polished stone all reflect light back into the room. A matte, dark backsplash soaks it up. If you can choose, go with a light glossy tile and a grout color close to the tile color. Contrasting grout creates a grid of lines, and every line is another stop for the eye.

Large tiles beat small ones in a tiny room. Fewer grout lines mean a calmer wall. If you rent, peel-and-stick tile can cover an ugly backsplash in an afternoon. Keep it out of the area directly behind the burners unless the package says it is rated for heat.

A mirror or back-painted glass panel can work at the end of a galley or on the wall facing a window. It doubles the light and the view. Keep mirrors away from the stove, where grease and steam will cloud them within days.

Glossy cabinet fronts, light counters and a bright sink add reflection too. If you are replacing a sink, see the [best white undermount kitchen sink](/blog/best-white-undermount-kitchen-sink/).

## Does Open Shelving Make a Kitchen Look Bigger?

Yes, open shelving makes a kitchen look bigger when you keep it sparse. Removing a bank of upper cabinets opens the wall and lets light reach the corners. But shelves crammed with mismatched items look like clutter, and clutter shrinks a room.

### Trick 9: Replace One Run of Uppers With Shelves, or Take the Doors Off

You do not need to rip out every cabinet. Replace one short run, usually the one in the corner or beside the window, with two floating shelves. Keep the rest closed for the messy stuff.

Renters can get the same effect by removing a pair of cabinet doors. Unscrew the hinges, put the doors, hinges and screws in a labeled bag, and store them under the bed. Put them back before you move out.

Follow these rules for open shelves:

1. Store only items you use every week, so dust never settles on them.
2. Stick to one or two colors, such as white plates and clear glasses.
3. Leave about a third of each shelf empty.
4. Keep shelves away from the stove, where grease builds up fast.
5. Put pantry food in matching clear jars or move it to a cabinet. Our [best airtight food storage containers](/blog/best-airtight-food-storage-containers/) guide helps here.

Glass-front cabinet doors are a middle ground. They show depth like open shelves but keep dust and grease off. Add a battery puck light inside and the cabinet glows at eye level.

## Draw the Eye Up to Use Every Inch of Height

Small kitchens are short on floor space, not height. Most have 8-foot ceilings, and the top 18 inches above the upper cabinets often sit empty or collect dusty clutter.

### Trick 10: Take Storage Vertical

Using the wall frees the counter, and a clear counter makes the room look bigger. A [magnetic knife strip](/blog/best-magnetic-knife-strip/) replaces a bulky knife block. A rail with S-hooks holds spatulas and ladles. A slim shelf above the sink holds dish soap and a plant.

Use one material and finish for all of it, such as matte black or brushed steel. Three different rail styles on one wall look busy. Our [vertical storage ideas for small kitchens](/blog/vertical-storage-ideas-for-small-kitchens/) walk through every option, from pegboards to over-door racks.

### Trick 11: Lead the Eye to the Ceiling

Hang window shades at the top of the window frame, or even a few inches above it. The window looks taller and so does the wall. Paint the ceiling white or a very light tint of the wall color, never a darker shade.

If your upper cabinets stop short of the ceiling, the gap is a choice. Either leave it empty and paint it the wall color so it disappears, or use matching baskets for rarely used items. Do not fill it with a row of decorative bottles and cookbooks. That reads as clutter at the top of the room.

## Pick Hardware and Fixtures That Disappear

### Trick 12: Slim, Consistent Hardware

Chunky knobs, ornate pulls and mixed finishes add dozens of small stops for the eye. Swap them for slim bar pulls in one finish. A good rule is to size pulls at about one third of the drawer width. On doors, a 4 to 6-inch pull works for most standard cabinets.

Match the hardware to the cabinet color for the calmest look. White pulls on white cabinets almost vanish. If you want contrast, pick one finish, such as matte black or brass, and use it on every pull, the faucet and the light fixture.

Swapping hardware is renter-friendly if you keep the originals. Before you buy, measure the distance between the screw holes on your current pulls. That is called the center-to-center measurement. Buy new pulls with the same spacing and you will not need to drill new holes.

An [over-the-sink cutting board](/blog/best-over-the-sink-cutting-board/) adds prep space without adding a thing to the counter.

## Open Up the Window and the Floor

### Trick 13: Strip the Window Down

The window is your biggest light source, and heavy curtains block it. Take down valances, swags and thick curtains. Use a simple roller shade or a light linen café curtain (a short curtain that covers only the bottom half of the window) instead.

Clear the windowsill too. One small herb pot is plenty.

### Trick 14: Make the Floor Read as One Surface

The floor is a large share of what you see in a small kitchen. Treat it as one calm, continuous surface.

- If you can choose flooring, run planks along the longest wall. It makes the room look longer.
- Match the kitchen floor to the next room where possible. One floor flowing through makes both rooms feel larger.
- Skip small, busy rugs. Use one long, low-pile runner in a light, plain color, or no rug at all.

Then raise things off the floor. Furniture on legs and cabinets with a visible toe kick (the recessed strip at the base of lower cabinets) show more floor. The more floor you see, the bigger the room looks.

## How Do You Make a Small Kitchen Feel More Open?

You make a small kitchen feel more open by protecting the sightlines. A sightline is the view your eye follows from the doorway into the room. If that view runs straight to a window or a far wall without stopping, the room feels open.

### Trick 15: Clear the Sightline From the Doorway

Stand in the doorway and look. Whatever your eye hits first is the most important thing to fix. Often it is a fridge covered in magnets, a tall trash can or an overflowing dish rack.

- **Clear the fridge door.** Keep two or three items on it, or none. A fridge covered in paper looks like a bulletin board.
- **Hide the trash.** Move it inside a lower cabinet, or use a slim can that tucks beside the fridge.
- **Fold the dish rack away.** A roll-up rack over the sink, or a small rack stored in a cabinet after use, keeps the counter clear.
- **Use see-through furniture.** Clear acrylic stools and a small glass or round table let light pass through. A fold-down wall table disappears when you are done eating.
- **Put the tallest things at the edges.** A tall pantry cabinet or fridge belongs at the end of a run, not in the middle of the view.

Bare counters also leave bugs nothing to find. See [how to keep pests out of a small kitchen](/blog/how-to-keep-pests-out-of-a-small-kitchen/).

## How Can I Make a Small Kitchen More Functional?

A kitchen that works smoothly also looks bigger, because nothing piles up. Function and looks are the same job here. Set up zones so each task happens in one place, with its tools within arm's reach.

Here are the zones we set up in every small kitchen:

1. **Prep zone:** Your clear counter stretch, with knives, cutting boards and mixing bowls in the drawer or cabinet right below.
2. **Cooking zone:** Beside the stove, with oils, salt, spatulas and the pans you use most.
3. **Cleanup zone:** Around the sink, with soap, towels and the trash can.
4. **Storage zone:** The fridge and food cabinet, near the prep zone if possible.
5. **Serving zone:** Plates and glasses in the upper cabinet closest to the dishwasher or the dish rack.

Once the zones are set, use these measurements to check the room works.

| Measurement | Standard or target | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Counter height | 36 inches | Comfortable prep height for most adults |
| Counter depth | 24 inches | Standard; a clear 24 x 36-inch area fits most prep |
| Upper cabinet depth | 12 inches | Holds dinner plates, keeps the counter usable |
| Counter to upper cabinets | About 18 inches | Room for small appliances and lighting |
| Walkway for one cook | 42 inches | Lets you open the oven and dishwasher and still pass |
| Walkway for two cooks | 48 inches | Room to pass behind each other |
| Landing space beside fridge | At least 15 inches | A spot to set things down when the door is open |

An aisle under 42 inches is normal in older apartments. The fix is a completely clear floor, with no carts or bins parked in the aisle.

Cook in a way that suits the space. Plan [a full meal on only two burners](/blog/how-to-cook-a-full-meal-with-only-two-burners/), and batch cook with our [easy meal prep ideas for one person](/blog/easy-meal-prep-ideas-for-one-person-in-a-small-kitchen/).

## Very Small Kitchen Ideas for Studios and Galleys

The tiniest kitchens need a few extra moves.

**Studio kitchens** sit in full view of the living area, so make them blend in. Match the wall color to the rest of the room and keep the counter almost bare. A rolling cart can hold small appliances and roll into a closet.

**Galley kitchens** are two parallel runs with a narrow aisle. Keep one side emptier than the other so the aisle does not feel like a tunnel. A light or mirror at the far end pulls the eye down the room.

**Kitchens with no window** need extra light. Layer under-cabinet strips, a bright ceiling fixture and light inside any glass cabinets. Keep every surface light, and skip dark colors entirely.

**Kitchens with a mini fridge** gain counter space, but only if the fridge stays tidy. Keep it frost-free, as we explain in [how to defrost a mini fridge](/blog/how-to-defrost-a-mini-fridge/), and stock only what you will eat this week. Our guide on [how to stock a small kitchen for solo cooking](/blog/how-to-stock-a-small-kitchen-for-solo-cooking/) helps you buy less and waste less.

## A Weekend Before-and-After Plan

Most of these tricks fit in one weekend. Take a photo from the doorway first, then work in this order.

**Saturday morning: clear and sort**

1. Take everything off the counters, the fridge door and the windowsill.
2. Empty one cabinet at a time. Donate duplicates and anything you have not used in a year.
3. Put daily items back at waist to shoulder height, and everything else up high or down low.
4. Return only daily-use items to the counter.

**Saturday afternoon: light**

5. Install under-cabinet LED strips, mounted toward the front edge.
6. Replace every bulb with the same color temperature, ideally 2700K to 3000K with a CRI of 90 or higher.
7. Take down heavy curtains and hang a simple shade at the top of the window frame.

**Sunday: surfaces and finishes**

8. Paint the walls a light color with an LRV of 70 or higher, in satin or eggshell.
9. Swap the cabinet hardware for slim pulls in one finish, matching the old screw-hole spacing.
10. Mount a magnetic knife strip and a utensil rail, then move those items off the counter.
11. Replace small rugs with one plain runner, or remove them.

Take a second photo from the same spot, then live with it for a week before buying anything else.

## Common Mistakes That Make a Small Kitchen Look Smaller

These are the mistakes we see most often. Each one undoes a trick above.

- **Buying organizers before decluttering.** More bins just hold more stuff. Sort first, then buy only what fills a real gap.
- **Filling every shelf.** Open shelves packed edge to edge look worse than closed cabinets. Leave space empty on purpose.
- **Too many colors and finishes.** Three wood tones, two metal finishes and a bold accent wall create a dozen stops for the eye. Pick one wood, one metal and one main color.
- **Dark paint in a dark room.** A moody color can look great in a sunny kitchen. In a north-facing galley with one window, it shrinks the space.
- **Countertop appliance creep.** An air fryer, toaster, blender and coffee maker all on the counter eat half your prep space. Keep one or two out, and store the rest.
- **Ignoring the floor.** Bins, bags and a pet bowl in the walkway make the room feel half its size. Keep the floor clear from wall to wall.

Avoid these, and the kitchen both looks bigger and works better.
